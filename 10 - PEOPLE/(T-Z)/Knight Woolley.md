Knight Woolley (May 1, 1895, Brooklyn, New York, to 1984) was a member of the [[Yale University]] class of 1917 and of [[Skull and Bones]]. Before 1927 he had worked in the foreign department of the [[American Exchange National Bank]]. In that year he organized the private bank that [[W. Averell Harriman]] and [[E. Roland Harriman]] set up as a partnership, and he was a partner of [[Brown Brothers Harriman]] from the 1931 merger into the 1960s.[^1][^2] The record also places him as a director of the [[Union Banking Corporation]] of New York, as the author of a January 1941 letter to the New York state superintendent of banks about that bank, as the author of a January 1933 letter about the [[Consolidated Silesian Steel Company]] that the [[The Guardian|Guardian]] quoted in 2004, and as one of the registered holders of shares in the [[Holland-American Trading Corporation]], seized in 1942.

### Andover, Yale and Skull and Bones

Woolley prepared at [[Phillips Academy]], Andover, where he was class historian at the 1913 commencement, business manager of the school yearbook, the Pot Pourri, and an undergraduate treasurer.[^3][^4][^5] The class history of 1917 at Yale prints his biographical entry. It gives his birth in Brooklyn on May 1, 1895, and his preparation at the Brooklyn Polytechnic Preparatory School and Phillips Academy. It records that he was on the class crews, held the John Eliot Scholarship, was assignment editor of the [[Yale Daily News]], and that "He belongs to [[Psi Upsilon]] and Skull and Bones." The entry closes with the note that Woolley "intends to go into business."[^1] A passage on the class's 1917 military training places him on guard at the sick-stables when an inspection party arrived.[^6]

The class history's entries for [[Prescott Bush]], E. Roland Harriman, Woolley and [[Neil Mallon]] each state the same membership.[^7] [[Antony C. Sutton]]'s catalogue of members by class year lists Woolley under the class of 1917 ("The Order '17"), and his list of the Brown Brothers, Harriman partners names him in the same class.[^8] [[Kris Millegan]] wrote that the office Bush joined under [[George Herbert Walker]] employed "many of his classmates from his Yale class of 1917, including Roland Harriman and Knight Woolley," and that "The three had been close friends at Yale and were all members of Skull and Bones."[^9]

Sutton's account of the 1917 members involved in the [[Guaranty Trust Company]] and Harriman business begins with "the following five members in the class of '17 (only fifteen were initiated)." It names Woolley first, with the dates "Guaranty Trust from 1919-1920, Harriman Company from 1927-1931, then Brown Brothers, Harriman from 1933," and names [[Frank P. Shepard]] alongside him, a vice president of Guaranty Trust from 1920 to 1934.[^8]

### Harriman Brothers and the acceptance business

On March 17, 1927, the Harrimans set up a partnership bank. It was first called W. A. Harriman & Co., without the "Inc.," and soon renamed [[Harriman Brothers & Co.]] [[John A. Kouwenhoven]]'s 1968 firm history names Woolley as the man the Harrimans hired to run it. He came from the foreign department of the American Exchange National Bank, which was then in the process of merging into the [[Irving Trust Company]]. He brought several former colleagues with him, three of them specialists in foreign exchange, among them [[Harold D. Pennington]], who later managed the firm's operations and sat on the board of the Union Banking Corporation. Kouwenhoven also describes Woolley as already an authority on bankers' acceptances, citing his *A B C of Bankers Acceptances*, first published by the American Acceptance Council in 1924. The bank began with four employees in a single room, with capital from the Harrimans and from [[M. Samuel & Co.]], and Averell Harriman's instruction to Woolley was "Here's four million. Go ahead and run the bank." Within less than four years the firm had moved twice and had seventy employees.[^10] Rudy Abramson wrote in his biography of Averell Harriman that the bank started with employees in a single room at 39 Broadway.[^11]

The founding date appears in several forms. Kouwenhoven's text gives March 17, 1927. His partner table comments that Woolley "Had been general manager of Harriman Bros. & Co. from its founding in 1926 until he became a partner," and gives January 1, 1929 as the date he became a partner of Harriman Brothers & Co. and January 1, 1931 as the date of his partnership in the merged firm.[^12] *The Bankers Magazine* of January 1931 wrote that "Harriman Brothers & Co., a co partnership, was formed on February 1, 1927, to further the Harriman interests," that its partners included W. A. Harriman, E. Roland Harriman, Woolley and Prescott S. Bush, and that the directors of the affiliated W. A. Harriman & Co., Inc. included the same four with others. It added that forty-nine foreign banks represented the firm as correspondents, that the firm was a member of the New York Stock Exchange and an associate member of the New York Curb Market, and that it transacted no margin account business.[^13] [[Garry Wills]] gave 1926 and wrote that the Harrimans "put a classmate in as president," identifying Woolley.[^14] A notice of new partnerships in *The New York Times* of May 5, 1929 lists Harriman Brothers & Co., May 1, 1929, 39 Broadway, with the partners W. A. Harriman, E. Roland Harriman, George H. Walker, [[Dale M. Parker]] and Knight Woolley.[^15]

By the time of the merger, the Harriman banking partnership was doing a profitable business in acceptances and foreign exchange, held a seat on the [[New York Stock Exchange]], and carried on a brokerage business, though none on margin account.[^10] The partners' minute book for 1927 records that Woolley, as general manager, had received through [[Ellery Sedgwick James]] and accepted "the offer of a $250,000 participation" in an acceptance credit for a Berlin metal firm, along with [[Brown Brothers & Co.]] and two other banks.[^16] In June 1930 the exchange received a proposal to transfer the membership of William H. Bixby to Woolley, identified as a partner in Harriman Bros. & Co.[^17]

### The 1930 merger and the managing partnership

[[Time]] reported on December 22, 1930 that the two houses had merged "last week," counting 16 partners in the new firm of Brown Bros., Harriman & Co., eleven of them Yale graduates. The same report named Ellery Sedgwick James as a classmate of E. Roland Harriman, Prescott Bush and Woolley.[^18]

Kouwenhoven places the origin of the merger on a train to a Yale reunion in the spring of 1930, when "Ellery James, Knight Woolley, and Prescott Bush" had taken a drawing room in a parlor car and someone suggested that the two firms should merge. Kouwenhoven called the story "now current in various versions, as all good legends are," and recorded [[Thatcher M. Brown]]'s recollection that James first suggested talking with the Harrimans.[^16] Abramson also set the card game in a New Haven Railroad parlor car with James, Bush and Woolley.[^11] [[Zachary Karabell]], writing in *[[Financial History]]*, placed a railcar trip on the same line in the fall of 1930 and wrote: "Roland wasn't on the train that day, but Ellery James and Knight Woolley were." Karabell described Woolley as "rarely without a bespoke double-breasted suit" and as "managing partner of another one of the Harriman firms."[^19]

Kouwenhoven's photograph caption identifies Woolley as the man "who had organized the Harrimans' private bank in 1927," and as "managing partner of Harriman Brothers & Co. at the time when the merger with Brown Brothers & Co. was arranged in the final months of 1930." The caption shows him as a Brown Brothers Harriman partner on a business trip to Vienna in the summer of 1933.[^10]

Wills wrote that Bush "quickly became, with Knight Woolley, the principal expert on what projects to back" at the firm, and quoted Bush's oral-history interviews: "We [he and Woolley] were running the business, the day-to-day business, all the administrative decisions and the executive decisions. We were the ones that did it."[^14] Sutton's list of partners in the mid-1970s, which he gave as twenty-six partners, of whom nine were members of the order, includes Woolley for the class of 1917.[^8]

### Partnership and directorships, 1931 to 1967

The [[New-York Historical Society]] finding aid for the Brown Brothers Harriman records lists clippings for the merger, including a clipping titled "Knight Woolley elected director" dated between December 12, 1930 and January 23, 1931, photographs of Woolley with W. Averell Harriman dated 1933, and photographs of Woolley with the [[Union Pacific Railroad]] board and the Business Advisory Council between 1936 and 1939.[^2] A Brown Brothers Harriman advertisement of October 1943, marking the firm's 125th anniversary, lists the partners as Moreau D. Brown, Thatcher M. Brown, Prescott S. Bush, Louis Curtis, E. R. Harriman, W. A. Harriman (then newly named ambassador to Russia), Ray Morris and Woolley.[^20] A Brown Brothers Harriman notice in *The Economist* of July 22, 1961 lists "Knight Woolley" among the partners, with Prescott S. Bush, Robert A. Lovett, [[Thomas McCance]] and others; the firm's notices in *Trusts and Estates* for July 1956 and January 1961 list him in the same way.[^21][^22] Photographs dated July 1964 in the finding aid show the senior partners Harriman, Lovett, Bush and Woolley in a newly built room at 59 Wall Street, and a photograph caption dated July 28, 1964 shows them in a post-luncheon discussion; a photograph of Woolley, Harriman and Kouwenhoven at the Harriman estate is dated May 19, 1967.[^2][^23]

The finding aid also records that Woolley "was a director of [[Caledonian Insurance Company]] beginning in the 1930s," and that he later chaired its Finance Committee, to which the bank acted as investment advisor.[^2] An October 1942 survey of insurance directorships held by private bankers wrote that "Knight Woolley is a director of the Caledonian-American," counted thirty insurance companies with board or trustee members who were partners in the bank, and described W. A. Harriman of the same firm as then in London as President Roosevelt's personal representative and Lend-Lease co-ordinator.[^24]

A Securities and Exchange Commission summary of insider transactions reported in the *Chicago Daily Tribune* of July 17, 1943 lists a May purchase of 700 shares of [[American-Hawaiian Steamship Company]] by "Knight Woolley, director."[^25] The *Daily Worker* wrote on October 5, 1946 that W. Averell Harriman exerted influence over Pacific shipping lines, including the American-Hawaiian Steamship Co. as one of the Pacific's "Big Three," "through Attorney Knight Woolley, his man Friday, who represents Harriman's interests on the American-Hawaiian board," and added that Woolley was a partner of the Harriman bank.[^26] A brief in *United States v. Pullman Co.* (Supreme Court, October term 1946, appeal of the United States) lists "Guy Cary and Knight Woolley, directors of the Southern Railway Co." among the members of the board of the Air Reduction Co.[^27]

### Federal Reserve Bank of New York and Guaranty Trust

Sutton wrote in 1983 that Woolley "was also a director of the Federal Reserve Bank," and that "Guaranty Trust was represented by Knight Woolley."[^28] The twenty-fifth annual report of the [[Federal Reserve Bank of New York]], for 1939, states that the bank organized a general committee of New York money-market interests in August 1939, and a foreign exchange committee of five commercial-bank representatives, two representatives of private banks and investment houses, and one each for foreign banks and for Stock Exchange firms. It names "Mr. Knight Woolley, Brown Brothers Harriman & Co." among the members of the foreign exchange committee, "representing private banks and investment houses," with R. F. Loree of Guaranty Trust as chairman.[^29] The bank's Circular No. 1015 of January 8, 1931 announced that the Federal Reserve Board had reappointed Clarence M. Woolley of Greenwich, Connecticut, chairman of American Radiator and Standard Sanitary Corporation, as a Class C director for the three years from January 1, 1931.[^30]

*The Bankers Magazine* of January 1931 listed Guaranty Trust Company of New York among the corporations of which W. A. Harriman was a director.[^13]

### Union Banking Corporation

Sutton's 1976 chapter notes give the 1932 directors of the Union Banking Corporation as taken from the 1932 edition of the Bankers Directory and from Poor's Directory of Directors, and state that "J.L. Guinter and Knight Woolley were also directors."[^31] In his 1983 book on Skull and Bones, Sutton lists eight directors for 1932, among them Woolley.[^28]

On January 14, 1941, Woolley wrote to [[William R. White]], superintendent of banks for the State of New York, about the Union Banking Corporation, which he described as "a state institution under your supervision." The letter names four directors, "E. R. Harriman, [[Ray Morris]], Prescott S. Bush and our manager, H. D. Pennington," as "my partners," and writes on their behalf. It describes the corporation as "in effect a New York office, or agency, of the [[Bank voor Handel en Scheepvaart]], in Rotterdam," and says: "Should the United States enter the war, they feel they might be under some embarrassment because of their connection with the bank, even though we have no financial interest in the Union Banking Corporation, nor do we participate in its earnings." Woolley asked White for "a frank expression from you as to the action" the directors should take.[^32]

The letter also describes the bank's operations. Its only depositor was the Bank voor Handel en Scheepvaart, its activity was limited to occasional payments and a few purchases and sales of securities, and its accounts were frozen under the presidential proclamation of May 10, 1940. The company's accounts were kept at the [[Chase National Bank]], the [[National City Bank]], the Guaranty Trust Company and the Harriman firm itself. Morris or Pennington had to sign checks jointly with the company's president, [[Cornelius Lievense]], or with an assistant, and a representative of the firm had to be present whenever the company's safe deposit box at the Chase Bank was opened.[^32] A report of the corporation issued on January 1, 1941 and reprinted in the same source lists as directors Lievense, E. Roland Harriman, Pennington, Bush, J. Kouwenhoven, Ray Morris and J. G. Groeninger.[^32]

The letter gives the origin of the directorships as a meeting in Europe in 1925 between W. A. Harriman and [[Fritz Thyssen]], after which "various members of the Harriman organizations" served on the board. It states that the directors act "merely as a matter of business courtesy."[^32] White replied on May 13, 1941 that the department "would not feel free to object" if the directors resigned, but that "the department would be gratified if these gentlemen could find it possible to remain on the board during this period of uncertainty."[^33] A memorandum of the [[Treasury Department]] dated August 18, 1941 also concerns the company.[^34]

In the 1942 vesting orders, Woolley's name appears on the Holland-American Trading Corporation and not on the Union Banking Corporation. Vesting Order 248, executed October 20, 1942 and published in the [[Federal Register]] of November 7, 1942, vests the 4,000 shares of the Union Banking Corporation held for the Bank voor Handel en Scheepvaart. Its registered owners are E. Roland Harriman with 3,991 shares, Cornelius Lievense with 4, and Harold D. Pennington, Ray Morris, Prescott S. Bush, H. J. Kouwenhoven and Johann G. Groeninger with one share each. Woolley is not among them.[^35]

Vesting Order 261 covers the Holland-American Trading Corporation. It was executed on October 28, 1942 by [[Leo T. Crowley]], [[Alien Property Custodian]], filed December 18, 1942, and published in the *Federal Register* of December 19, 1942. It vests 500 shares, registered to E. Roland Harriman (491), Cornelius Lievense (4), and one share each to H. D. Pennington, Ray Morris, Knight Woolley, H. J. Kouwenhoven and J. G. Groeninger. The shares "are held for the benefit of Bank voor Handel en Scheepvaart, N. V., Rotterdam, The Netherlands, which bank is owned or controlled by members of the Thyssen family".[^36] The Guardian described the Holland-American and [[Seamless Steel Equipment Corporation]] seizures as affiliates against which "further seizures were made" after the Union Banking Corporation seizure.[^37]

### Sutton's characterization

Sutton wrote that "Averell Harriman, his brother Roland Harriman, and members E.S. James and Knight Woolley, through the Union Bank (in which they held a major interest) were prime financial backers of Hitler."[^28] The sentence is Sutton's.

### The Consolidated Silesian Steel letter

The Guardian reported in September 2004 that a letter of January 1933 from Woolley to W. Averell Harriman concerned the Consolidated Silesian Steel Company (CSSC), a Silesian steel and mining company, after Polish moves to nationalize the plant. The newspaper quotes the letter: "The Consolidated Silesian Steel Company situation has become increasingly complicated, and I have accordingly brought in [[Sullivan and Cromwell]], in order to be sure that our interests are protected." The letter continues: "After studying the situation Foster Dulles is insisting that their man in Berlin get into the picture and obtain the information which the directors here should have. You will recall that Foster is a director and he is particularly anxious to be certain that there is no liability attaching to the American directors." The Guardian cited [[National Archives]] files as the source of the letter and described Woolley as Bush's friend and fellow "bonesman."[^38]

### Council on Foreign Relations and the Luce correspondence

A table of corporations whose officers were members of the [[Council on Foreign Relations]], dated November 1951, lists Brown Brothers Harriman with E. Roland Harriman, Ray Morris, Knight Woolley and Thomas McCance among its officers who were Council members.[^39] A list of membership proposals sent to [[Allen Dulles]] with the agenda for the Council's board meeting of December 17, 1951 names Woolley as the proposer of Grinnell Morris, vice president of the Central Hanover Bank.[^40]

The New-York Historical Society holds the papers of [[Henry Luce]] (MS 3014). Its finding aid lists a correspondence file headed "Woolley, Knight (Mr. and Mrs.)," dated 1936 to 1957.[^41]

### The Hopkins telegram of July 9, 1941

A telegram of July 9, 1941 from the Acting Secretary of State to Ambassador [[John G. Winant]] in London, number 2483, carried a message from [[Harry Hopkins]] about British exports of consumer goods and "the next lend lease bill," and ends "Have seen Woolley." The telegram gives no first name or post for Woolley. The records found do not establish whether the Woolley named is Knight Woolley, whose partner W. Averell Harriman was then in London as Roosevelt's Lend-Lease representative.[^24][^42]

### The Boys' Club and memoir

A letterhead of the Boys' Club of New York in the correspondence of the Medallic Art Company, kept by the American Numismatic Society, lists Knight Woolley as assistant secretary, with E. Roland Harriman as vice president and Robert A. Lovett as assistant secretary.[^43] Woolley privately printed a memoir in 1975, titled *In Retrospect: A Very Personal Memoir*.[^44]

### Papers and dates

The New-York Historical Society holds the records of Brown Brothers Harriman (MS 78). Its people files include a folder headed "Knight Woolley (1895 - [1984]), 1923-1967," and the Tax Department scrapbooks include a folder on the partners from H to W for 1941 to 1964. The finding aid dates his life 1895 to 1984.[^2]
