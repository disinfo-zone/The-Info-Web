Goodwin Watson (1899-1976) was a social psychologist and professor at [[Teachers College]], [[Columbia University]], who headed the Analysis Division of the [[Foreign Broadcast Intelligence Service]] (FBIS) from November 1941 until November 1943. In 1943 a special subcommittee of the [[House Appropriations Committee]] found him unfit for federal employment, and the [[Supreme Court of the United States|Supreme Court]] later held the rider that cut off his pay to be a bill of attainder in [[United States v. Lovett]].[^1]

### Education and early writing

Watson was born in Whitewater, [[Wisconsin]]. He testified in 1943 that he was educated at the State Teachers College at Whitewater, the [[University of Wisconsin]], Denver University, [[Union Theological Seminary]], Columbia University, the [[University of Berlin]] and the [[University of Vienna]], that he had served as a [[Methodist Church|Methodist]] minister and as a director of religious education, that he had taught religious education at Union Theological Seminary, and that he had spent 17 years at Teachers College as instructor, assistant professor and professor.[^2]

His catalogued titles begin with [[The Measurement of Fair-Mindedness]] (1925) and [[Educational Problems for Psychological Study]] (1930), and continue through [[Redirecting Teacher Education]] (1938).[^3] His own list of ten books adds [[Case Studies for Teachers of Religion]], [[Experimentation and Measurement in Religious Education]] (1927), [[Tests of Personality and Character]] (1932) and [[Human Resources]] (1936, prepared for the [[American Council on Education]]). His 1927 study of American attitudes toward Oriental peoples, [[Orient and Occident]], was a research project for the [[Institute of Pacific Relations]].[^4]

In 1939 the [[Council for Pan American Democracy]] sent a delegation to [[Mexico]]. Watson chaired its education subcommittee, and his report was issued by the council as [[Education and Social Welfare in Mexico]], which identifies him as a professor of education at Teachers College.[^5]

### New America and Consumers Union

Watson testified that he returned from a visit to [[Germany]] in 1933, after Hitler had come to power, and that [[New America]] was his attempt to give young Americans an alternative to both the [[Socialist Party of America|Socialist Party]] and the [[Communist Party USA|Communist Party]], with one goal, the introduction of "scientific planning of our economic life under democratic political controls." His first step was a speech to the students of New College at Teachers College. The group that formed around him, Forward America, merged with Young America, headed by a young man named Elliston and led in activity by Richard Storrs Childs, to form New America. Its policy council included [[Harry F. Ward]], a professor of Christian ethics, and Thomas Wright, a New Jersey pastor. Watson described the membership as idealistic young people and "tired radicals," and said a cleavage between Ward and Wright on one side and himself on the other over method led him to drop out late in 1937 and to resign in the summer of 1938.[^6]

He testified that he was on the board of [[Consumers Union]], that Edward Adams had approached him about standing for it, and that its directors were elected by mail by the membership. He sponsored the American Investors Union, which he described as a development of Consumers Union, and said he had no further connection with it.[^7]

In August 1934 [[Common Sense (magazine)|Common Sense]] published his article "The Great Choice: Reformation or Transformation?"[^8]

### Society for the Psychological Study of Social Issues

In September 1940 the [[Society for the Psychological Study of Social Issues]] (SPSSI) appointed a Committee on Morale under [[Gardner Murphy]]. In September 1941 the society postponed other yearbooks to concentrate on civilian morale and named Watson editor. The volume was planned with [[Kurt Lewin]], then the society's president, and [[Theodore Newcomb]], its secretary. The resulting [[Civilian Morale]], the society's second yearbook, drew on a round table held in 1941 whose participants included [[Rensis Likert]], [[Gregory Bateson]], [[Paul Lazarsfeld]] and Lewin. Watson wrote the round table's summary, in which he was identified as "now with the Foreign Broadcast Monitoring Service."[^9] By his own account he also wrote the volume's chapters on the factors in morale, on morale and labor unions, and on morale among the unemployed of 1934.[^4]

The historian Ian Nicholson calls Watson "SPSSI's first president" in a 1998 article in the Journal of Social Issues.[^10] A 2011 TC Today photograph caption describes him as the "founder of the Society for the Psychological Study of Social Issues."[^11] The 1942 volume names Lewin as the society's president when the book was planned.[^9]

### Foreign Broadcast Intelligence Service, 1941 to 1943

[[Lloyd Free]] and [[Harold Graves]] recruited Watson for the service. Graves announced in October 1941 that the "eminent social psychologist of Columbia University" would accept a P-6 grade and head the Analysis Section.[^12] Watson accepted in a letter dated October 22, 1941, which read in part that "the urgency of the world crisis and the importance of the analysis of broadcasts have grown in my thinking to outweigh my doubts and reservations."[^13]

Watson testified in April 1943 that he had been connected with the [[Federal Communications Commission]] "Since November 15, 1941," that he was responsible for about 40 people, and that the division's weekly and special reports went to the [[United States Department of State|State Department]], Military Intelligence, Naval Intelligence and the [[Lend-Lease Administration]]. He gave the division's output as 70 issues of the Weekly Review of Official Foreign Broadcasts, 18 issues of the Radio Report on the Far East, 13 issues of the Central European Radio Analysis and some 70 special reports, written for agencies that included the [[Office of Strategic Services]], the [[Coordinator of Inter-American Affairs]] and the [[Board of Economic Warfare]].[^14]

A records description of the service gives November 15, 1941 as the start of his directorship of the Analysis Division and November 15, 1943 as the day Newcomb succeeded him.[^15] A CIA history states that on November 17, 1941 he "became the first chief of the analysis section."[^16] Newcomb was second only to Watson in the division by February 1943.[^17] [[Daniel Bessner]]'s dissertation on [[Hans Speier]] states that after Watson left under pressure Speier became the service's Acting Chief Analyst, overseeing thirty people, citing a Newcomb letter of December 4, 1943.[^18]

In September 1942 Watson and [[Nathan Leites]] went to [[London]] to set up an analysis operation working with the [[Office of War Information]]. Watson remained there only a short time, and a two-man staff stayed through the war.[^19] From London he called the [[BBC]] monitoring system inadequate, because British and American interests were often at variance, and recommended FBIS posts in [[Cairo]], [[New Delhi]], [[Melbourne]], [[Chungking]], [[Vladivostok]], [[Stockholm]], [[Gibraltar]] and [[Istanbul]]. A November 1942 memorandum by Graves called the recommendation "not feasible."[^20] Earlier in 1942 he told the Portland office that its Chinese and Russian copy fell far short of what the analysts needed (March 23) and told Free that the BBC was not covering the required programs and that the only solution was monitoring in England for about 20 hours of broadcasts daily (April 4).[^21]

On December 30, 1942 Watson proposed distributing FBIS analysts among other offices, to bring them "closer to the people who use our findings."[^22] In April 1943 an agreement moved the Analysis Division into the [[Social Security Building]], where OWI was housed.[^22] On January 19, 1943 he wrote that the division was already working an average of 50 hours a week, so that a staff cut was the only answer to a pay-raise limit.[^23] As late as November 5, 1943 he wrote to the San Francisco bureau's Bradford Coolidge that analysis would be developed there, after conferences involving [[Owen Lattimore]], then newly named head of the West Coast OWI, [[Robert D. Leigh]] and [[Audrey Menefee]].[^24]

Watson's correspondence reached other agencies. On July 7, 1942 he wrote to [[Nelson Rockefeller]] about an offer that the [[CIAA]] had made to the FBIS analyst [[John W. Gardner]]. He wrote that the service was reluctant to let Gardner go, and that the two offices should decide where he could do the most good. On February 8, 1943 he answered an OSS request that would have required two more analysts, and suggested a letter to help FBIS obtain supplemental funds.[^25] Watson was one of the two creators of [[Central European Radio Analysis]], and [[Frederick L. Schuman]] left [[Williams College]] in October 1942 to join the division's German Section.[^26]

[[Frank B. Keefe]] later told the House that Watson's division analyzed the broadcasts from foreign countries and interpreted them "for our Army and Navy."[^27]

### Charges before the House, 1941 to 1943

[[Martin Dies]], chairman of the [[House Un-American Activities Committee]] (HUAC), wrote to [[James L. Fly]] of the Federal Communications Commission on November 18, 1941, expressing "deep concern" over the selection of a man who had "been a propagandist for communism and for the [[Soviet Union]] for many years." Dies named thirteen organizations he called communist fronts and said Watson belonged to all of them. Fly replied that Watson belonged to one of them, Consumers Union, which he described as "a respected research organ." Fly also said that of some 200 published articles by Watson, only two or three concerned the Soviet Union, and those were objective studies.[^28]

The House attached a rider barring Watson's salary to an appropriation bill, and the [[Senate Appropriations Committee]] deleted it. Watson testified before that committee on February 4, 1942. Afterward he wrote that no senator had charged him with being a communist, though there was "considerable hostility" to his "socialistic views," which he called a "new standard" for federal employment.[^29]

On January 19, 1943 [[Hamilton Fish III]] read into the House record a list of Watson's affiliations from 1936 to 1939, describing him as a man who "comes from Warwick in my district." The list included an advisory committee of [[Reconciliation Trips]] to the U.S.S.R.; an advisory board of the [[American Students Union]]; a directorship of Consumers Union, which Fish described as "radical, left-wingers, and Communists"; leadership of the [[Open Road]], which Fish said sponsored trips to the Soviet Union; a 1939 petition against the Dies committee; an endorsement for the [[American League for Peace and Democracy]]; and a 1938 speech to teachers sponsored by Teachers College, as reported by the [[New York Herald Tribune]] on June 29, 1938. Fish also quoted a speech of November 28, 1941 in which Dies told the House that he had informed Fly that Watson had "probably associated himself with a large number of front organizations of the Communist Party." Each entry is Fish's characterization.[^30]

On February 1, 1943 Dies gave a House speech attacking thirty-nine employees, among them Watson, Schuman and [[William E. Dodd Jr.]] Dies called Watson "Chief Broadcast Analyst of the Federal Communications Commission, at a salary of $6,500," said the salary had been raised from $5,600 after the House once refused to pay it, and cited a [[The World Tomorrow|World Tomorrow]] article and a Common Sense article in which, he said, Watson favored "a sudden, complete, and violent, that is, Communist, change from our present form of society to a Socialist society." He said that four folders of exhibits documented "Goodwin Watson's Communist views and affiliations." He also said that Dodd had been refused a State Department visa on the basis of "information furnished the Department by our committee and [[Federal Bureau of Investigation|F. B. I.]]" The speech described the men as "irresponsible, unrepresentative, crackpot, radical bureaucrats." Dodd, Watson and Schuman headed the list.[^31] The same period brought House attacks on other FBIS employees, [[Peter Rhodes]], Menefee and Speier.[^32]

On February 4, 1943 [[Joe Hendricks]] of [[Florida]] gave notice of an amendment to the Post Office and Treasury appropriation bill forbidding the use of its funds to pay thirty-nine named employees, including "Goodwin B. Watson," Schuman and Dodd. On February 5 the House, sitting as the Committee of the Whole, rejected the amendment, 130 to 137 on a division and 146 to 153 on tellers, and then agreed, 163 to 111 on tellers, to a separate amendment naming only William Pickens.[^33]

On March 31, 1943, in a House exchange with a [[California]] member over a claim Dies disputed, Dies said that "We were investigating Goodwin Watson and Fred Shuman" and that a subcommittee headed by [[Joe Starnes]] of [[Alabama]], whom he called his "distinguished and able colleague," would make a report.[^34]

### The Appropriations subcommittee, 1943

[[House Resolution 105]], adopted February 9, 1943, authorized the Committee on Appropriations, "acting through a special subcommittee," to examine charges that employees were unfit by reason of present or past association with organizations whose aims were subversive to the government. The subcommittee was [[John H. Kerr]] of [[North Carolina]] (chairman), [[Albert Gore Sr.|Albert Gore]] of [[Tennessee]], [[Clinton P. Anderson]] of [[New Mexico]], [[D. Lane Powers]] of [[New Jersey]] and Keefe; its counsel was Major Matt H. Allen of North Carolina. Its first sitting was on Friday, April 9, 1943, and Watson testified over several sittings.[^35] The hearings were held in secret executive session. The accused were permitted to testify, the lawyers of their employing agencies were excluded, and the evidence came "largely" from reports of the Dies Committee and from FBI reports "treated as too confidential to be made public."[^1]

On April 7, 1943 Kerr told the House that the committee had been asking for six weeks for the full evidence and had been able to get it for only "3 of the 38 men who are charged in this resolution." Dies replied, "You have evidence on 10."[^36]

Watson opened with a written statement. It reported letters or telegrams from psychologists including [[Harold Lasswell]], [[Hadley Cantril]], [[Gordon Allport]], [[Henry A. Murray]], Gardner Murphy and [[J. B. Rhine]], a letter from Teachers College's dean, [[William F. Russell]], a petition of 57 signers and a protest signed by 188 students, and a resolution of the [[Eastern Sociological Society]] dated April 25, 1942.[^37] It listed 36 organizations in which he had paid dues, attended conventions, held office or given time, among them the [[Committee for National Morale]], the [[Institute for Propaganda Analysis]], the [[Foreign Policy Association]], the [[American Civil Liberties Union]], New America and Consumers Union, and stated that he had never been a member of an organization known to him to be communist in origin or control.[^38]

On "The Great Choice," exhibited as charge 18, Watson testified that it was the most radical article he had written, nine years earlier, that it argued for thorough and fundamental change that was peaceful and legal, that it was an academic idea that had appealed to him at the time, and that "I do not now believe it to be true." Keefe asked whether Watson had ever written an article disavowing it, and Watson answered that he had not. Keefe also asked whether a Congress "having the interests and welfare of the American way at heart" would be justified in calling a person to account for writings advocating "the destruction of the profit system," and Watson answered "Yes; I would." Asked whether such writings, standing alone, should put a government on notice and demand some investigation, he answered "I would think so."[^8]

The subcommittee reported on April 21, 1943. It described its work as "in the nature of an inquest of office," adopted a definition of subversive activity as "conduct intentionally destructive of or inimical to the Government of the United States," and listed twelve organizations with which Watson had admitted association: the American League for Peace and Democracy, the American Committee for Democracy and Intellectual Freedom, the American Student Union, the [[American Youth Congress]], the [[Conference on Pan-American Democracy]], Consumers Union, the Coordinating Committee to Lift the Embargo, the Descendants of the American Revolution, the [[International Workers Order]], the [[League of American Writers]], the [[National Emergency Conference for Democratic Rights]], and the Medical Bureau and North American Committee to Aid Spanish Democracy. It stated that these organizations had not been adjudged subversive by the courts or by Congress. It found that Watson had associated with men and associations whose aims were subversive, had written articles for magazines "reputedly controlled by subversive interests," and had criticized the profit system and advocated its overthrow, and concluded that his membership and association and his views as expressed in his writings "constitute subversive activity within the definition adopted by your committee," so that he was "unfit for the present to continue in Government employment." The same report found insufficient evidence to recommend that Schuman be held unfit, and found Dodd unfit. The Committee on Appropriations approved the findings and directed the offering of an amendment to the urgent deficiency appropriation bill, H.R. 2714.[^39]

### Section 304 and the November 1943 cutoff

On May 17, 1943 Keefe read excerpts of Watson's testimony to the House. In the course of the reading he quoted Watson's 1934 article and said that when Watson was asked about it, Watson answered "I just do not believe in that any more. That was the effusion of an academic mind written in 1934," and "I disavow myself of that sort of philosophy."[^40]

The text of Section 304 as proposed in the committee's report barred payment of the salaries of Watson, Dodd and [[Robert Morss Lovett]] without a date or a reappointment clause.[^39] The version the House attached to the [[Urgent Deficiency Appropriation Act of 1943]] barred payment from appropriated funds after November 15, 1943 to Lovett, Watson and Dodd, unless the President had appointed them with the advice and consent of the Senate, and excepted accrued leave and salary and payment for service on a jury or in the armed forces.[^1] On May 18, 1943 Kerr told the House that these employees "have no property rights in their offices" and that "One Congress can take away their rights given them by another."[^41]

The Senate Appropriations Committee eliminated the provision and the Senate sustained that action. After the first conference report the Senate voted 69 to 0 against a report that kept it, the House insisted, and the Senate adopted it after the fifth conference report.[^1] [[Franklin D. Roosevelt]] signed the bill and stated that the Senate had "yielded, as I have been forced to yield, to avoid delaying our conduct of the war," and that the provision was "not only unwise and discriminatory, but unconstitutional."[^1]

Watson and Dodd kept working for a few days after the cutoff "to establish a court case" before they resigned. Schuman had already resigned and returned to Williams College.[^42]

### United States v. Lovett, 1946

Lovett, Watson and Dodd sued in the [[United States Court of Claims]] for pay for work performed after November 15, 1943, while the agencies kept them at work for varying periods. Two judges of that court had read Section 304 as "a mere stoppage of disbursing routine, nothing more."[^1]

The Supreme Court heard argument on May 3 to 6, 1946 and decided the case on June 3, 1946. Justice [[Hugo Black]], writing for the Court, held that Section 304 "falls precisely within the category of Congressional actions which the Constitution barred" as bills of attainder, and that it "operates as a legislative decree of perpetual exclusion" from government service. He wrote that "This permanent proscription from any opportunity to serve the Government is punishment, and of a most severe type." Black concluded that "legislative trials and punishments were too dangerous to liberty to exist in the nation of free men they envisioned. And so they proscribed bills of attainder. Section 304 is one."[^1]

The judgment in favor of Lovett, Watson and Dodd was affirmed. Justice [[Felix Frankfurter]], joined by Justice [[Stanley Reed]], concurred on the ground that the Court should avoid the constitutional questions, and Justice [[Robert H. Jackson]] took no part.[^1]

### Later career and publications

Watson's later titles include [[Youth After Conflict]] ([[Association Press]], 1947), [[Action for Unity]] ([[Harper and Brothers]], 1947, with a 1946 edition issued by the [[Office of Jewish Information]]), [[Some Hypotheses Concerning Social Change]] (Teachers College, 1950), [[What Psychology Can We Trust?]] (Teachers College, 1961), [[No Room at the Bottom]] ([[Project on the Educational Implications of Automation]], [[National Education Association]], 1963), and [[Social Psychology: Issues and Insights]] ([[Lippincott]], 1966).[^3][^43][^44]

He edited [[Concepts for Social Change]], published by the [[National Training Laboratories]] in 1967.[^45] The [[Journal of Applied Behavioral Science]] was founded in 1965 under Watson's editorship, according to Teachers College.[^46]

In April 1967 the [[United States Office of Education]] published [[Innovation in Higher Education: Developments, Research, and Priorities]], produced under a contract with [[Duke University]], with sections by [[Samuel Baskin]], [[Sister Patricia Jean Manion]], Watson and [[James P. Dixon]]. Watson wrote the third section, "Innovation: Processes, Practice, and Research." The volume identified him as Distinguished Service Professor and Director of Applied Behavioral Sciences at [[Newark State College]] and as serving part-time as Associate Director for Program Development and Research of the [[Union for Research and Experimentation in Higher Education]], whose president was Baskin.[^47] The Union's published history dates its founding to 1964 by ten college presidents, names Baskin as first president in 1965, and records the 1969 adoption of the name Union for Experimenting Colleges and Universities.[^48] The publisher Pearson states that Watson was "the founder of the [[Union Graduate School]]" and "Chief of the Analysis Division of the Foreign Broadcast Intelligence Service."[^49]

An obituary of Watson by Newcomb appeared in the American Psychologist in May 1979.[^50]
