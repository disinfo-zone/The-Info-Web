Brent Scowcroft (March 19, 1925 to August 6, 2020) was a retired [[United States Air Force|Air Force]] lieutenant general who served as national security adviser to President [[Gerald R. Ford]] from 1975 to 1977 and to President [[George H.W. Bush]] from 1989 to 1993.[^1][^2] He chaired the President's Commission on Strategic Forces in 1983, was a member of the [[Tower Commission]] in 1987, was vice chairman of [[Kissinger Associates]] until January 1989, founded [[The Scowcroft Group]] in 1994 and chaired the [[President's Foreign Intelligence Advisory Board]] from 2001 to 2005.[^3][^4]

### Air Force and the Nixon White House

[[Henry B. Gonzalez]], the chairman of the [[House Banking Committee]], told the House on April 28, 1992 that early in his career Scowcroft served one year as air attaché at the [[United States Embassy in Belgrade]] in [[Yugoslavia]].[^5] After earning a Ph.D. and working in academia from 1962 to 1968, he held a succession of national security posts in the [[Department of Defense]].[^5] In 1971 President [[Richard Nixon]] appointed him military aide to the President, and in 1973 [[Henry A. Kissinger|Kissinger]] chose him as deputy assistant to the President for national security affairs.[^5] The Scowcroft Group's biography of him describes a twenty-nine-year military career that began with graduation from [[United States Military Academy|West Point]] and ended at the rank of lieutenant general after service as Deputy National Security Advisor.[^3] [[R. W. Apple Jr.]], who wrote the introduction to the Tower Commission report, described him as "a retired Air Force general who served several Republican presidents as a key foreign-policy adviser."[^6]

### Deputy to Kissinger, 1973 to 1975

A memorandum for the record that Admiral [[Thomas H. Moorer]] prepared on October 26, 1973 reproduces Kissinger's record of schedule for a meeting in Washington on the night of October 24 to 25, 1973 (10:30 p.m. to 3:30 a.m.), which lists the attendees as Kissinger, [[James R. Schlesinger]], [[William Colby]], Moorer, Scowcroft, [[Alexander Haig]] and [[Jonathan Howe]]. The memorandum lists among the actions taken that day the setting of [[DEFCON]] III, during the [[Yom Kippur War]].[^7] Kissinger later recalled that immediately after the alert was set he instructed Scowcroft to call [[Anatoly Dobrynin]] to tell him to desist from all action until there was a U.S. reply, "and to warn him that any unilateral actions by the Soviet Union would have the most serious consequences."[^7] The Foreign Relations of the United States editors note that [[Ray Cline|Cline]] called the meeting a "curious little rump NSC meeting" in the Winter 1974 to 1975 issue of [[Foreign Policy]].[^7] Gonzalez told the House that Scowcroft often took charge of the [[National Security Council]] while Kissinger was fulfilling his duties as Secretary of State.[^8]

### National security adviser to Ford, 1975 to 1977

On May 27, 1975 Ford met in the [[White House Cabinet Room|Cabinet Room]] with Schlesinger, Colby, [[Donald Rumsfeld]], [[Robert Ingersoll]] and Scowcroft, who is listed in the memorandum of conversation as Deputy Assistant to the President for National Security Affairs, about a [[New York Times]] article by [[Seymour Hersh]] on American submarine intercept missions in Soviet waters. Ford said: "Somehow someone must be made an example of." Scowcroft said: "The real problem is not the press. The real problem is the people who are leaking the material to the press. They are the ones we have to go after."[^9]

At a news conference on November 3, 1975, Ford announced that "Brent Scowcroft, who has been serving ably for 3 years as Deputy Assistant at the White House, will move up to Assistant to the President for National Security Affairs" when Kissinger relinquished that post to devote his full time to being Secretary of State.[^10] The report of the Tower Commission summarizes that "Under President Ford, Lt Gen Brent Scowcroft became National Security Advisor, with Henry Kissinger remaining as Secretary of State."[^11]

In April 1976 Scowcroft briefed Vice President [[Nelson Rockefeller]] before an April 20 courtesy call on [[Anastasio Somoza]] at the Nicaraguan Ambassador's residence in Washington, covering Nicaraguan affairs and U.S.-Nicaraguan relations.[^12] Gonzalez told the House that Scowcroft resigned the post during the [[Jimmy Carter|Carter]] administration but stayed active as a member of the President's general advisory committee on arms control.[^13]

### Kissinger Associates and the BNL account, 1982 to 1989

In 1982 Scowcroft joined Kissinger in setting up Kissinger Associates, according to Gonzalez, who said Scowcroft served as vice chairman and head of the firm's Washington, D.C. office until he became national security adviser in January 1989.[^14] The firm's biography of him states that in that capacity he advised and assisted US and foreign corporate leaders on global joint venture opportunities, strategic planning and risk assessment.[^3]

[[Frederick Lacey]], who was appointed as special counsel in the [[BNL Scandal|Banca Nazionale del Lavoro]] investigation, wrote that Scowcroft had served as vice president of Kissinger Associates and had headed its Washington, D.C. office before joining the Bush administration as national security adviser in January 1989.[^15] Lacey's report states: "It is clear that, while at Kissinger Associates, Scowcroft and Eagleburger came into contact with BNL management. I have seen nothing, however, to suggest that they knew of BNL-Atlanta's wrongful loans to Iraq or influenced the prosecution of this matter."[^15] The passage refers to [[Lawrence Eagleburger]], who had been president of the firm and became deputy secretary of state.

Gonzalez told the House that Kissinger, Scowcroft and Eagleburger "maintain that they were unaware of the BNL loans to [[Iraq]]. I offer no definitive proof that they were aware of the BNL loans."[^16] He reported that Scowcroft worked on the account of [[Banca Nazionale del Lavoro|BNL]] at Kissinger Associates, met BNL management on numerous occasions, and briefed the bank's board on international political and economic developments on three occasions between 1986 and 1989, and that when the president of BNL traveled to the United States for the annual International Monetary Fund conference he met Kissinger and Scowcroft in [[New York City|New York]].[^17] Gonzalez reported that a former head of BNL's American operations said he attended a 1987 meeting in New York with [[Alan Stoga]], the head of BNL's Rome headquarters, Kissinger, Scowcroft and Eagleburger, convened to give BNL advice on doing business in several countries including India, and that Stoga and Scowcroft took the BNL officers to lunch afterward.[^18] Gonzalez said: "BNL was a client of Mr. Scowcroft's while he was the vice-chairman of Kissinger Associates. Mr. Scowcroft regularly provided advice to BNL's management and received hefty fees in return."[^19]

Luigi Sardelli, BNL's regional manager in the United States, gave the Banking Committee written answers in 1993 in which he stated that in February 1989 Dr. Pedde, BNL's managing director, asked him to contact Stoga, that he understood the bank was looking for space in Washington and that, because Scowcroft had just been appointed to the Bush White House, his mission may have been to find out whether a room would be available at Kissinger Associates in Washington for a BNL officer. Sardelli wrote that he did not recall posing the question to Stoga, and that he never discussed any loan to Iraq with Kissinger Associates.[^20]

### Scowcroft Commission, 1983

On January 3, 1983 President [[Ronald Reagan]] announced the establishment of a bipartisan Commission on Strategic Forces (the [[Scowcroft Commission]]) to review the strategic modernization program, with particular focus on the land-based intercontinental ballistic missile system and its basing alternatives, and named "The Honorable Brent Scowcroft, former Assistant to the President for National Security Affairs, to serve as Chairman." The other members were [[Thomas C. Reed]] as vice chairman, [[Nicholas Brady]], [[Harold Brown]], [[William Clements]], [[John Deutch]], Alexander M. Haig Jr., [[Richard Helms]], [[John Lyons]] of the [[AFL-CIO]], Vice Admiral Levering Smith and [[James Woolsey]].[^21] The statement notes that Reagan signed the establishing executive order the same day in the Cabinet Room with Scowcroft, several commission members, Secretary of State [[George P. Shultz]] and Secretary of Defense [[Caspar Weinberger]] present.[^21] Executive Order 12400 set the commission at no more than fifteen members, directed it to report by February 18, 1983, provided that information supplied to or developed by it would not, to the extent permitted by law, be available for public inspection, and assigned its administrative support to the Secretary of Defense.[^22]

On April 19, 1983 Reagan, acknowledging "Brent Scowcroft, the Commission's Chairman," announced that the commission had unanimously arrived at its recommendations and that he would send them to Congress with his endorsement; he said the commission had held dozens of formal meetings and talked to over 200 technical experts.[^23] On April 9, 1984 Reagan issued a statement on the commission's final report, saying that it reiterated the original recommendations, namely an arms control structure with incentives for stability at reduced levels, deployment of 100 [[MX missile|MX missiles]] and development of a small single-warhead ICBM, and that it recommended extreme caution in proceeding to engineering development of an active strategic defense system.[^24]

### Council on Foreign Relations, 1984 to 1986

The annual report of the [[Council on Foreign Relations]] for July 1, 1984 to June 30, 1985 lists Scowcroft among the directors with terms expiring in 1986, alongside Harold Brown, [[Lane Kirkland]], [[Juanita Kreps]] and [[Robert Scalapino]]. He is listed on the Membership Committee chaired by [[Theodore Hesburgh]] and on the five-member board Nominating Committee with [[Nicholas Katzenbach]], [[Philip Geyelin]], [[William D. Rogers]] and [[Marina Whitman]]. The report records a Council session of former national security advisers, chaired jointly by [[Winston Lord]] and Eagleburger, at which [[Zbigniew Brzezinski]], [[Walt Rostow]] and Scowcroft spoke, and a Council book, Michael Krepon's *Strategic Stalemate*, with parallel prefaces by Scowcroft and [[Paul Warnke]].[^25]

### Tower Commission, 1987

Scowcroft was one of the members of the President's Special Review Board, chaired by [[John Tower]], with [[Edmund Muskie]] as the other member.[^26] The board examined the [[Iran-Contra affair]] and the role of the National Security Council in it. Apple's introduction reports that Scowcroft considered the lack of written records "appalling" and added: "It may be that some went into the shredder, but we can't prove it."[^26]

### National Security Directive 26, October 2, 1989

National Security Directive 26, "U.S. Policy Toward the Persian Gulf," is dated October 2, 1989 and was addressed to the Vice President, the Secretaries of State, the Treasury, Defense and Energy, the Attorney General, the Director of the Office of Management and Budget, the Assistant to the President for National Security Affairs, the Director of Central Intelligence, the Chairman of the Joint Chiefs of Staff and the directors of the Arms Control and Disarmament Agency and the United States Information Agency. Its section on Iraq reads in part: "Normal relations between the United States and Iraq would serve our longer-term interests and promote stability in both the Gulf and the Middle East. The United States Government should propose economic and political incentives for Iraq to moderate its behavior and to increase our influence with Iraq." It continues that "any illegal use of chemical and/or biological weapons will lead to economic and political sanctions" and that "Any breach by Iraq of IAEA safeguards in its nuclear program will result in a similar response." A further paragraph states: "We should pursue, and seek to facilitate, opportunities for U.S. firms to participate in the reconstruction of the Iraqi economy, particularly in the energy area," and that the United States should consider sales of non-lethal military assistance, training courses and medical exchanges to Iraq's defense establishment on a case by case basis.[^27]

Gonzalez told the House that after the BNL offices in [[Atlanta]] were raided in August 1989, the White House and the National Security Council acted to protect the [[Commodity Credit Corporation Iraq Program|credit guarantee program for Iraq]] that the [[Commodity Credit Corporation]] ran. He said that State Department officials, including Secretary [[James A. Baker III|Baker]], "repeatedly referred to NSD-26 which stated that 'the President wished to improve relations with Iraq'" as the justification for pressing other agencies to approve the program.[^28] State Department talking points for the November 8, 1989 meeting of the National Advisory Council deputies, which Gonzalez reproduced, read: "In NSD-26, the President mandated that we seek to improve and expand our relationship with Iraq."[^29]

Gonzalez stated that under Scowcroft's direction the NSC staff was heavily involved in winning approval of the one billion dollar program for fiscal 1990 in late 1989, and that Scowcroft himself was directly involved in trying to win the release of the second 500 million dollar installment, which was delayed because of the BNL scandal.[^30] A State Department memorandum of March 5, 1990, which Gonzalez quoted, states: "National Security Council staff [NSCS] contacted the USDA March 2 to inquire about the delay after the Iraqi Ambassador complained to General Scowcroft."[^31] Gonzalez said the [[United States Department of Agriculture|Department of Agriculture]] sent a background memorandum to Scowcroft in preparation for the NSC meeting in May 1990 stating that "it cannot be overemphasized that any constraint on CCC credit guarantees must not be based on a foreign policy rationale," and said the program was suspended at that meeting. He also quoted a May 25, 1990 Treasury Department memorandum summarizing a meeting of Agriculture, Treasury and State officials: "The NSC prevailed on Agriculture to say only that their investigation showed the improprieties may have occurred and remained silent on the suspension."[^32]

On May 18, 1992 Gonzalez named Scowcroft, [[C. Boyden Gray]], [[John Schmitz]], [[Richard Haass]], [[Clayton Yeutter]] and others as White House and NSC staff involved in the decision and in the administrative review of the BNL scandal, said that "many White House and NSC staff were involved" and asked why neither Scowcroft nor Eagleburger had recused himself, and said: "So the White House and NSC, armed with National Security Directive 26, sprang into action in order to save the CCC Program for Iraq."[^33] The same Record pages print his letter of October 1, 1991 asking Scowcroft for the President's report on exports of nuclear, biological, chemical and ballistic missile technology to Iraq, Scowcroft's reply of November 1, 1991 offering a copy of the report "as a courtesy," Gonzalez's letter of April 29, 1992 asking Scowcroft for all NSC information on [[Carlos Cardoen]] and [[Gerald Bull]], and his letter of the same date to Bush asking that Scowcroft and the other named staff be made available for interview.[^34]

Gonzalez described an interagency group of lawyers chaired by [[C. Nicholas Rostow]], the NSC's legal adviser, which met in April 1991 and which he called the "Rostow gang." He said the group "gave the White House a direct hand in regulating the flow of information to the Congress, thus limiting oversight of Iraq policy."[^35] In a May 2, 1991 letter to Bush, Gonzalez detailed his concerns about the links among BNL, Kissinger, Scowcroft and Eagleburger.[^36]

The report of the independent counsel Lacey, printed in the Banking Committee record, quotes a February 8, 1991 memorandum from Rostow to Scowcroft at the National Security Council on the BNL indictment, which names [[Robert Mueller]] and states: "I have informed Robert Mueller ... of your reluctance to seek an indictment of Iraqi government officials whom we are unlikely to take into custody."[^37]

[[Alan Friedman]] wrote that [[Bryan Siebert]] of the [[United States Department of Energy|Energy Department]] "had been waging a lonely and unsuccessful battle to get James Baker and National Security Adviser Brent Scowcroft to do something about the steady flow of U.S. exports that were materially assisting Iraq's nuclear weapons program." Friedman wrote that on May 18, 1990 Scowcroft telephoned Secretary of Agriculture Yeutter and asked him "not to put out any press release today saying we are terminating the program" of credit guarantees, and that Scowcroft's aides "objected to any measures 'that singled out Iraq.'" Friedman quoted Scowcroft's staff as saying of cancelling the second 500 million dollars: "We would be shooting ourselves in the foot." Friedman wrote that [[Matrix Churchill]] "was not discussed at the cabinet meeting, even though the [[Central Intelligence Agency|CIA]] had reported its activities to both the [[White House]] and the [[State Department]] in November 1989." According to Friedman, Rostow told Gonzalez's investigator that "it would be a cold day in hell before Congress got NSD 26."[^38]

### Beijing after Tiananmen, 1989

On July 2, 1989 Scowcroft and Deputy Secretary of State Eagleburger met [[Deng Xiaoping]] and Premier [[Li Peng]] in the [[Great Hall of the People]] in [[Beijing]]. The memorandum of conversation records Deng saying that the turmoil in China had begun shortly after Bush took office, that the United States was too deeply involved in it, that U.S. foreign policy had cornered China, and that the aim of what he called the counter-revolutionary rebellion had been to overthrow the People's Republic. Deng said that the person who tied the knot should be the one to untie it, and that his hope was that the United States would do so.[^39]

Scowcroft and Eagleburger returned to Beijing in December 1989. At a banquet on December 9 Qian toasted the health of Scowcroft, Eagleburger and Lilley and the resumption and development of normal Sino-U.S. relations. On December 10 Scowcroft, Eagleburger, Ambassador [[James R. Lilley]], [[Chase Untermeyer]] and the National Security Council's Asian affairs director [[Douglas Paal]] attended a working luncheon with Foreign Minister [[Qian Qichen]] at the Diaoyutai State Guesthouse, and Scowcroft met Li Peng from 8:45 to 9:40 a.m., with a meeting with the General Secretary scheduled for 9:45. Li Peng asked Scowcroft whether the United States considered the Cold War ended after the Malta meeting.[^40]

### Gulf War decisions, 1990

On July 28, 1990, in a telephone call from [[Kennebunkport, Maine|Kennebunkport]], Maine, Bush told King [[Hussein of Jordan|Hussein]], who was about to travel to Baghdad, that he had "just had lunch with Barbara, my daughter, and Brent Scowcroft," that there were problems with Iraq's behavior he would rather not discuss on that line, and that the United States had tried to send signals to [[Saddam Hussein]] with a congressional delegation. The King asked Bush to tell Scowcroft hello.[^41] On August 2, 1990, aboard [[Air Force One]] en route to [[Aspen, Colorado|Aspen]], Colorado, Bush told King Hussein and President [[Hosni Mubarak]] that the United States was "very surprised at this outrageous invasion."[^42] On August 3 Bush told Prime Minister [[Margaret Thatcher]] by telephone that "Brent will be talking with Woerner" ([[Manfred Wörner]]) and that "Brent is here and will get in touch"; Thatcher thanked him for "all the kind things you said at Aspen," and Bush replied: "I told Brent to keep you informed."[^43]

On August 2, 1990 Iraqi tanks sped the 80 miles to Kuwait City to begin the occupation of [[Kuwait]].[^44] [[James P. Pfiffner]] wrote that on August 3, 1990, at a White House meeting, Scowcroft made the argument that the invasion was something the United States could not leave unanswered.[^44] Pfiffner quoted a White House official as saying: "it was Brent's presentation at one of the meetings on August 3, that Friday after the invasion, that made clear what the stakes were, crystallized people's thinking and galvanized support for a very strong response." Pfiffner's note attributes the quotation to an article by [[Andrew Rosenthal]] in the New York Times of February 21, 1991.[^45] In late September 1990 [[Colin Powell]] went to the White House with [[Richard Cheney]] to meet with Bush and Scowcroft, for what Pfiffner described as the most formal presentation of the sanctions option to the president.[^46]

### Essay against an attack on Iraq, August 2002

On August 15, 2002 Scowcroft published an essay in the [[Wall Street Journal]] under the headline "Don't Attack Saddam."[^47][^48] A news report of the essay quotes him as writing that if the United States "were seen to be turning our backs" on the Israeli-Palestinian dispute "in order to go after Iraq, there would be an explosion of outrage against us."[^47] The same report quotes him as writing: "There is a virtual consensus in the world against an attack on Iraq at this time. So long as that sentiment persists, it would require the U.S. to pursue a virtual go-it-alone strategy against Iraq, making any military operations correspondingly more difficult and expensive."[^47]

### President's Foreign Intelligence Advisory Board, 2001 to 2005

On October 5, 2001 the White House announced that President [[George W. Bush]] intended to appoint "Lieutenant General Brent Scowcroft who will serve as Chairman" of the President's Foreign Intelligence Advisory Board, for a term of two years, together with Cresencio Arcos, [[Jim Barksdale]], Robert Addison Day, [[Stephen Friedman]], [[Alfred Lerner]], [[Ray Hunt|Ray Lee Hunt]], [[Rita Hauser]], [[David E. Jeremiah]], [[Arnold Kanter]], [[James C. Langdon Jr.]], [[Elisabeth Pate-Cornell]], [[John Streicker]], [[Pete Wilson]] and [[Philip Zelikow]].[^49]

[[Steve Clemons]] wrote on October 27, 2005 that Scowcroft was not asked by the President to return to the board when his term expired at the end of 2004, that most had assumed he would continue as chairman, and that at a gathering at the home of Brzezinski just after the New Year Scowcroft reportedly said: "I just got fired." Clemons wrote that on January 6 Scowcroft spoke with Brzezinski at the [[New America Foundation]].[^50] [[Jeffrey Goldberg]] published "Breaking Ranks: What Turned Brent Scowcroft Against the Bush Administration?" in The New Yorker of October 31, 2005, in which Scowcroft described his objections to the administration's Iraq policy; Goldberg's article also drew on an interview with George H.W. Bush.[^51] [[Robert Bryce]] wrote in Salon on November 17, 2005 that Bush had dismissed Scowcroft from the chairmanship about a year earlier because of his criticism of the decision to invade Iraq.[^52]

On October 27, 2005 the White House announced that Stephen Friedman would be appointed to the board for a term beginning on December 20, 2005, and that he would be designated its chairman upon appointment. The announcement also named Hunt, [[Donald Evans]], [[Arthur B. Culvahouse]], [[William O. DeWitt Jr.]], [[Lee Hamilton]] and others as members.[^53]

### The Scowcroft Group

The Scowcroft Group states that it was founded in 1994 by Scowcroft and is an international business advisory firm that has served US and multinational firms in a variety of sectors.[^4] Its services include geopolitical risk, sanctions and export controls, energy and resource security and scenario planning, and it lists partnerships with Fulcrum Macro Advisors, Vriens & Partners and McLarty Associates.[^54]

### Aspen Strategy Group roster, 2016

On August 8, 2016 [[Tom Pritzker]] wrote to [[Jeffrey Epstein]] that he had just finished a presentation on leadership to the "grand pooh baahs of Aspen Strategy Group." Epstein asked "who attends," and Pritzker replied: "You have to be a bit of a wonk to know some of the names, but Nye, Scowcroft and [[Nicholas Burns|Nick Burns]] run it (Scowcroft being replaced by [[Condoleezza Rice|Condi]])." The message lists members including [[David Petraeus]], [[Madeleine Albright]], [[Stephen Hadley]], [[Jake Sullivan]], [[William Burns|Bill Burns]] and [[John Sawers]] (MI6).[^55]
