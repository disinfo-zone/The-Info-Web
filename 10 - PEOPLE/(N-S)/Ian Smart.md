Ian Michael Henry Smart (February 28, 1935 to August 30, 2008) was a British [[Foreign Office]] diplomat who served as third secretary at the British embassy in [[Tel Aviv]] from October 1958 to January 1961.[^1][^2] He told [[Seymour Hersh]] in an interview in New York on July 23, 1989, for [[The Samson Option (GFE)|The Samson Option]], that in his last year there he was reporting a suspicion that the construction at [[Dimona]] looked like a nuclear reactor.[^3] He was assistant director of the [[International Institute for Strategic Studies]] from 1969 to 1973, then deputy director and director of studies of the [[Royal Institute of International Affairs]], and published on nuclear weapons and energy policy.[^4][^5]

### Foreign Office career

Smart served with [[British Armed Forces|H.M. Forces]] from 1956 to 1958.[^6] He was appointed a member of the Foreign Service on October 6, 1958, and went to Tel Aviv on October 28, 1958.[^1] He was third secretary at the Tel Aviv embassy under the ambassador, [[Francis Rundall]].[^2] He was transferred to the Foreign Office in January 1961 and promoted second secretary on February 28, 1962.[^7]

From 1962 to 1964 he was private secretary to the parliamentary under-secretary of state, first [[Peter Smithers]] and then [[Robert Mathew]], who succeeded Smithers in that post.[^8] From 1964 to 1965 he was private secretary to the minister of state for foreign affairs; the 1965 list names the minister as [[George Thomson]].[^8] He was promoted first secretary in 1965 and seconded to the International Institute for Strategic Studies that year.[^6] In 1966 he became first secretary at the British embassy in [[Washington, D.C.]], and he was still there in 1967.[^9] He resigned from the Diplomatic Service in 1969 "on approved employment."[^10] He married in 1964.[^11]

### Tel Aviv, 1958 to 1961

As the pace and intensity of construction grew, [[Beersheba]] "inevitably became an international city," Hersh wrote. "The French presence was palpable, as upward of 2,500 French men, women, and children made their life in the Negev." Hersh also wrote that the top scientists were provided with double pay and subsidized seven-room apartments in Beersheba, which those who stayed long enough were eventually given.[^12] Foreign diplomats and military attachés in Tel Aviv reported on these matters, Hersh wrote. "There were constantly recurring rumors of the bomb," but the cover stories, "usually revolving around seawater desalinization or agricultural research, somehow held."[^12]

Hersh described Smart as a young diplomat on his first foreign assignment, as third secretary of his country's "small embassy in Tel Aviv." Hersh wrote that Smart "would go on to become an international expert in nonproliferation," and that in those years he "was merely curious" and suspicious.[^13]

Smart recalled that there was "a lot of talk by the end of 1960 about Dimona," prompted "for one thing, by the sheer progress of the site. It was already very apparent on the skyline. And from the road you could see the cooling tower base of the [reactor] dome and the beginning of the rib structure." The second cause was the French presence in Beersheba, where, in his words, "There was an apartment block they used with a lot of Renault Dauphins about ... all carrying French registration."[^13]

Hersh wrote that the Israeli government, when officially asked about activity at Dimona, gave the British embassy a series of explanations. Smart recalled that one early claim was that the area was a desert grasslands research institute. He heard a second while driving with a group of Israeli Defense Force troops in the Negev. When he pointed out the cooling tower, an officer replied, "Ah yes. That's the new manganese-processing plant."[^13]

Smart told Hersh that throughout the last year of his posting he was "reporting the 'suspicion' that this looked like a nuclear reactor. But how do you get more than a suspicion without putting a U-2 over it?"[^13] Hersh added, in his own narration, that the Eisenhower administration, "as Smart could not know," was in its third year of U-2 overflights of Dimona by 1960.[^13]

### Dimona in the official record

The draft post-mortem on the intelligence community's failure to recognize the reactor, dated January 1961, records that on May 25, 1959 the United States naval attaché in Tel Aviv reported a British source as saying that the resignation of General [[Dan Tolkowsky]] on April 26, 1959 was due to his opposition to the atomic weapons effort of [[Shimon Peres]]. The attaché was unable to confirm the account.[^14] The post-mortem states that "collaboration with the French on this project had been initiated by 1957."[^15]

On June 5, 1959, an official of the [[Norwegian Foreign Ministry]], [[Olaf Solli]], told [[William M. Fullerton]] of the [[Atomic Energy Commission]] that an agreement between Norway and Israel, considered "Secret" by both governments, provided for safeguards and inspection rights in connection with the sale of heavy water from [[NORATOM]] to Israel.[^16] The post-mortem records that the State Department and the Atomic Energy Commission had the information in July 1959, and that it was not disseminated to the intelligence community until December 13, 1960.[^17]

The Fullerton memorandum records that Solli said "appropriate U.K. officials had been informed of the agreement with Israel and wished also to inform the U.S. Government." It states that NORATOM "purchased heavy water from the U.K. (which had originally been sold to the U.K. by Noratom)" and "shipped the heavy water from the U.S." so that no Norwegian export license was needed.[^16] Cohen and Burr describe the route differently: the heavy water "was shipped directly from the UK to Israel," with NORATOM as the seller.[^18] Cohen and Burr also write that London ultimately did not share the information with Washington, citing British press reports of 2005 and 2006 based on newly declassified British archives.[^19]

The post-mortem's chronology lists a British Joint Intelligence Committee statement on the atomic energy site near Beersheba, transmitted from London in a document dated October 27, 1960, and a "British photograph" of the site, its source given as "UK," acquired on August 30, 1960 and available to the [[Joint Atomic Energy Intelligence Committee]] on November 8, 1960.[^20] The paragraph on the cables states that the United Kingdom believed a reactor was under construction near Beersheba, and that ground photography of the possible site "was received through PIC channels from the United Kingdom on 8 November."[^20] The unnumbered text preceding paragraph 18 says that the U.S. Embassy in Tel Aviv forwarded on August 2, 1960 a report from a U.S. nuclear engineer that the Israelis were constructing a major reactor with French assistance, and that "Three months were required to obtain adequate confirmatory information via US collection agencies and UK liaison channels." That information resulted in a statement of the committee on December 7, 1960.[^21]

Paragraph 38 of the post-mortem states that information received from the United Kingdom in the first week of December 1960 through atomic energy intelligence liaison channels "revealed to the JAEIC members for the first time that Norway had furnished about 20 tons of heavy water to Israel in 1959 and 1960." The same paragraph records that the Department of State asked the U.S. Embassy in Oslo on December 12, 1960 for further information on the agreement, and that the embassy reported on December 22 and 30 on its status and on Norway's reluctance to exercise its inspection safeguards.[^22]

The National Archives at Kew catalogues Foreign Office files on Israel's atomic energy programme for these years: FO 371/142379, "Research into atomic energy in Israel" (1959); FO 371/149586, "Nuclear Energy Programmes: Israel" (1960); and FO 371/157284 to FO 371/157286, "Atomic energy programmes of individual countries: Israel" (1961). The Prime Minister's file PREM 11/3399 is titled "Development of nuclear reactor in Negev desert" (1960 to 1961).[^23][^24] None of these files is digitised.[^23] Smart's posting ran from October 1958 to January 1961, which covers the 1959 and 1960 entries.[^1][^7]

### Later career and publications

[[Geoffrey Kemp]] places Smart in the spring of 1969 as "then based in Washington" in the introduction to his Adelphi Paper 106.[^25] Smart was assistant director of the International Institute for Strategic Studies from 1969 to 1973.[^4] The Institute published his [[Adelphi Papers]] no. 63, Advanced Strategic Missiles, in 1969, a 31-page study.[^26] Adelphi Paper no. 78, Future Conditional, appeared in 1971 with 45 pages.[^27] The series also includes, under his name, an item titled "I. Introduction" in its August 1971 issue and a report titled "Committee discussions on the utility of military force in modern societies" in December 1973.[^28] With Geoffrey Kemp he wrote "SALT and European Nuclear Forces" for the 1973 volume SALT: Implications for Arms Control in the 1970s, and he wrote "Perspectives from Europe" for SALT: The Moscow Agreements and Beyond (1974).[^29] With A. N. Oppenheim he wrote the chapter "The British diplomat" in The Management of Britain's External Relations (1973), edited by Robert Boardman and A. J. R. Groom.[^30]

In August 1974 [[The New York Times]] printed an article by Smart on the effect of the Cyprus crisis on [[NATO]], which identified him as "deputy director and director of studies at the Royal Institute of International Affairs, London." The [[Central Intelligence Agency]] reproduced the article in its internal clipping publication News, Views and Issues, no. 12, dated August 23, 1974.[^31] The authors of a 1975 study by the Royal Institute of International Affairs thanked "Ian Smart, the Deputy Director of Chatham House," for comments on their drafts, and a 1976 volume on oil describes him as "deputy director and director of studies" of the Institute.[^5] The Royal Institute's 1977 book The Future of the British Nuclear Deterrent, number 1 in its British Foreign Policy to 1985 series, ran to 82 pages.[^32] His article of the same title appeared in the International Institute for Strategic Studies journal [[Survival]] in January 1978 (volume 20, issue 1, pages 21 to 24).[^33] A memorandum submitted by Smart appears in the sixth report of the [[House of Commons]] Expenditure Committee for the 1978-79 session on the future of the United Kingdom's nuclear weapons policy, and the report lists him with [[Lawrence Freedman]] as a witness on January 23, 1979.[^34]

[[HM Stationery Office]] published Multinational Arrangements for the Nuclear Fuel Cycle in 1980 as Energy Paper 43, 77 pages.[^35] [[Johns Hopkins University Press]] published World Nuclear Energy: Toward a Bargain of Confidence, edited by Smart, in 1982.[^36] Global Insecurity (1982), developed from a research project sponsored by the [[Atlantic Institute for International Affairs]] and published by [[Houghton Mifflin]], names [[Daniel Yergin]] and [[Martin Joseph Hillenbrand]] among its creators and includes the chapter "Energy and the power of nations" by Ian Smart. Its contributor note describes him as "an adviser on international energy affairs and chairman of Ian Smart Ltd" and as formerly deputy director of the Royal Institute of International Affairs.[^37] A second 1982 volume lists him as "now a consultant," and a third states that he "was educated at Oxford and Leeds universities."[^38] His essay "A Defective Dream," on the Atoms for Peace policy, appears in a 1985 volume whose editor describes him as "an international energy policy expert."[^39] Volume two of Energy Policies and the Greenhouse Effect (Royal Institute of International Affairs, 1991) credits Smart with a contribution in chapter 1.[^40]

Articles under the name Ian Smart appear in [[Energy Policy]] from 1984 to 1986, including "Pricing petroleum products: Strategies of eleven industrial nations" (1984) and "World uranium market" (1985). They appear in [[International Affairs]] in 1975 and 1989, including "Nuclear Proliferation Problems" (1975) and "OPEC: twenty-five years of prices and politics" (1989), and in [[Nature]] in 1980, with "Fuel for the 'back-end' debate." Records found do not establish whether these items are by the diplomat; their subjects match his other work.[^41]

The [[Canadian Institute of International Affairs]] published International Terrorism in 1987 and The World in Flux in 1990, the latter as volume 48, number 1 of its Behind the Headlines series.[^42]

### Death and obituary

[[The Times]] published an obituary headlined "Ian Smart: diplomat and authority on nuclear policy" in September 2008.[^43] A London Gazette notice of November 27, 2008 gives his date of death as August 30, 2008, his last address as [[Leominster]], and his occupation as "Diplomat (Retired)."[^44] Between March 8, 1999 and July 31, 2000 an Ian Michael Henry Smart, born February 1935 and living at Leominster, was a director of the Imperial War Museum Trading Company Limited.[^45]

### Same-name records

A second Foreign Office officer, [[Alexander Basil Peter Smart]], was a first secretary in 1967. Records found do not establish any connection between him and Ian Michael Henry Smart.[^46]

A different Ian Smart, born 1944, wrote on Caribbean literature, including a 1990 study of the poet [[Nicolás Guillén]]. Records found do not establish any connection to this page.[^47]

A 1975 volume, The Political Implications of North Sea Oil and Gas, was published by [[Universitetsforlaget]] under the names of [[Martin Sæter]] and Ian Smart, under the auspices of the Norwegian Institute of International Affairs and the Royal Institute of International Affairs. Its subject matches the energy work above, and records found do not establish whether the co-author is the diplomat.[^48]
