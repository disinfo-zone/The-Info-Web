Hans Simons (July 1, 1893 to March 28, 1972) was a German jurist and Weimar civil servant who directed the [[Deutsche Hochschule für Politik]] in [[Berlin]] and held a Prussian provincial presidency. He was dismissed in 1933, emigrated to New York, served as dean of the [[New School for Social Research]] Graduate Faculty, and became the school's third president.[^1]

### Father and family

Simons was born in [[Velbert]] in the [[Rhine Province]], the son of [[Walter Simons]], a jurist who became president of the [[Reichsgericht]] and foreign minister.[^2][^3] Walter Simons served as foreign minister from June 25, 1920 to May 10, 1921 in the cabinet of [[Konstantin Fehrenbach]] and represented the Reich at the conferences at [[Spa, Belgium|Spa]] and [[London]]. In 1919 he was undersecretary and commissioner-general of the German delegation at the [[Treaty of Versailles|Versailles]] negotiations under [[Ulrich von Brockdorff-Rantzau]]. In 1918 he had advised [[Max von Baden]] on international law.[^2] In 1922 he founded the "SeSiSo-Kreis," a salon that met at the [[Hotel Kaiserhof]] in Berlin with [[Hans von Seeckt]] and [[Wilhelm Solf]]. [[Harry Graf von Kessler]] was among its members.[^2] In the 1920s he chaired the board of trustees of the [[Moltke family]] foundation, which held the [[Kreisau]] estate.[^2]

Hans Simons was one of the three sons of Walter Simons and his wife Erna, née Rühle.[^2] He married twice and had three children.[^4] The son of his first marriage later assembled the family papers on which the 2018 biography of Simons drew, since no personal estate survives.[^5]

### Schooling, the dissertation and the League of Nations

Simons studied law and political science in Berlin, [[Munich]], [[Tübingen]] and [[Bonn]] between 1912 and 1914. He served in the [[World War I|First World War]] until 1917 as an Oberleutnant and was gravely wounded, in the head according to the New School's history.[^5] He then worked as an administrator in the eastern territories occupied by the German Empire.[^1][^4] He resumed his studies at the University of [[Königsberg]] in 1920 and completed a dissertation in 1921 titled "Die Präambel zur Pariser Völkerbundssatzung und das Völkerrecht" (the preamble to the Paris Covenant of the [[League of Nations]] and international law).[^1][^4]

### Hochschule für Politik and the Reich Interior Ministry

Simons was managing director of the Deutsche Hochschule für Politik from 1920 to 1922.[^4] The Hochschule drew on American money, notably from the [[Rockefeller Foundation]] and the [[Carnegie Corporation]]. Its American associations built bridges between Hochschule faculty, foundation administrators and American academics.[^6] From 1922 to 1924 Simons was a Regierungsrat and then an Oberregierungsrat in the [[Reich Ministry of the Interior]] in Berlin.[^4][^7] From 1925 to 1930 he was director of the Hochschule and professor of law. His essay "Lehre und Lehrer" in the Hochschule's 1931 volume [[Politik als Wissenschaft]] covers the institution's structure, which combined permanently associated politicians with lecturers hired case by case.[^8] In 1935 and 1936 he published two articles in [[Social Research]] on European international law and collective security.[^9] From 1928 to 1930 he was managing director of the [[Abraham Lincoln-Stiftung]] in Berlin.[^4]

### Prussian provincial posts and the 1933 dismissal

Simons was acting Regierungspräsident in [[Stettin]] in 1930 and 1931, and Regierungspräsident of the [[Regierungsbezirk Liegnitz]] in [[Silesia]] from 1931 to 1932.[^2][^4] He was a member of the [[Social Democratic Party of Germany]].[^2] He ran unsuccessfully for a seat in the parliament as a Social Democrat.[^1] He was dismissed in 1933 and emigrated with his second wife and her two daughters.[^5] The Nazi Party blacklisted him in 1933.[^1] From 1933 to 1934 he worked as a self-employed sales agent (Handelsvertreter).[^4]

### Exile and the Graduate Faculty

Simons reached [[New York City|New York]] in 1935 on a fellowship of the [[University in Exile]] at the New School.[^1][^2] Alvin Johnson's report to the trustees of February 1935 lists him among the members added to the Graduate Faculty in 1934.[^10] He taught political science and international relations in the Graduate Faculty and gave public lectures on democracy and totalitarianism at the school and at other universities.[^1] The [[Emergency Committee in Aid of Displaced Foreign Scholars]] filed him among its "Non-Grantees" in Series I.B of its records at the [[New York Public Library]].[^11] He became an American citizen in 1940.[^1][^2] In 1942 he studied Germany's position in European postwar reconstruction with [[Adolph Lowe]], [[Arnold Brecht]] and [[Hans Staudinger]], in the Research Division of the Graduate Faculty and the [[Institute of World Affairs]].[^12] He was dean of the Graduate Faculty from 1943 to June 1950.[^1][^4]

### Wartime lists

In May 1943 [[Goodwin Watson]] sent [[Robert D. Leigh]] of the [[Foreign Broadcast Intelligence Service]] a list of recruits for the Analysis Division. Nearly all of the names were German exiles. Simons appears on the list with [[Sigmund Neumann]] and [[Arnold Wolfers]].[^13]

### President of the New School

In June 1950 Simons was selected as the third president of the New School, succeeding [[Bryn Hovde]], who had led the school for five years.[^1] He inherited a school with serious financial problems. Enrollment rose from about 5,000 to about 8,000 students during his tenure, and construction began on the buildings that became the [[Eugene Lang College]] complex.[^1] He defended the right of faculty to take part in political activity, however unpopular that was with other members of the school.[^1]

A file in the New School central administration records holds Simons's "Future of the New School," dated September 18, 1958. Its scope note relates it to a Publicity Office record in which Simons responds to the "McGrath Report."[^14] The New School Associates records hold a presidential talk on "Hans Simon's report on India," dated January 28, 1960.[^15]

His resignation is dated to 1960. Correspondence in his Adult Division file is "mainly after 1960, when Hans Simons resigned as president of The New School."[^16] He retired from the presidency in 1960.[^1] The scope note of the [[John R. Everett]] records lists his presidency as 1950 to 1961.[^17] Press releases in the school's records announce the appointment of [[Henry David]] as president between January 18 and March 19, 1961, and record his inauguration on October 23, 1961.[^18][^19] David resigned on March 22, 1963.[^20]

### Project MKUltra memoranda, 1961

Two [[Project MKUltra]] subproject files released by the [[Central Intelligence Agency|CIA]] carry 1961 memoranda from the period of Simons's presidency. A draft memorandum for the record dated May 29, 1961 on Subproject 134 states that the subproject supports the research studies of a person whose name is redacted, and the CIA's September 1977 release to the New School reinserted "New School for Social Research" by hand in red at that point. The draft describes the relevance of the study as "its potential contribution to clandestine indirect assessment, i. e., without the subject's awareness," names "relationships between body type and temperament (personality characteristics)" as the earlier work on which the investigator would build, sets the cost at $22,000 for one year, and states that the investigator "is neither cleared nor witting of true Sponsor."[^21] A June 9, 1961 memorandum headed "Recommendation for Funding" in the same file recommends funding "despite the superficiality of the proposal" and says "This work fits into our indirect assessment requirement." The project summary gives actual work as expected to commence in September 1961, with the contractor field blank.[^22]

A memorandum for the record dated August 24, 1961 on Subproject 137 supports the work of a "Dr." whose name is redacted, "of the" a redacted institution, and the CIA's 1977 release again inserted "New School for Social Research, New York City" in red. The memorandum describes completing "a psycholexicon on handwriting," states that she "has an international reputation for her work in graphology and speech," that she is "advanced in years" and her health "failing," and that her manuscript is "eighty percent complete."[^23] The grant was $2,000. A February 1963 accounting memorandum in the file states that the recipient "died in 1962 and did not send in an accounting," and an obligation-file certification records that a manuscript had been published.[^24]

A 1977 CIA folder in the John R. Everett records of the New School describes "a report of two projects conducted at the New School in 1962 that received funding through MKUltra," and says that the first "attempted to discern relationships between body type and personality characteristics."[^25] The investigators' names remain redacted in both subproject files. The graphology teacher [[Klara G. Roman]] taught at the New School from 1948 until shortly before her death in 1962.[^26]

### India and the Ford Foundation

In 1960 Simons retired from the presidency to work as a consultant and director for the [[Ford Foundation]].[^1] He was an adviser and interlocutor for the Ford Foundation and other organizations.[^5]

### Occupation Germany and the Basic Law

Simons served as an American liaison officer in [[Frankfurt]] am Main from 1947 to 1949.[^4] He took part in the making of the Basic Law as an American liaison officer to the [[Parlamentarischer Rat]] in 1947 and 1949.[^2] He was a civilian employee of the [[Office of Military Government, United States]] (OMGUS).[^3] He was called into the deliberations on the Basic Law.[^5] He received the [[Großes Verdienstkreuz]] of the Federal Republic in 1959.[^4] A high order from [[Theodor Heuss]] was conferred on him together with Arnold Brecht and Hans Staudinger.[^5]

### Death

After a long illness, Simons died on March 28, 1972 at his home in [[Yonkers]], New York.[^1][^3][^4][^27]
