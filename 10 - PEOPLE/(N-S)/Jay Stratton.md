John F. Stratton Jr., known as Jay Stratton, is a former [[U.S. Navy|US Navy]] intelligence senior executive (a civilian member of the Senior Executive Service at the [[Office of Naval Intelligence]], previously at the [[Defense Intelligence Agency]]) and a veteran of the [[United States Army]] and [[United States Air Force Reserve]], who was the first director of the [[UAP Task Force]], a Navy-led unit that the [[Pentagon]] set up in 2020 to collect and analyze reports of [[Unidentified Anomalous Phenomena|unidentified aerial phenomena]].[^1] His speaker biography states that he retired in early 2022 after more than 32 years of military and federal service.[^2] He grew up in East Texas and, according to a 2026 interview, joined the Army while still in high school.[^3]

This page is not about [[Julius A. Stratton]], the MIT physicist, provost and president (1901 to 1994) who was known to colleagues as "Jay."[^4] The 2022 company release identifies the task force's first director as "John F. Stratton Jr."[^1]

### Career and titles

A Navy senior executive biography (PDF created June 21, 2021) states that Stratton "entered Senior Executive Service in September 2018," had "over thirty-two years of experience" with the United States Army, the United States Air Force Reserve and the federal civil service, and was then "Senior Analyst, Nimitz Operational Intelligence Center" of the Office of Naval Intelligence, "responsible for delivering comprehensive intelligence to Navy leadership." It lists earlier posts as Director of Intelligence (J2) at the Joint Warfare Analysis Center; Deputy Director Executive Support in the office of the Deputy Chief of Naval Operations for Information Warfare (N2N6); Director Air Warfare/SPEAR at the Nimitz Operational Intelligence Center; Chief of Air and Space Warfare, Defense Warning Office, Defense Intelligence Agency; Aerospace Engineer at ONI; Aerospace Engineer (Flight Test) at [[Naval Air Systems Command]]; and Senior Weapons Systems Engineer at [[ManTech International]]. Reserve assignments include Senior Intelligence Officer of the 459th Air Refueling Wing, Chief Space Technology at the [[Defense Technology Security Administration]], Detachment Commander with the II Marine Expeditionary Force at Camp Leatherneck, Afghanistan, and Military Advisor to the President's Special Advisor for Middle East Regional Security at the US Embassy in Tel Aviv. He holds a Bachelor of Science from [[Embry-Riddle Aeronautical University]] and a master's degree from [[Air University]], and the biography lists the DIA Director's Award, the Navy Meritorious Civilian Service Award, the [[Central Intelligence Agency]] Meritorious Unit Commendation and the ODNI Meritorious Unit Commendation among his awards.[^5]

The 2022 release states that Stratton was "recently retired" from a Defense Intelligence Senior Executive post with the Office of Naval Intelligence and had "over 32 years in the national intelligence community."[^1] The publisher's synopsis of his memoir says he "served the U.S. on the battlefield and as a senior member of the National Intelligence Community" and "specialized in emerging and disruptive aerospace technologies."[^6] His speaker biography calls him "a highly decorated intelligence officer." It lists postings at the [[Office of the Secretary of Defense]], the [[Joint Staff]], [[United States Strategic Command]], Naval Air Systems Command and two U.S. embassies, and service in the United States Army and the United States Air Force Reserve. It states that he was "a Senior Advisor for the White House's [[Office of Science and Technology Policy]]," that he worked on UAP for "over 16 years," and that at retirement he held "the civilian equivalent rank of a 2 star General or Admiral."[^2]

[[George Knapp]] of [[KLAS-TV]] wrote in February 2023 that "From 2008 until 2021, nearly everything related to UFO activity came across Jay Stratton's desk," and that Stratton "worked at the highest levels of Naval Intelligence, which loaned him to the Defense Intelligence Agency."[^7] [[The Black Vault]], writing about the 2020 emails discussed below, identifies him as "an Office of Naval Intelligence Senior Analyst from the Nimitz Operational Intelligence Center and former Director of the UAP Task Force."[^8]

Sources differ on when he left the task force. Knapp reported on May 23, 2022 (posted 9:12 PM Pacific time) that "In January 2021 ... Stratton was surprisingly transferred out of the job," and that Stratton "has never spoken publicly about his work and is not commenting now."[^9] The speaker biography gives early 2022 as the date of his retirement.[^2]

### Recruitment and AAWSAP

[[Luis Elizondo]]'s account places the first contact in early 2009. He describes Stratton as "a serious male in his midthirties," whom Elizondo regarded as a fellow special operator. He writes that Stratton and a colleague, who appears under the pseudonym [[Rosemary Caine]], arrived at his Arlington office wearing blue credentials identifying them as government intelligence officials, and asked for his counterintelligence and security experience for "a highly classified program led out of our office at DIA." Stratton said he had helped create the program under the name [[AAWSAP]], which Elizondo says was focused on "unconventional technologies" and needed a senior intelligence officer to handle counterintelligence and security.[^10]

[[Dave Fravor]]'s written statement to the House Oversight subcommittee on July 26, 2023 says: "It was not until 2009, until Jay Stratton had contacted me to investigate. Unbeknownst to all, he was part of the ATIP program in the Pentagon led by Lue Elizondo."[^11]

The [[All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office]] historical record report of March 2024 describes the program's funding and origin. It states that "At the direction of Senate Majority Leader [[Harry Reid]] (D-NV), the Defense Appropriations Acts of Fiscal Years 2008 and 2010 appropriated $22 million for the DIA," and that "DIA established AAWSAP in 2009, which was also known AATIP."[^12] The report says that the contract went to a private sector organization, that "a DIA employee set up and managed the contract," and that "AAWSAP/AATIP was terminated in 2012 upon the completion of its deliverables due to DIA and DoD concerns about the project."[^12] It states that "Unlike AAWSAP, AATIP was never an official DoD program," and that after the program ended the AATIP name was used by "some individuals associated with an informal, unofficial UAP community of interest within DoD."[^12] The report also records a letter of June 24, 2009, in which "Senator Reid sent a letter to then Deputy Secretary of Defense William Lynn III requesting that AAWSAP/AATIP be made a DoD Special Access Program." Lynn declined "based on the recommendation of then-Under Secretary of Defense for Intelligence, James R. Clapper, Jr., that such a designation was not justified."[^12]

Elizondo also writes that Stratton secured an additional $10 million for the program from Reid, and that the money was later taken by another Defense Department program.[^13] He writes that when the program lost its funding and faced internal opposition, Stratton worked with him to move its remnants under Elizondo's portfolio of national programs.[^13]

### The Nimitz encounter and Interloper

Elizondo writes that Stratton "investigated the incident before I joined the team" and "had written a detailed AAWSAP/AATIP report" on the November 2004 encounter involving the [[USS Nimitz]] that became known as the [[Tic Tac]]. He writes that pilots later told Stratton they had been debriefed by intelligence officers and saw no follow-up.[^14]

Fravor's 2023 statement addresses the same incident: "What is shocking to us is that the incident was never investigated. None of my crew were ever questioned. The tapes were never taken and after a couple days it turned into a great story with friends."[^11]

Elizondo writes that Stratton drafted the operation plan "Interloper," an ambitious plan to use a carrier strike group as bait to lure UAP for observation and data collection, which he calls "a classic 'honey pot,'" with dates, call signs, ship names and radar data, and that the Joint Staff declined to give it a compartment designation in 2016. He also credits Stratton with proposing the term "unidentified aerial phenomena" in place of UFO.[^15] Knapp's February 2023 report makes the same attribution, saying that Stratton "decided to abandon the acronym UFO in favor of UAP."[^7]

### Leadership of the UAP Task Force

On August 14, 2020 the [[Department of Defense]] announced that [[David L. Norquist]], the Deputy Secretary of Defense, had approved the task force on August 4. The release states that "The Department of the Navy, under the cognizance of the Office of the Under Secretary of Defense for Intelligence and Security, will lead the UAPTF."[^16] The charter, dated September 1, 2020, states that "Leadership of the Task Force will be designated by the Director of the [[Naval Intelligence Activity]]."[^17]

A briefing for the [[House Permanent Select Committee on Intelligence]] dated May 17, 2021 carries the title slide "Director; UAP Task Force Naval Intelligence Activity."[^18] The [[Office of the Director of National Intelligence]] assessment of June 25, 2021 states that "The Director, UAPTF, is the accountable official for ensuring the timely collection and consolidation of data on UAP."[^19]

Elizondo writes that after Navy intelligence leadership tasked Stratton with building a whole-of-government interagency task force, which he built from the ground up, with members from the [[Federal Bureau of Investigation]], the [[National Reconnaissance Office]], [[NASA]] and the [[Federal Aviation Administration]], the Secretary of Defense announced the task force "after a couple of years" and named Stratton its first director. He writes that Stratton continued to push the UAP issue forward, briefing members of Congress and the [[White House]] [[National Security Council]] and laying the groundwork for future legislative action, and that the [[Legacy Program]] "started quietly pushing back on Jay."[^20]

Knapp's June 2022 report on [[Travis Taylor]] quotes Taylor saying that "Jay Stratton, the director of the UAP Task Force asked me if I would be interested in being the chief scientist." The report adds that Stratton "worked with AAWSAP, then its successor program AATIP. Later, he took charge of a third effort, the UAP Task Force, long before Congress formally created that team."[^21]

In his written statement of July 26, 2023, [[David Grusch]] said that "In 2019, the UAP Task Force Director asked me to identify all special access programs and controlled access programs," and that he was "a member of the UAP Task Force from 2019 to 2021."[^22]

The AARO report describes the task force's record in one paragraph. It says the task force "helped standardize, destigmatize, and increase the volume of UAP reporting," and that its "methods and processes directly led to the identification of the People's Republic of China's (PRC) high altitude balloons." The report attributes the balloon claim to "AARO discussions with UAPTF leadership."[^23]

At the May 17, 2022 hearing of the House Permanent Select Committee on Intelligence, [[Ronald Moultrie]] was asked about AATIP and said: "I am familiar with Blue Book. I am familiar with AATIP. I haven't seen other documented studies that have been done by DOD in that regard."[^24]

### The triangular photograph

In December 2020 [[The Debrief]] reported a photograph of a triangular object rising from the ocean, attributed to an F/A-18 pilot. Emails of December 3 and 4, 2020, released under FOIA case 21-F-0299, include a message from the Deputy Chief of Naval Operations office to [[Susan Gough]] that reads "Jay [Stratton] tells me the photo is part of the active investigation, so it would probably be withheld from release for that reason."[^8] The Black Vault wrote that the message "appeared to confirm the existence of the triangle UAP photograph."[^8]

In a statement to The Black Vault, Stratton said that at the time "I was handling multiple cases," and that "We had photographs from F/A-18 pilots, instances of UAP that appeared triangular/pyramidal, and other unrelated cases." He added: "No such photo was ever provided to us by an F/A-18 pilot showing a triangle emerging from the water." Asked why he had not corrected the record, he said: "Keep in mind that's an email on an unclassified system. I don't recall if I corrected it on a separate system at the time, but generally, there was no formal process for publicly addressing inaccuracies related to potentially classified reporting."[^8] The Black Vault reported that no such photograph "was ever received by the UAPTF."[^8] The article's author, [[Tim McMillan]], told the same outlet: "I remain confident in the accuracy of the information shared."[^8]

### Radiance Technologies

[[Radiance Technologies]] announced on May 12, 2022 that Stratton had joined as "SME for Intel Strategy," as a member of the chief executive's strategy team, where he would lead and develop efforts in scientific and technical intelligence. The release quotes the company president, [[Tim Tinsley]]: "Jay's extensive experience in the Intelligence Community will be valuable in identifying and cultivating new strategic teaming opportunities within both industry and government." It quotes Stratton: "I am very excited to join Radiance Technologies."[^1]

In an October 13, 2022 report, Knapp wrote that Stratton urged Radiance to hire Taylor, and quoted Taylor: "Jay and I are working on growing the capabilities, doing reverse engineering of foreign materials."[^25]

In a December 2022 article in Reason, [[Mick West]] wrote that "In 2022, government contractor Radiance Technologies hired both the task force's director, John F. Stratton Jr., and its informal chief scientist, Travis S. Taylor, presumably for something at least speculatively government- and UAP-related."[^26]

On December 3, 2023, [[Liberation Times]] published an article in which the attorney [[Daniel Sheehan]] alleged that the Central Intelligence Agency supplies Radiance with non-human materials. The article reported that "multiple sources" had told it the allegation was untrue, and that Stratton and Taylor "are long-time advocates for more transparency on this topic and are in no way involved in any coverup."[^27]

### Skinwalker Ranch

George Knapp wrote in February 2023 that Stratton and his colleague [[James Lacatski]], a DIA rocket scientist, "set out to create an office" to analyze reports of unknown craft, that Lacatski, "reading about a UFO hotspot named 'Skinwalker Ranch,'" visited the property and pitched a formal investigation of it, and that "Reid agreed to fund it, and Bigelow landed the contract."[^7] The All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office report records that the contract went to a private sector organization.[^12]

Elizondo credits Stratton with coining the term "hitchhiker effect" for the tendency of phenomena at [[Skinwalker Ranch]] to follow investigators home and disturb them and their families long after they leave the property.[^28]

In a 2026 KLAS report on his memoir, Knapp wrote that Stratton "spent a lot of time at Skinwalker Ranch in Utah and said he took home devious 'hitchhikers.'" The report continues: "According to Stratton, these hitchhikers never left him and continue to bother him in his own home almost two decades later."[^29]

### Memoir and public statements

The publisher's synopsis states that Stratton, "presented with a video of a mysterious advanced aerospace vehicle over a sensitive nuclear weapon site," cofounded AAWSAP, that he "learned that there is a deeply hidden UAP crash retrieval and reverse engineering program that has been covering up the existence of non-human intelligent life for eighty years," and that the book describes "close encounters that profoundly altered his understanding of humanity's place in the universe and took a disturbing toll on him and his family." The audiobook is read by Stratton and [[Hal Puthoff]].[^6]

In a PEOPLE interview published October 13, 2026, Stratton said that on an autumn day in 2005 a colleague brought him a classified-system video, recorded by security forces near a nuclear missile silo, of a black triangular craft with lights at each corner that accelerated out of view, and that this "was the moment that changed my world." The article says a representative of Stratton stated that the memoir was vetted by 21 agencies and departments before release. Stratton said that from 2010 his wife and sons were visited at their home by glowing orbs and "shadowy figures," and that "Once I retired, it pretty much stopped." PEOPLE reported that the book describes a nuclear submarine used to lure a craft for monitoring, and that military contractors hold at least a dozen recovered craft, and it quotes the book on a source's account: "there was indeed a crash in Roswell, New Mexico in 1947," with "everything" recovered, "the crash and non-human bodies." The article notes that the Pentagon publicly denied a multi-decade UAP cover-up in 2024. Stratton appears in the 2025 documentary *The Age of Disclosure*, produced by [[Dan Farah]].[^30]

The [[Hollywood Reporter]] reported on June 3, 2026 that Stratton's memoir, *Out of the Shadows: Revealing the Truth About Non-Human Intelligent Life*, will be published by the HarperCollins imprint [[William Morrow]] on October 13, and that his book deal was first announced in 2024. The publisher's synopsis, as quoted in the report, says that "While serving at the Defense Intelligence Agency, Stratton learned of UAP incursions over nuclear weapon sites, leading him to co-found the Advanced Aerospace Weapon System Applications Program (AAWSAP)." It also says that Stratton "discovered an 80-year coverup of the existence of non-human intelligent life."[^31]

[[Deadline]] reported the same month that "For over 16 years, Stratton investigated UAP and non-human intelligent life on behalf of the federal government," and that the film and television rights are held by Dan Farah. A related Deadline report says that [[Terence Winter]] is adapting the memoir for television.[^32]

A [[NewsNation]] report on the memoir described Stratton as "a former director of the Unidentified Aerial Phenomena Task Force, a predecessor to the current All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office," and stated that "There's no indication what Stratton plans to reveal."[^33]

A KLAS report by George Knapp and John Forest, posted before the release, says that Knapp interviewed Stratton "at a UAP conference in Alabama," and gives the release date as mid-October.[^29]

Stratton was the guest on the February 7, 2023 episode of [[Weaponized]], hosted by [[Jeremy Corbell]] and George Knapp, titled "The Pentagon's Top UFO Hunter." The episode runs 1 hour 37 minutes.[^34]

[[Garry Nolan]] of [[Stanford University]] told Stanford magazine in 2023 that Stratton gave pilots who feared exposure to UAP propulsion systems Nolan's name, and the article quotes Stratton: "He has just been one of the guys in my Rolodex."[^35]

### Press and critics

The Black Vault, citing a New York Post report, quoted an anonymous former Defense Department official who described the task force as "a complete and utter waste of money" and its former director, Jay Stratton, as "a complete nutjob."[^36]
