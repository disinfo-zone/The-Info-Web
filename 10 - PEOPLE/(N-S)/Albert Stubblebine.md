Major General Albert Newton Stubblebine III (February 6, 1930 - February 6, 2017) was a [[U.S. Army]] general who served as commanding general of the [[United States Army Intelligence and Security Command|INSCOM]] from 1981 to 1984, during which he was the most senior American military officer publicly associated with belief in psychic phenomena and their military applications. His command authority provided the institutional protection that allowed the [[Stargate Project|Grill Flame]] and Center Lane remote viewing programs to survive bureaucratic resistance and continue operating through the early 1980s. He died on his 87th birthday.

### Military Career

Stubblebine graduated from the [[United States]] Military Academy at West Point in 1952 and pursued a career in Army intelligence and signals work. He advanced through assignments in Army intelligence analysis, signals intelligence, and special operations-adjacent programs, eventually reaching the senior Army intelligence command positions.

He was appointed commanding general of INSCOM in 1981. INSCOM was the Army's primary intelligence collection and counterintelligence organization, responsible for signals intelligence, human intelligence collection, and operational security programs across the Army. The STARGATE remote viewing program, which had been operating at [[Fort Meade]] since 1977 under code names Gondola Wish and Grill Flame, fell within INSCOM's organizational umbrella.

### Psychic Research Advocacy

Stubblebine was a genuine believer in the potential of unconventional human capabilities for military applications. He believed that with sufficient training and mental discipline, soldiers could develop abilities that conventional science had not fully measured or explained. This conviction encompassed remote viewing - which the Grill Flame program was already investigating - as well as more extreme propositions including the possibility of passing the human body through solid matter by achieving a sufficiently altered mental state.

Reports from officers who served under him described Stubblebine attempting to walk through the wall of his Pentagon office, based on his conviction that this was theoretically achievable through the right mental training. These reports, which circulated within the Army intelligence community and later became part of the broader cultural record of the STARGATE era documented in Jon Ronson's *The Men Who Stare at Goats* (2004), illustrated both the sincerity of his beliefs and the distance between those beliefs and mainstream military culture.

He pushed for expanded use of psychics throughout Army intelligence and organized training programs intended to develop soldiers' latent psychic capabilities. These included consciousness training exercises at [[Fort Bragg]] for special operations personnel. The cultural environment these programs created - sometimes called the "[[First Earth Battalion]]" culture after Lieutenant Colonel [[Jim Channon]]'s influential 1979 concept paper proposing a psychic warrior force - was an institutional artifact of Stubblebine's INSCOM command.

### Support for STARGATE

Stubblebine's most consequential contribution to the government remote viewing program was his command-level support. When skeptics within Army intelligence and Pentagon bureaucracies questioned the program's value, Stubblebine's rank and genuine enthusiasm provided protection. He participated in remote viewing demonstrations, received briefings on operational results, and used his position to continue program funding and staffing through his INSCOM tenure.

His advocacy also had limits: the program's credibility within mainstream Army intelligence was not enhanced by its association with a commander known for attempting to walk through walls. Officers who might otherwise have engaged with the remote viewing program's results distanced themselves from it to avoid association with what they considered an embarrassingly unconventional command climate.

The sources differ on how and when his INSCOM command ended. [[Jim Schnabel]] wrote in [[The Independent]] of August 27, 1995 that Stubblebine "retired from the Army in 1984," and that "the fatal blow to his career came in 1983," when an INSCOM staff officer visited the [[Monroe Institute]]; Schnabel attributed that account to unnamed former colleagues and to [[Ed Dames]].[^1] A [[Defense Intelligence Agency]] addendum to a memorandum for the record dated December 10, 1985 lists "Stubblebine, Albert MG (Ret.)" as commanding general of INSCOM from May 1981 to June 1984.[^2] The [[Gasconade County Republican]] of [[Owensville, Missouri]] reported on May 16, 1984, in a local item on a retirement ceremony whose honoree appears to be a person named Huff, that "Major Gen. Albert N. Stubblebine III, Commanding General, U.S. Army Intelligence and Security Command, Arlington Hall Station, Va." presided and spoke.[^3] The project manager's records place the decision of his successor, [[Harry Soyster]], to discontinue Center Lane on July 13, 1984 (see below).[^4] The departure has also been described as a relief from command in 1984 followed by retirement from the Army; no source for that wording is identified. After his departure, the program continued but lost some of its most senior Army champion. The DIA subsequently took over primary program management.

### The Record

Stubblebine received his first Grill Flame briefing as incoming commander of INSCOM on June 30, 1981, from the outgoing project manager, Lieutenant Colonel [[Murray Watt]], and his successor, Lieutenant Colonel [[Robert Jachim]]. After the Senate intelligence committee's budget subcommittee cut the Army's psychoenergetic operations from the National Foreign Intelligence Program at the end of fiscal 1982, he "decided to continue INSCOM's project and provide funding with Security and Investigative Activities (S&IA) monies" under the provisional special access program [[Center Lane]]. The project manager's records of his meetings show him on November 30, 1983, confirming "his decision to hold a 'bonding' ceremony" for the RAPT training, to be led by Lieutenant Colonel [[John B. Alexander|John Alexander]], and on January 6, 1984, saying of Alexander's unauthorized debriefing of [[Russell Targ]] that "he had not instructed LTC Alexander to debrief Targ because Alexander was not a trained intelligence officer."[^5]

At the briefing of June 30, 1981, attended by Brigadier General Flynn, Colonel Weinstein, Colonel White, and the two project managers, "MG Stubblebine instructed us to ensure that there were no legal problems concerning our joint contractual effort with DIA, especially to be watchful of mixing OM&A and R&D monies," and, reviewing the customer evaluations, "expressed the thought that the evaluations were a clear indication that we were on to something and that work in this area had to continue."[^6]

On September 27, 1982, four days before the cut, he heard the full Grill Flame briefing at building 2561, "well over an hour" of it on the project book and two unevaluated cases; "he was unaware of the problems we had had in getting information from Europe, concerning [[James Dozier|Dozier]]." Coming from a visit to Lieutenant General [[Lincoln D. Faurer|Faurer]] of the [[National Security Agency|NSA]], he dismissed one option for housing the unit as soon as it was clear it had no spaces or money of its own, and "before I could finish, MG Stubblebine selected SSD, 902D as the organization to which the project would go. As I tried to continue with other options, MG Stubblebine then indicated I must not have heard him for he had already made his decision." His guidance included "Get out of the briefing mode!" The assistant chief of staff for intelligence wrote to him in April 1983 that "I applaud your efforts to establish a Special Access Program," and that INSCOM should present itself as engaged in "a similar, cooperative effort of its own initiative" rather than the former joint program. In 1983 he briefed the Army Science Board, the under secretary [[James Ambrose]], and the Secretary of the Army, [[John Marsh]], himself. On August 26, 1983, after Buzby, Atwater, and McMoneagle briefed him on the project and the Monroe Institute, "the CG started to relate some of his experiences and personal thoughts in the field of parapsychology," and "directed me to expand the program with quality people, develop a screening program to identify qualified people for the project; and employ advance techniques for intelligence collection." His chief of staff asked whether the project was receiving information from Lieutenant Colonel John B. Alexander and was told that Alexander's involvement "was entirely overt and that we neither had nor wanted any contact with him." On January 6, 1984, he asked to be kept informed of the "[[Los Alamos]] Project," "as it was his initiative," and agreed not to fund a new Monroe Institute proposal. On March 13, 1984, the director of the Army staff cancelled RAPT, the Hemi-Sync screening course, and on July 13 his successor, Harry Soyster, decided to discontinue Center Lane.[^4]

### Post-Military Career

Jim Schnabel wrote in The Independent of August 27, 1995 that Stubblebine "retired from the Army in 1984 to become an executive at BDM Corporation, a Washington-area defence and intelligence contractor," and that he "left BDM a few years ago."[^1] A Defense Intelligence Agency addendum to a memorandum for the record dated December 10, 1985 lists "Stubblebine, Albert MG (Ret.)" as commanding general of [[United States Army Intelligence and Security Command|INSCOM]] from May 1981 to June 1984 and "presently employed by BDM Corp. in the Wash., D.C. area."[^2] His biography on the website of [[Canadian Subsidiary Technology Inc.]], on whose board he sat, states that "upon retirement, he served as the Vice President for Intelligence at BDM and ran his own consulting firm."[^7] [[John B. Alexander]] wrote in 2011 that Stubblebine went to work for BDM "as the Vice President for Intelligence Operations" and that Alexander's 1985 meetings, later called the [[Advanced Theoretical Physics Working Group]], were to be held in the company's secure facilities. A [[Department of Energy]] visit request of May 8, 1985 names the BDM McLean facility for an "Advance Theoretical Physics Conference" of May 20 to 25, 1985, and [[Oke Shannon|James Shannon]]'s attendee list gives "Bert Stubblebine" under BDM. [[George P. Hansen]] wrote in *The Trickster and the Paranormal* that [[Karl Pflock]] worked for BDM International between 1989 and 1992 and that Stubblebine, chairman of [[Psi Tech]], "also worked for the company" during that time.[^8] The company's chairman from 1990 was [[Frank Carlucci]], and its owner from 1990 to 1997 was the [[Carlyle Group]]; see [[BDM Corporation]].

After retirement, Stubblebine became involved in alternative health movements and supplement businesses. He married psychiatrist Rima Laibow, and the two became prominent voices in alternative medicine and health freedom advocacy, including campaigns against mandatory vaccination and opposition to pharmaceutical industry regulation. This later career was distant in character from his military intelligence background but reflected a consistent pattern of skepticism toward mainstream institutional authority.

He was prominently featured in Jon Ronson's *The Men Who Stare at Goats* (2004) and the 2009 film adaptation, which used him and the Grill Flame culture as the central subjects of its examination of psychic warfare research within the U.S. military.

Stubblebine died February 6, 2017 - his 87th birthday - at his home in [[New Mexico]].
