William French Smith was the U.S. Attorney General under [[Ronald Reagan]] who signed the secret 1982 Memorandum of Understanding with [[Central Intelligence Agency]] director [[William J. Casey]] that exempted CIA assets from drug crimes reporting. The agreement, hammered out between the CIA and Justice Department, fundamentally altered the relationship between intelligence operations and law enforcement.[^1]

### The Secret 1982 Agreement

In early 1982, Smith and Casey signed a formal Memorandum of Understanding under which narcotics violations by non-employees were not on the list of crimes the CIA was required to report to the Justice Department.[^2] Crimes committed by people "acting for" an intelligence agency no longer needed to be reported either. Gary Webb, after quoting Inspector General [[Fred Hitz]]'s March 1998 testimony that the arrangement had been "hammered out" in 1982, wrote that for thirteen years "the CIA and Justice had a gentleman's agreement to look the other way."[^3] In an interview for the PBS series Frontline, Hitz said: "We didn't find any evidence of an understanding that official Washington was going to look the other way with respect to allegations of drug trafficking."[^4] The agreement was signed just as the CIA was launching both the [[Contras|Contra]] project and operations in Afghanistan, timing that [[Robert Parry]] argued proved premeditation: "That could only have been done for one purpose. They were anticipating what eventually happened. They knew drugs were going to be sold." Civil rights lawyers later filed suit charging the agreement was illegal and led to policies protecting rather than stopping drug trafficking.[^3]

### Third-Country Funding for the Contras, June 1984

The day after the [[National Security Planning Group]] meeting of June 25, 1984, Casey and CIA General Counsel [[Stanley Sporkin]] met Attorney General Smith. [[Lawrence Walsh]] wrote that Smith determined that third-country funding of the Contras was legally permissible as long as no U.S. funds were used for the purpose and the third country did not expect the United States to repay the aid.[^5] The Iran-Contra committees wrote that Smith orally expressed his opinion to Casey that solicitation was lawful as long as there was no quid pro quo.[^6] At the NSPG meeting [[George P. Shultz]] had said: "I think we need to get an opinion from the Attorney General on whether we can help the Contras obtain money from third sources."[^7]
