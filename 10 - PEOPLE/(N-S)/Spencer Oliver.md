[[Spencer Oliver]] was the chief counsel for the [[United States House of Representatives Committee on Foreign Affairs|House Foreign Affairs Committee]]. He was involved in the [[Iran-Contra Affair]] hearings. In 1991, he discussed the events of 1987 with [[Ari Ben-Menashe]], admitting that the Democrats knew the witnesses were lying and that the hearings were a cover-up to avoid impeaching the president.[^1]

### The 1972 telephone tap

The Senate Select Committee on Presidential Campaign Activities names R. Spencer Oliver Jr. as the Democratic National Committee official, the son of Robert Oliver, the Mullen and Company lobbyist for Hughes Tool, whose telephone [[James McCord]] bugged in the first Watergate entry of May 27, 1972.[^2] A 1976 memorandum by [[Bruce Solie]], chief of the Central Intelligence Agency's Security Analysis Group, identifies the "Spencer Oliver" named in a Freedom of Information request from McCord as, according to a 1972 newspaper article, the Executive Director of the State Chairmens Association of the Democratic National Committee, in whose office telephone "an alleged listening device was found."[^3] The Wilson Center's biography of Oliver lists senior positions at the Democratic National Committee from 1966 to 1976, followed by service as Chief of Staff of the Helsinki Commission (1976 to 1985) and as chief counsel of the House Foreign Affairs Committee until 1993; the OSCE Parliamentary Assembly's biography lists him as its Secretary General from 1992 to 2015.[^4]

### Stoffberg letter (1992)

On January 10, 1992, Oliver, chief counsel of the House Foreign Affairs Committee, wrote to the United States District Court for the Eastern District of New York requesting leniency for [[Dirk Stoffberg]], a convicted arms dealer, on the ground that Stoffberg had provided the committee with information about the 1980 hostage events. Judge [[Jack Weinstein]] cited the request in an amended sentencing memorandum and reduced the sentence.[^5] On February 4, 1992, Representative McEwen of Ohio announced that he would offer a privileged resolution on the letter before the House debated House Resolution 258, which created the October Surprise task force.[^5] A reprinted newspaper account quotes Representative Henry Hyde: "By what authority did Spencer Oliver intervene in this case, and why wasn't the minority notified?"[^6]
