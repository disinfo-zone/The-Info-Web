[[Richard Stavin]] was a prosecutor with the [[Department of Justice|Justice Department]]'s Organized Crime Strike Force in [[Los Angeles]]. He was put in charge of the investigation of payola in the record business, whose target was the promoter Joe Isgro, after [[Marvin Rudnick]] was taken off it, and he ran the two-year strike force and [[Federal Bureau of Investigation|FBI]] investigation of suspected labor racketeering involving [[Eugene Giaquinto]], president of [[Music Corporation of America|MCA]]'s home video division; agents attempted to tap Giaquinto's MCA office telephone in September 1987. After a dressing-down by [[Michael DeFeo]] and [[David Margolis]] of the strike force in Washington in December 1988, and the assignment of the department's senior litigation counsel, William Lynch, to take over the payola case, Stavin resigned in May 1989, leaving both cases unresolved. "I was convinced I was about to become the next Marvin Rudnick," he said. Rudnick was removed from the department that July.[^3]

According to [[Cheri Seymour]], Stavin, Rudnick and the FBI agent [[Thomas Gates]] believed on the basis of wiretaps that MCA had been penetrated by organized crime, the wiretaps were sealed, and the [[Los Angeles Organized Crime Strike Force]] was disbanded.[^1] The investigation produced the tax-evasion conviction of the MCA middleman [[Sal Pisello|Salvatore Pisello]], affirmed by the Ninth Circuit in 1989.[^2]
