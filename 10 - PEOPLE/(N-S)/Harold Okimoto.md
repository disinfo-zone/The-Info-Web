Harold Okimoto is a figure linked to [[Robert Booth Nichols]] and alleged organized crime activities, particularly the [[Yakuza]]. He is described as a 67-year-old car dealer in Hawaii and believed by some sources to be a top Japanese organized crime figure based in Hawaii, acting as a top agent in the Yakuza overseas.[^1]

[[Michael Riconosciuto]] claimed that Nichols was under Okimoto's influence and referred to Nichols as Okimoto's "god-son." Okimoto was also represented as an intelligence person who had worked under [[Frank Carlucci]] for years, and reportedly performed services for the [[United States|U.S. government]] during [[World War II]], for which he was rewarded.[^1]

The writer Cheri Seymour wrote that Okimoto's company, [[Island Tobacco]], sold cigarettes at the Casino du Liban, which was controlled by the [[Syria|Syrian]] mob and used as a front by narcotics people.[^1]

### FBI Affidavit and Hawaii Registrations

The 1987 continuation wiretap affidavit of the FBI agent [[Thomas Gates]] states that a Hawaii-plated car seen after [[Eugene Giaquinto]]'s July 15, 1987 meeting was registered to [[Wells Fargo]] at a Honolulu residential address where a Harold Tadao Okimoto lived, which was also the address Nichols gave at his 1985 arrest; that Honolulu police records showed Okimoto arrested for gambling in 1977; and that Hawaii motor-vehicle records showed more than fifty vehicles registered to Wells Fargo Bank at different residences, while the bank had no auto-leasing business or banking facilities in Hawaii under that name. Gates wrote that the registration method led him to believe "some type of covert activity may be involved."[^2] On July 27, 1987 Nichols gave Giaquinto the same Hawaii telephone number he had given at his 1985 arrest, saying "Harold (possibly Okimoto)" would have a tape put on the answering machine.[^2]

[[Peter Zokosky]] told Seymour and [[Kelly O'Meara]] in 2001 that Nichols had once been caught stealing from Okimoto in Hawaii, after which Okimoto brought him into his business. Seymour summarized a December 2, 1982 FBI Los Angeles report as stating that Okimoto took Nichols "under his wing" in Honolulu and had reportedly been involved in racketeering since 1962.[^3] John Connolly reported in [[Spy (magazine)|Spy]] in January 1993 that Nichols listed Okimoto as a former employer on a weapons-permit application and that intelligence sources believed Okimoto a high-ranking member of the Yakuza.[^4] Seymour's 1992 VORTEX account lists Okimoto among the directors of Meridian Arms, the subsidiary of [[Meridian International Logistics]].[^5]
