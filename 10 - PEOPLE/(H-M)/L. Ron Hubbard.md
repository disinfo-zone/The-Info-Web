Lafayette Ronald Hubbard was born at [[Tilden, Nebraska]], on March 13, 1911, according to a [[San Luis Obispo County]] Sheriff's record that the [[Federal Bureau of Investigation|FBI]]'s [[Los Angeles]] office transcribed in April 1967.[^1] The [[United States Navy]]'s personnel file on him, released from the [[National Archives and Records Administration|National Archives]], begins with a [[United States Marine Corps Reserve|Marine Corps Reserve]] certificate: he enlisted in the Marine Corps Reserve on May 1, 1930, was transferred to inactive status the same day, and was discharged by special order of the [[Commandant of the Marine Corps|Major General Commandant]] on October 22, 1931 as a first sergeant with character "Excellent."[^2]

### Naval Intelligence reserve commission, 1941 to 1942

In 1941 Hubbard applied for a commission in the [[United States Naval Reserve|Naval Reserve]] at the [[Washington Navy Yard|Navy Yard]] in Washington. A letter in his file from Arthur F. Johnson, Ph.D., a professor under whom he had studied engineering, recommended him "for a commission in the United States Naval Reserve, for duty in Naval Intelligence."[^3] A memorandum of the [[Chief of Naval Operations]] (Op-16-C) dated April 21, 1941 told the Reserve Section of the [[Office of Naval Intelligence]] that he was applying for assignment to the Public Relations Office and recommended the rank of lieutenant. On June 4, 1941 the Intelligence Officer of the Navy Yard, after interviewing him, recommended appointment as lieutenant (junior grade) in class I-V(S), found him "deficient in academic educational background," and wrote that he "appears to possess qualifications for assignment to the Public Relations Branch, or Foreign Intelligence Branch with particular reference to his familiarity with countries of the Caribbean Area."[^3]

On December 15, 1941 the District Intelligence Officer of the [[Twelfth Naval District]] in [[San Francisco]] ordered him to temporary duty at the Zone III office at [[Vallejo]], in addition to his regular duties; he reported on December 16 and was detached on December 17.[^4] He then went to [[Australia]]. A report to the Commandant of the Twelfth Naval District signed L. D. Causey records that he reached [[Brisbane]] on the [[SS President Polk]] on his way to [[Manila]], was allowed to stay there for onward transport, and "by assuming unauthorized authority and attempting to perform duties for which he has no qualification he became the source of much trouble." Causey wrote that he was "garrulous and tries to give impressions of his importance" and "will require close supervision for satisfactory performance of any intelligence duty," and that he was sent orders in February 1942 to return to the United States on [[USS Chaumont]].[^5] In his own letter of October 19, 1943 requesting orders to landing vessels, Hubbard listed among his qualifications "Service in South West Pacific (ONI) Dec. 41 to March 42."[^6]

On April 16, 1942 the [[Office of the Cable Censor|Chief Cable Censor]] asked that he be detached from intelligence duties in the Twelfth Naval District and ordered to the Office of the Cable Censor in New York. The Chief of Naval Operations endorsement of April 18, 1942 recommended approval and, because the Censorship Organization "is no longer under the cognizance of the Director of Naval Intelligence," that he be transferred out of class I-V(S).[^7]

### Commands, the Coronado Islands board and release from active duty

Hubbard's own summary lists command of [[USS YP-422]] from June to October 1942 and of [[USS PC-815]] from January to July 1943.[^6] The file contains a board of investigation convened by the Commander, [[Fleet Operational Training Command, Pacific]], which sat from June 30 to July 3, 1943 on the firing of three shots from PC-815 and the anchoring of the vessel near the [[Coronado Islands]] (Mexican territory) on June 28, 1943. The board found that no Navy directive authorized the ship to anchor there on June 27 and 28, and Hubbard's fitness report records a letter of admonition from the Commander, Fleet Operational Training Command.[^8] He was hospitalized at the Naval hospital in [[San Diego]] on July 16, 1943 with a duodenal ulcer and returned to duty on October 8, 1943. He later attended the Navy's school of military government at [[Princeton University]] and was admitted to [[Oak Knoll Naval Hospital]] in Oakland on April 10, 1945 from the Naval Civil Affairs Staging Area at the [[Presidio of Monterey]]. A Board of Medical Survey recommended that he appear before a retiring board; the [[Bureau of Naval Personnel]] instead directed his release from active duty, which took effect on February 16, 1946.[^9]

On November 14, 1947, writing from [[North Hollywood]], he tendered his resignation from the Naval Reserve, citing "my bad health for which I was separated from the Navy." The Chief of Naval Personnel replied on December 13, 1947 that the resignation would be held in abeyance and warned that it would end all benefits; Hubbard then asked that it be disregarded. On May 12, 1950 the [[Secretary of the Navy]] approved a Physical Evaluation Board finding that he was fit for the duties of his rank at the time of his release, and his resignation of May 27, 1950 was accepted under honorable conditions.[^10] The separation form in the file lists the American Defense, Asiatic-Pacific and Victory medals and the expert rifle and pistol badges.[^10] On September 28, 1972 and again in 1974 the [[Scientology|Church of Scientology]] wrote to the Navy's [[National Personnel Records Center]] in St. Louis: the Navy declined on November 14, 1972 to release the file to Russell Salamon, and a May 28, 1974 letter from the Church's [[Flag Operations Liaison Office]], Eastern United States, in New York, enclosing a letter from Hubbard, stated that he was commissioned in 1941, "served in the South Pacific" and "was relieved by fifteen officers of rank," and listed fifteen decorations including a Purple Heart.[^11] A Navy memorandum for the record of December 17, 1984 directed that no action be taken on the file "without the specific knowledge of the Navy Liaison Office."[^11]

### Pasadena, Parsons and Allied Enterprises, 1945 to 1946

According to the scholar Henrik Bogdan, Hubbard first visited the house at 1003 South Orange Grove Avenue in [[Pasadena]] on August 1, 1945 with the illustrator [[Lou Goldstone]], then moved in and began a relationship with [[Jack Parsons]]'s sister-in-law and companion [[Sara Northrup]], called Betty.[^12] On January 23, 1946 the [[Agape Lodge]] member [[Jane Wolfe]] wrote to [[Karl Germer]] that Betty "has transferred her emotional life into the hands of one Ron Hubbard, a very likeable Irishman, who lives there."[^13] Parsons wrote to [[Aleister Crowley]] that he had "met Capt. L. Ron Hubbard a writer and explorer," that Hubbard "has no formal training in Magick" but "an extraordinary amount of experience and understanding in the field," and that "We are pooling our resources in a partnership which will act as a parent company to control our business ventures."[^14] Between January 4 and March 4, 1946 Hubbard acted as scribe and seer in the [[Babalon Working]], and on March 6 Parsons reported: "First instructions were received through Ron the seer. I have followed them to the letter."[^14]

On May 12, 1946 the Agape Lodge member [[Louis Culling]] wrote to Germer that Parsons "signed a partnership agreement with this Ron and Betty whereby all money earned by the three for life is equally divided between the three," that Parsons "has put in all of his money," and that Hubbard and Northrup "have bought a boat for themselves in [[Miami]] for about $10,000."[^15] Bogdan names the partnership Allied Enterprises, formed in January 1946 to buy yachts on the East Coast, sail them to California and sell them, and gives Parsons's investment as $20,970.80 against Hubbard's $1,183.91.[^12] Germer wrote to [[Grady McMurtry]] on May 24, 1946 that Crowley's cable reply read "SUSPECT RON PLAYING CONFIDENCE TRICK JACK EVIDENTLY WEAK FOOL OBVIOUS VICTIM PROWLING SWINDLERS."[^16] On July 5, 1946 Parsons wrote from Miami: "I have them well tied up: they cannot move without going to jail," and described Hubbard sailing at 5 p.m. and a squall that forced the boat back, "where I took the boat in custody."[^14] On August 10, 1946, according to the complaint Sara Northrup filed in 1951, she and Hubbard married at [[Chestertown, Maryland]]; she stated that Hubbard's divorce from his first wife, [[Margaret Grubb Hubbard]], was obtained at [[Port Orchard, Washington]], in December 1947.[^17]

On October 5, 1969 [[The Sunday Times]] of London published an account by [[Alexander Mitchell]] of Hubbard's dealings with Parsons, drawn from papers held by "a former admirer of Crowley"; it quoted Hubbard's statement in Scientology publications that "Crippled and blinded at the end of the war I resumed studies of philosophy and by my discoveries recovered so fully that I was reclassified in 1949 for full combat duty."[^18] A clipping of the article is in the FBI's Church of Scientology file: the Washington Field Office sent it to the Bureau in June 1974 after the legal attache in London made a request dated May 10, 1974, saying it had been obtained on June 7, 1974 from the State Department's Passport Office, from a passport file the FBI attributed to Hubbard.[^18] Bogdan reports that the Church paid the newspaper a settlement; its statement of December 28, 1969 said the [[Ordo Templi Orientis]] group practiced "savage and bestial rites" and that Hubbard had been "sent in to handle the situation" at 1003 Orange Grove, adding: "Hubbard's mission was successful far beyond anyone's expectations. The house was torn down. Hubbard rescued a girl they were using."[^12] Bogdan observes that Hubbard never spoke publicly about Parsons or the Babalon Working.[^12]

### Dianetics, the FBI and the 1951 letters

In 1948 the [[San Luis Obispo]] County Sheriff arrested Hubbard on August 17 on a charge of petty theft (checks) from Los Angeles County; he posted $500 bail and the San Gabriel Justice Court disposed of the case on August 31, 1948 on payment of a $25 fine. The FBI's Los Angeles office reported this by teletype addressed to the Liaison Section on April 12, 1967.[^1] His book [[Dianetics: The Modern Science of Mental Health]] was published in 1950 by [[Hermitage House]], and the [[Hubbard Dianetic Research Foundation]] was chartered in New Jersey with offices in Elizabeth, New York, Washington, Chicago, Los Angeles and Honolulu.[^19]

The FBI's Internal Security Section interviewed Hubbard on March 1, 1951. The memorandum records that he said he believed Communists inside the Foundation were undermining it, that he had turned the names of several suspected Communists over to the FBI office in Los Angeles, and that an individual whose name is redacted in the release had been instrumental in driving his wife, Sara Northrup, "to the point of insanity."[^19] On February 23, 1951, he wrote later, his apartment was entered and he was injected and given an electric shock.[^20] In a letter of May 1951 on Foundation letterhead from [[Wichita, Kansas]], addressed to the [[United States Attorney General|Attorney General]], he wrote that "the Communist Party or members of the Communist Party have in the past year wiped out a half a million dollar operation for me" and that he had written to the FBI in Los Angeles, Chicago and Washington. He wrote that Sara Northrup "had attached herself to a Jack Parsons, the rocket expert, during the war and when she left him he was a wreck. Further, through Parsons, she was strangely intimate with many scientists of Los Alamo Gordos [sic]," and he asked why "these people remain at large, free of our press, destructive of our efforts."[^20] A statement dated June 11, 1951 over the name Sara Northrup Hubbard, preserved in the FBI's headquarters file, said that what she had said of Hubbard "in courts and the public prints" had been "grossly exaggerated or entirely false."[^20] In a June 12, 1954 letter to the Phoenix [[Better Business Bureau]] on [[Hubbard Association of Scientologists International]] letterhead, the organization wrote that Hubbard "served with distinction as a naval officer and was selected to Naval Civil Affairs by reason of his knowledge of the Oriental psychology."[^19]

The FBI's file also holds a text of psychopolitics lectures attributed to Beria ("You must dominate the hospitals and universities"). Foster's 1971 report quotes the same "Address by Beria" from the booklet "Brain-washing: A Synthesis of the Russian Textbook on Psychopolitics," describes it as disseminated by the Scientology leadership, and adds that "Its style resembles more that of Mr. Hubbard than that of the Marxist-Leninist phraseology standard in Soviet official literature."[^21][^22]

### Scientology, the Sea Organization and the official inquiries

Hubbard is the founder of Scientology; the Associated Press gave the founding year of the Church of Scientology in Los Angeles as 1954.[^23] The [[Board of Inquiry into Scientology]], appointed by the State of Victoria on November 27, 1963 with [[Kevin Victor Anderson]], Q.C. as its sole member, reported in 1965 that "Scientology is evil; its techniques evil; its practice a serious threat to the community," that Hubbard "falsely claims academic and other distinctions, and whose sanity is to be gravely doubted," and that his B.S. and C.E. claims did not correspond to any [[George Washington University]] degree.[^23] The [[House of Commons]] printed [[John G. Foster|Sir John Foster]]'s enquiry on December 21, 1971; it quotes the July 25, 1968 announcement by the Minister of Health, [[Kenneth Robinson]], that the Government considered Scientology "socially harmful" and that Hubbard and his wife Mary Sue were excluded from the United Kingdom.[^22] The Foster report discusses the "Sea Org" cruising under the Panamanian flag.[^22] The [[Internal Revenue Service]] dispute with the Church, which the Associated Press dated from 1967, ended in a 1993 closing agreement under which the Church dropped its lawsuits and paid $12.5 million; the [[Religious Technology Center]]'s director, [[Mark Rathbun]], called the document "a peace treaty."[^24] A [[Los Angeles Times]] article of 1978 in the CIA's clipping file calls the [[Apollo (Scientology ship)|Apollo]], a 3,280-ton vessel, the flagship of the Church and "for nearly a decade the personal yacht" of Hubbard.[^25]

### The Guardian's Office and Operation Snow White

The grand jury indictment returned in the District of Columbia on August 15, 1978 states that Hubbard, "who held the title of 'Commodore' by virtue of his role as the founder and leader of Scientology exercised overall supervision of the [[Guardian's Office]]"; that [[Mary Sue Hubbard]] held the titles Controller and Commodore Staff Guardian; and that the head of the world-wide Guardian's Office, [[Jane Kember]], reported directly to both.[^26] On November 21, 1973 Kember directed the United States Guardian's Office to obtain all [[Interpol]] documents "connecting its founder, L. Ron Hubbard, to criminal activity."[^26] The stipulation of evidence signed on October 26, 1979 records Guardian's Office agents inside the [[Department of Justice]], the Internal Revenue Service, the [[Coast Guard Intelligence]] office and the [[Drug Enforcement Administration]]; the Justice Department agent was monitoring the office handling Freedom of Information Act suits against the [[Central Intelligence Agency]], the [[Energy Research and Development Administration]], the Army and the Defense Department, to give "an early warning system to any potential subpoena, civil suit, or arrest warrant against L. Ron Hubbard or Mary Sue Hubbard."[^27] The program was called [[Operation Snow White|Snow White]].[^27]

FBI agents searched Scientology offices in Los Angeles and Washington on July 7, 1977.[^27] Mary Sue Hubbard, [[Henning Heldt]], [[Duke Snider]], [[Gregory Willardson]], [[Richard Weigand]], [[Mitchell Hermann]], [[Cindy Raymond]], [[Gerald Bennett Wolfe]] and [[Sharon Thomas]] were found guilty on the stipulated evidence. Mary Sue Hubbard was convicted on Count 23 (conspiracy) and sentenced on December 6, 1979 by Judge [[Charles R. Richey]] to five years and a $10,000 fine; the [[United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit]] affirmed in a per curiam opinion of October 2, 1981.[^28] The government's sentencing memorandum stated that the crimes included "infiltration and theft of documents from a number of prominent private national and world organizations, law firms and newspapers" and "the framing of private citizens who had been critical of Scientology, including the forging of documents which led to the indictment of at least one innocent person."[^29]

In its memorandum of intended decision dated June 1984, Judge [[Paul Breckenridge]] of the [[Los Angeles Superior Court]], in Church of Scientology of California v. [[Gerald Armstrong]], wrote that "The evidence portrays a man who has been virtually a pathological liar when it comes to his history, background, and achievements," that Hubbard "runs the Church in all ways through the [[Sea Organization]], his role of Commodore, and the Commodore's Messengers," and that "He has, of course, chosen to go into 'seclusion.'" The same memorandum found the testimony of former Scientologists including Gerald and Jocelyn Armstrong, [[Laurel Sullivan]] and [[Howard Schomer]] "credible, extremely persuasive."[^30]

On February 17, 1978 the CIA's [[Office of Legislative Counsel]] told the Office of General Counsel's Freedom of Information Division, in response to OGC Memo 78-0912 on the suit Scientology v. Director, FBI, that its indices and the Review Staff material supplied to Congress contained nothing on the Founding Church of Scientology and its affiliated organizations.[^31]

### Scientologists at SRI

Three people central to [[Stanford Research Institute]]'s remote viewing work (later the [[Stargate Project]]) were reported in print as Scientologists. The [[Time (magazine)|Time]] cover story of March 4, 1974, "Boom Times on the Psychic Frontier," called Scientology a "bizarre and controversial cult" and said [[Hal Puthoff]] was its promoter and that [[Ingo Swann]] also practiced it.[^32] The [[New Scientist]] of October 17, 1974, in an article by [[Joseph Hanlon]], wrote of Puthoff: "Puthoff has gone through encounter groups and other West Coast fads, and is now a Scientologist (as is Ingo Swann)."[^32] [[Ray Hyman]] wrote in [[The Humanist]] in 1977 that Puthoff "has made it to the level of a Class III Operational Thetan in the Church of Scientology."[^32] In a [[Washington Post]] article of 1977 [[John L. Wilhelm]] wrote that Puthoff was "a high-ranking Scientologist," that [[Pat Price]] "also was a high-level 'Operating Thetan,'" that the laboratory assistant to Puthoff and [[Russell Targ]] in many of the [[Uri Geller]] experiments was a Scientologist, and that Swann "revealed that there are 'fourteen Clears' working at SRI"; Wilhelm wrote that "the involvement of Scientologists in the tests at SRI partially accounts for at least two government agencies pulling back from funding further work there."[^33] A psychological report on six subjects in the SRI parapsychology study, held in the CIA's STARGATE collection, stated that "at least four of the subjects in this study describe to me an interest in scientology and two of them indicate that they have been involved in the Church of Scientology for some time."[^34] [[Jim Schnabel]] wrote in 1997 that Puthoff and Swann had joined Scientology in the 1960s, that Puthoff resigned in the mid-1970s and afterward supported a group of anti-Scientologists, and that Swann later resigned too.[^35] In a magazine article on Price's appointment to a [[West Virginia]] coal company, left by the CIA's [[Office of Technical Service]] with the [[House Permanent Select Committee on Intelligence]] on November 16, 1977, Price "credits Scientology for 'opening new vistas and abilities of self to me.'"[^34] The transcript of an SRI remote viewing session of October 4, 1973 has Price, tracking Targ, [[Hugh Crane]] and Ann Price and Puthoff through [[Menlo Park]], say: "Just took a look at the Scientology Center. They're not there."[^34]

### Death

Hubbard died on Friday, January 24, 1986 at a ranch near San Luis Obispo, California, after a stroke, according to the [[Washington Times]] of January 29, 1986 preserved in the CIA's reading room; Church president [[Heber Jentzsch]] said Hubbard's body had become "an impediment to his research into the spirit of man" and that "he had discarded his body." The article notes that his eldest son, [[Ronald E. DeWolf]], had alleged in a 1982 lawsuit that Hubbard was dead or incapacitated.[^36] A funeral service text in the CIA's STARGATE collection, attributed to Hubbard, is titled "Founding Church of Scientology."[^37]
