Sybil Leek, known as "Britain's most famous witch," was an astrologer and author who was reportedly recruited by [[British Intelligence]] during [[World War II]]. She was involved in a black propaganda effort, where she provided phony horoscopes for the Germans who believed in astrology[^1]. Her work allegedly included writing a chart that convinced [[Rudolf Hess]], the Deputy Führer of [[Nazi Germany]], to fly to England, leading to his capture[^1]. Jacobsen quotes her son, [[Julian Leek]], as saying, "Mum stayed silent about the Hess affair all her life," and, of the affair, "It's still officially classified."[^2]

After the war, Leek moved to America. The [[New York Times]] obituary gave the year as 1964, after a dispute with the Witchcraft Research Association, of which she had been president, and listed her residences as New York City, Houston, Las Vegas and, for 15 years, Indialantic, Florida.[^3] She was quoted in a classified [[Department of Defense]] monograph on [[Extrasensory Perception]] (ESP) as a noted astrologer and author.[^4]

In July 1972, [[John LaMothe]]'s report, *Controlled Offensive Behavior - USSR*, identified Sybil Leek as a "noted astrologer and author." The report states that "there is great danger that within the next ten years the Soviets will be able to steal our top secrets by using out-of-the-body spies."[^4] Jacobsen wrote that Leek advised the [[Central Intelligence Agency]] on witchcraft and the supernatural[^5], and that "the CIA hired magicians, hypnotists, and even Sybil Leek, Britain's famous white witch."[^6] While her sources were sometimes considered biased in favor of ESP research programs, her insights were noted within the intelligence community[^1].

### Writings

The New York Times obituary credited her with more than 60 books, among them *The Diary of a Witch* (1968), *The Assassination Chain* with Bert Sugar (Corwin, 1976), *Astrological Guide to the Presidential Candidates* (Abelard-Schuman, 1972), and *Numerology*, *Fortune Telling*, *Phrenology*, *Guide to Telepathy* and *The Story of Faith Healing* (all Macmillan). She wrote on the Burgess-Maclean case and the Algerian War as a journalist and columnist. She told an interviewer in 1969, "We are entering the age of Aquarius, and people are searching," and once said she was "a Druid, not a mere witch."[^3] Her son Julian illustrated the 1969 Macmillan *Book of Fortune Telling*, *Phrenology*, *Numerology* and, with Stephen Leek and Gerda Davis, the 1973 *Book of Herbs*.[^7]

### Death

Leek died on October 26, 1982, in [[Melbourne, Florida]], at Holmes Regional Medical Center, after a nine-month battle with cancer. The [[United Press International]] report of October 27, 1982, quoted her son Julian Leek: he said that most people had misunderstood what his mother meant by witchcraft, rejected any association of the practice with devil worship or ritual sacrifice, and described it as a scientific religion. The report recorded that she had written 64 books and claimed that her family had practiced witchcraft since 1134.[^8] Her husband Brian Leek had died in England in 1974; two sons survived her, and her ashes were to be taken to England for burial.[^3]

The New York Times obituary gave her birth date as February 22, 1917, and her age as 65; a 2002 [[BBC]] Inside Out page gave her birth year as 1923.[^3][^9]
