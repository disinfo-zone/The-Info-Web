Alvin Saunders Johnson (December 18, 1874 to June 7, 1971) was an American economist, editor and educator who co-founded the [[New School for Social Research]] in New York and served as its director and then its president.[^1][^2] He was associate editor of the [[Encyclopaedia of the Social Sciences]] and the organizer of the [[University in Exile]], the New School division that gave refuge to scholars whose careers were being upended in Europe after 1933.[^3] The [[New York Times]] reported on June 9, 1971, that he died "Monday night" at his home in Upper Nyack, aged 96, and had been president emeritus since 1945.[^4]

### Nebraska, Columbia and the New Republic

Johnson was born near [[Homer, Nebraska]], and took a bachelor's degree in classics at the [[University of Nebraska]] in 1897. He received a doctorate in economics from [[Columbia University]] in 1902.[^1] He taught between 1902 and 1917 at [[Bryn Mawr College]], Columbia, [[Cornell University]], [[Stanford University]], the University of Nebraska, the [[University of Texas]] and the [[University of Chicago]].[^1] From 1902 to 1906 he edited the [[Political Science Quarterly]], and in the same period he took part in a study of prostitution in New York City made for the [[Committee of Fifteen]], published in 1902 as *The Social Evil*.[^1]

From 1917 he was economics editor of the [[New Republic]] until about 1923.[^1] The school's histories page describes him as editor of the magazine from 1917.[^2]

### The New School, 1919 to 1946

The school's archive says that in 1919 Johnson joined a group of New Republic colleagues and Columbia professors, including [[Charles Beard]], [[James Harvey Robinson]], [[John Dewey]] and [[Wesley Clair Mitchell]], to found the school.[^1] The school's histories page gives 1918 as the year Johnson co-founded it.[^2] The Times obituary gives 1919 and lists Beard, Dewey, [[Thorstein Veblen]], Robinson and Mitchell as his co-founders.[^4] Johnson's own memoir names Mrs. Willard Straight and Mrs. George Bacon among the liberals who launched it.[^5]

The school had no endowment. Rutkoff and Scott's history records that Robinson and Beard found in [[Herbert Croly]] and [[Dorothy Payne Whitney Straight]] "sympathetic and resourceful patrons," and that ten-year pledges of nearly $300,000 came largely from Straight.[^6][^7] In 1922 the New Republic was short of money, and the same history reports that its owners wanted the school closed so that Straight would support the magazine.[^8]

Johnson became director in April 1922, after serving as a trustee, and wrote to Straight after taking the post.[^1][^9] He held the post as director and later president until 1946.[^1] The school's histories page says he officially retired in December 1945 and died in 1971 in [[Upper Nyack, New York]].[^2] He continued as president emeritus, raising money for the school, for about twenty-five years after that.[^1]

### The University in Exile

In April 1933 Johnson began to raise $60,000 a year for two years to support a faculty of fifteen scholars at $4,000 each.[^3] Before the largest gift he had collected between $7,000 and $15,000.[^10]

[[Hiram J. Halle]] gave the entire sum. Rutkoff and Scott write that Halle had pledged the whole amount Johnson required before the announcement of May 13, 1933, that he wanted his name kept out of it, and that he had "made his fortune in petroleum refining and finance."[^10] In Johnson's account, Halle telephoned him after a letter Johnson had written to [[Edwin R.A. Seligman]] reached him. Johnson had answered Halle's question about the amount pledged with "Twelve thousand," and Halle said: "I am prepared to guarantee the sixty thousand a year for two years, so that you can go ahead at once." Johnson writes that [[Frank Altschul]] vouched for Halle's credit, saying that Halle's signature on a check for a million dollars would be accepted by any bank, and that Altschul advised keeping the offer quiet because nobody would contribute if he knew Halle was ready to carry the whole load.[^11] The historian [[Daniel Bessner]] describes Halle as part owner of the [[Gulf Oil Company]] and associated with the [[Rockefeller Foundation]].[^12]

Rutkoff and Scott write that Johnson recruited two additional "Rockefeller scholars," the Italian political scientist [[Max Ascoli]] and the Austrian [[Erich Hula]], and that Johnson persuaded the Foundation to appoint [[Hans Simons]] in Hula's place after Hula first rejected the offer.[^13] The same history lists Johnson's 1934 additions as Alfred Kahler, Fritz Lehmann, Arthur Feiler and Hans Staudinger.[^13] Ascoli joined the University in Exile in late 1933.[^14] Johnson's account of the appointments ends with [[Kurt Riezler]]: "With Riezler the roster was closed."[^15] The school's history records that the Foundation and [[Harold Laski]] of the [[London School of Economics]] recommended [[Leo Strauss]] to Johnson.[^16]

### Rockefeller Foundation and the choice of scholars

Johnson and the Foundation agreed on about a dozen individuals to rescue. The Foundation's associate in [[Paris]], [[Tracy Kittredge]], wrote on March 29, 1933.[^17][^18] Rutkoff and Scott write that the [[Emergency Committee in Aid of Displaced Foreign Scholars]] acted for the Foundation in these cases, and that the Foundation wished to salvage "the most empirical segment of German social science."[^19][^20] The Rockefeller Archive Center's account of the Foundation's refugee program says its officers worked closely with Johnson from 1933 onward, and it cites a July 31, 1941 letter from [[Marc Bloch]] to Johnson in the Foundation's records.[^21]

Johnson writes that the Foundation "had never been very cordial to the New School or to me," and that he decided to ask its president, [[Raymond Fosdick]], for traveling expenses and a two-year stipend for five scholars. He revised the figure to fifteen on the bus to [[Rockefeller Center]] and to twenty-five in the elevator, and when Fosdick asked how many could be cared for he answered that the project was not worth undertaking "unless we are prepared to take care of a hundred." Fosdick said he would consult his trustees and telephoned the next day to say they had approved.[^22] He also writes that the Foundation supplied expert information on which refugees were Nazi or Fascist.[^22] The Times obituary of Johnson repeats the Fosdick account and states that the Foundation approved the request and assured the financing of 100 emigres.[^4]

### The Emergency Committee

The Emergency Committee was formed in May 1933, when [[Alfred E. Cohn]], [[Bernard Flexner]] and [[Fred M. Stein]] approached [[Stephen Duggan]] of the [[Institute of International Education]]. [[Felix Warburg]] and [[Alan Gregg]] advised the organizers, and [[Livingston Farrand]] chaired the committee.[^23] The committee made grants to host institutions rather than to scholars, and it made 335 grants from about 6,000 applicants.[^24] Duggan invited Johnson to lunch after the University in Exile was announced and told him of the committee's plan.[^25] [[Edward R. Murrow]] was its assistant secretary until 1935.[^23]

The committee's records hold a New School file for 1933 to 1945, and they include Johnson's name in executive committee correspondence for 1940 to 1945. They also hold applicant files filed under the heading "Non-Grantees" for [[Hans Speier]], [[Gerhard Colm]], [[Arnold Brecht]], Hans Simons, [[Hans Staudinger]], [[Jacob Marschak]], [[Adolph Lowe]], [[Emil Lederer]], Ascoli, Riezler and [[Wilhelm Reich]].[^26]

### The Ecole Libre

In 1941 Johnson facilitated the establishment of the [[Ecole Libre des Hautes Etudes]] at the New School, which the school's archive calls the Free French School. The school's finding aid says the Ecole Libre offered courses from 1942 to 1946, that it was legally part of the New School, and that its board included [[Henri Focillon]], [[Jean Perrin]], [[Paul van Zeeland]], [[Jacques Maritain]] and [[Gustave Cohen]]. The school severed the Ecole Libre in June 1947.[^27] The Foundation's records group an Ecole Libre file, titled "Refugee Scholars - New School for Social Research - (French Institute)," under its New School grants.[^28]

### Reporting on the faculty

Rutkoff and Scott write that after 1947 Johnson's anticommunist rhetoric intensified, "and in his private letters he acknowledged that he had been meeting regularly with FBI officials since the 1930s to report on the faculty."[^29] The same history quotes [[Sidney Hook]] that on at least one occasion Johnson "wanted to get rid of a Communist Party member" but "delegated it to [[Clara Mayer]]," and says that in 1940 Johnson supported the adoption by the [[Graduate Faculty of Political and Social Science]] of "an explicitly anticommunist clause in its charter" in response to the school's difficulty with the [[United States Department of State|Department of State]] and immigration authorities.[^29]

Rutkoff and Scott write that a Rockefeller Foundation memorandum "indicated that Johnson had also monitored files of 'enemy aliens' in the early 1940s for the federal district attorney." Their endnote for the passage cites a memorandum of January 27, 1942, in the "Hans Eisler File" of the Rockefeller Foundation Archives, with a letter from Johnson to Urban of December 9, 1948.[^30] In the early 1950s, the same history reports, the school's administration received "inquiries from congressional and Senate committees, the FBI, and numerous individuals."[^31]

### Enemy alien hearing board and the 1942 panel

Johnson's memoir records that after America entered the war he was appointed to an enemy alien hearing board, with [[Maurice Davidson]], chairman, and [[Joseph Leary Delaney]]. He writes: "Case after case came before us, well prepared in advance by the FBI. After we had studied the record and had heard the FBI investigators, we would hear the accused."[^32]

In early 1942, Rutkoff and Scott write, Johnson, Hans Staudinger and [[Alexander Makinsky]] met with State Department officials in Washington at a meeting Johnson requested after complaining of departmental "high jinks." They spent the day testifying before what the history describes as "a governmental panel comprised of representatives from the FBI, the Visa Division, Naval Intelligence, and the Department of the Army," and Johnson "had to defend himself against charges of heading a 'Communist outfit.'" Rutkoff and Scott add that officials of the Visa Division regarded the creation of the Gaullist Ecole Libre at the New School as dangerous to American policy.[^33]

### Encyclopaedia of the Social Sciences

The *Encyclopaedia of the Social Sciences* appeared under Seligman as editor-in-chief, with Johnson as associate editor. The title page of volume three, published in 1930, gives that credit.[^34] Bessner writes that Johnson accepted Seligman's offer of the associate editorship in 1927, and that "both the Rockefeller and Carnegie Foundations contributed $1.25 million to the project, which the [[Social Science Research Council]] oversaw."[^35] Rutkoff and Scott name a different pair of funders, the [[Laura Spelman Rockefeller Memorial]] and the [[Russell Sage Foundation]], through which Seligman "secured financial support."[^36]

### New York anti-discrimination work

In 1943 Johnson served as vice-chairman of New York State's [[Commission on Discrimination in Employment]]. The New School's finding aid says he helped draft and pass the New York Fair Employment Act of 1945, and that Governor [[Thomas E. Dewey]] sent him the signing statement and pen.[^37] The [[New York State Division of Human Rights]] describes the 1945 measure as the [[Ives-Quinn Act]], which Dewey signed and which prohibited employment discrimination based on race, creed, color and national origin.[^38] In February 1947 Johnson resigned as chairman of the State Committee Against Discrimination in Education because of his duties on the State Commission for a State University.[^39]

### Papers and later years

The New School Archives hold the Alvin Saunders Johnson collection, 6.5 cubic feet dated 1908 to 1973, in four series: biographical, correspondence, subject files and writings. Its correspondence and subject files concern fundraising, a proposed labor college and a Faculty of Retired Professors.[^40] The finding aid places institutional papers from Johnson's New School years at the [[Yale University]] Library, and says that papers from his [[West Nyack, New York]] home went to the Love Library at his alma mater, the University of Nebraska, which it places in Omaha. Bessner cites a Johnson collection at the Love Library in Lincoln.[^40][^41] Yale University's Manuscripts and Archives holds the Alvin Saunders Johnson papers (MS 615), 1902 to 1969, 5.75 linear feet in 15 boxes, given by Johnson, [[Agnes de Lima]] and Edith Levy in 1967, arranged as correspondence, biographical and subject files, and writings. Its scope note says the papers contain only a very limited amount of material on the New School, the University in Exile, the Ecole Libre and the state commission on discrimination in employment.[^42] Johnson's autobiography, *Pioneer's Progress*, was published in 1952.[^40] He received honorary degrees from [[Brandeis University]] in 1957, [[Hebrew Union College]] in 1960 and [[Freie Universität Berlin]] in 1964.[^37]
