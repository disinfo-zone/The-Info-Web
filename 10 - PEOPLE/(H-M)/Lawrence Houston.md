Lawrence Houston (1913 to August 15, 1995) "helped draft legislation that created and shaped the CIA, then served as the spy agency's general counsel from its founding in 1947 until his retirement in 1973." Director [[John Deutch]] said he had served "longer than the combined tenures of his eight successors" and "played a central role in virtually every significant Agency undertaking." He supervised the legal work of the exchange of [[Rudolf Abel]] for [[Francis Gary Powers]] and of the ransom of the [[Bay of Pigs Invasion|Bay of Pigs]] prisoners. He held the [[National Security Medal]]. He died at [[Westport, Massachusetts]].[^1]

### The Agreement of 1954

"In 1954, the CIA pointed out to the [[Department of Justice]] that, in many cases involving CIA, prosecution would require public disclosure of sensitive Agency operations and procedures." The department answered that the agency should investigate such allegations itself. "If, after investigation, it appeared that prosecution would be precluded by the need to reveal sensitive information, then the Agency should so indicate in its files and not refer the case to the Department of Justice." The presidential commission of 1975 found that the department had "abdicated its statutory duties and placed on the Director of Central Intelligence the responsibility for investigating criminal conduct and making the prosecutorial decision." The arrangement lasted until January 1975.[^2]

### Kennedy

On May 7, 1962, Houston and the director of security, [[Sheffield Edwards]], briefed Attorney General [[Robert F. Kennedy|Robert Kennedy]] on the agency's use of [[Johnny Roselli]] and [[Sam Giancana]] against [[Fidel Castro]]. The occasion was the prosecution of [[Robert Maheu]]'s wiretappers in [[Las Vegas]], which the agency wished dropped. "If you have seen Mr. Kennedy's eyes get steely and his jaw set and his voice get low and precise you get a definite feeling of unhappiness," Houston testified. He recalled the instruction: "If we were going to get involved with Mafia personnel again he wanted to be informed first." [[J. Edgar Hoover]] recorded Kennedy's "great astonishment" two days later. The inspector general concluded in 1967 that "the Attorney General was not told that the gambling syndicate operation had already been reactivated." Houston sent Edwards's memorandum of record to Kennedy on May 15. On the agency's copy of his covering note [[Richard Helms]] wrote: "Sen. Kennedy read this on 8 March '67." Helms told Director [[John McCone]] on August 16, 1963, that Edwards's memorandum was the only written record of the agency's relationship with Giancana.[^3][^4]

### Nosenko and Garrison

The agency's own account of the confinement of [[Yuri Nosenko]] states that the [[CIA Office of Security|Office of Security]] "together with the Office of General Counsel became increasingly concerned with the illegality of the Agency's position," and that their representations to Helms ended it in October 1967.[^5]

On March 3, 1967, two days after [[Jim Garrison]] arrested [[Clay Shaw]], the chief of the [[Domestic Contact Service]] in [[New Orleans]], [[Lloyd Ray]], recommended that Houston be briefed on Shaw's past reporting "to be on the safe side." On May 1 [[Herbert J. Miller Jr.|Jack Miller]], a former assistant attorney general, telephoned Houston with information "from within Garrison's office" supplied by [[Walter Sheridan]] of [[NBC]]. His memorandum of October 2, written as the agency's [[Garrison Group]] considered its response, recommended doing nothing beyond resisting subpoenas.[^6]
