Eugene Hasenfus was born on January 22, 1941 in Marinette, [[Wisconsin]], and died on November 26, 2025 in [[Menominee, Michigan]], at age 84 after a nine-year battle with cancer, according to an [[Associated Press]] report citing his funeral home obituary.[^1] He was the only one of four men aboard a Fairchild C-123K cargo plane to survive its shootdown over southern [[Nicaragua]] on October 5, 1986.

### Air America and the CIA's record of him

His brother William Hasenfus of [[Oshkosh]], Wisconsin told reporters on October 7, 1986 that Eugene was a former Marine who during the [[Vietnam]] War flew on [[Air America]] aircraft in Vietnam, [[Laos]], [[Cambodia]] and [[Thailand]], responsible for weighing and balancing equipment, and that he had gone to work for a [[Florida]] air freight firm in the summer of 1985 after working in construction.[^2] A CIA memorandum for the record of a [[Senate Foreign Relations Committee]] briefing on October 10, 1986 states that Hasenfus worked in 1966 as a packer and kicker for the CIA proprietary [[Air Asia]], later worked for Air America, and resigned in February 1974; it states that the pilot William J. Cooper also worked for Air Asia, and that the co-pilot [[Wallace Sawyer]] later worked for [[Southern Air Transport]].[^3]

### The shootdown

On October 5, 1986 a [[Sandinista]] soldier fired a shoulder-launched missile at the C-123K, which was flying arms to the [[Contras]]. Cooper, the pilot, Sawyer, and a third crew member were killed; Hasenfus, the cargo handler or "kicker," survived and was captured.[^4][^5] The independent counsel's report states that the shootdown "settled irrevocably the question of CIA assumption of Enterprise assets," because with a $100 million contra aid bill awaiting final approval the agency could not associate itself with the operation.[^6] [[Felix Rodriguez]], in [[Miami]], reached the Office of the Vice President by telephone, and the notes of Colonel [[Samuel Watson]] of October 6 record "C-123 = Ollie's" and list the crew, with the kicker's name garbled.[^7]

The identity of the airframe is recorded differently by different sources. A DEA spokesman in Miami, Jack Hood, told *The New York Times* that "we believe that the C-123 that was shot down in Nicaragua was the same plane that was used in the Barry Seal operation," and the CIA Inspector General's Mena case file refers to Seal's C-123K as N4410F.[^8][^9] The CIA's October 10, 1986 briefing record says the plane "was sold as U.S. military surplus in 1983" and that its purchaser had not been determined.[^3] *The [[The Christian Science Monitor]]* reported that news reports said it was bought six months earlier by a California company, [[Four Aces Inc.]], and leased to Southern Air Transport, which said it did not own it and had serviced it; the independent counsel's report states that Southern Air Transport received $1,935,596 for contra-related services including the purchase of a C-123.[^10][^11] The Inspector General's December 24, 1996 review found no document indicating a CIA link with N4410F and none showing that Southern Air Transport owned or leased a C-123K.[^9] See [[Barry Seal]] for the Seal records.

### Press conference and the names Gomez and Medina

On October 9, 1986 Hasenfus said in [[Managua]] that he had made ten flights supplying the contras, four from [[Aguacate]] air base in [[Honduras]] and six from [[Ilopango Airbase]] in El Salvador, that 24 to 26 "company people" assisted in El Salvador including "two Cuban nationalized Americans that worked for the CIA," and that he was to be paid $3,000 a month plus housing. He named "Max Gomez" and "Ramon Medina" as overseers of housing, transportation, refueling and flight plans.[^10][^7] Gomez was Felix Rodriguez and Medina was [[Luis Posada Carriles]].[^6] The independent counsel's chapter on Clair George records that Hasenfus had publicly identified Gomez, and that [[Clair George]] was charged with falsely denying before Congress that he knew Gomez's true identity.[^12] [[Gary Webb]] wrote in *Dark Alliance* that [[Alan Fiers]], then in charge of the CIA's Contra program, said the [[Sandinistas]] found among the crew's effects "all sorts of pocket litter that identified and connected them back to Ilopango," including cards of [[NHAO]] employees and flight logs.[^13]

### The CIA and State statements

On October 10, 1986 the CIA and the [[State Department]] briefed the Senate Foreign Relations Committee for three hours. Assistant Secretary of State [[Elliott Abrams]] stated that "there is no U.S. Government involvement" in the supply system; Clair George and Fiers gave a statement denying a CIA connection with the C-123 or its crew; and the briefing record states that "no one knows why Mr. Hasenfus has claimed that he worked for CIA," that "he might have been exposed to pressure by the Sandinistas," or that he "might have been hired under 'false-flag' circumstances."[^3] The same day Fiers briefed House intelligence committee staff and described Rodriguez as a twenty-year Agency veteran who retired in 1978.[^14] In *Dark Alliance* Webb wrote that administration officials sent false press advisories, booked spokesmen on talk shows and sent CIA officials to give false testimony to congressional committees, and that this delayed congressional investigation long enough to pass the $100 million aid bill.[^13]

### Trial and release

Hasenfus was tried in Nicaragua and sentenced to 30 years in prison on charges related to delivering arms to the contras; he was pardoned a month later by President [[Daniel Ortega]].[^1] The independent counsel found that [[Oliver North]]'s notebooks of October 8 to 15, 1986 record conversations with [[Richard Secord]] and others about getting a lawyer for Hasenfus and about death benefits and funerals for the dead pilots; the Enterprise's manager [[Edward de Garay]] and North's friend [[William Haskell]] went to [[Panama]] to find a lawyer, without success, and Secord and [[Albert Hakim]] had [[Willard Zucker]] hire a [[Switzerland|Swiss]] lawyer who could not reach Nicaraguan officials. After the shootdown Secord told [[Rafael Quintero]] to set aside a $75,000 check and a $50,000 check to establish a defense fund; Quintero endorsed the first with a forged signature and sent both to a [[Bahamas]] bank. In December 1986 the Enterprise's air-support manager John Piowaty carried $6,000 in cash from Southern Air Transport in Miami to Hasenfus's wife Sally, two months of his salary, and the Enterprise arranged $3,000 in cash for Sawyer's burial at a funeral home in [[Magnolia, Arkansas]].[^6]
