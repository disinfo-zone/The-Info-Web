Lee Herbert Hamilton was born April 20, 1931, in Daytona Beach, Florida. He served as a Democratic member of the U.S. House of Representatives from Indiana's 9th congressional district from 1965 to 1999, a tenure of 34 years. During that period he served on the [[House Permanent Select Committee on Intelligence|House Intelligence Committee]], including as chairman, and was a central figure in congressional oversight of intelligence affairs. After leaving Congress he became President and CEO of the Woodrow Wilson International Center for Scholars and Director of the Center on Congress at Indiana University.[^1]

### House October Surprise Task Force

In 1992, Hamilton co-chaired the House October Surprise Task Force alongside Rep. Henry Hyde (R-Illinois), with [[E. Lawrence Barcella, Jr.]] serving as chief counsel. The Task Force was established to investigate allegations by [[Gary Sick]], [[Ari Ben-Menashe]], and others that [[Ronald Reagan|Reagan's]] 1980 presidential campaign had secretly negotiated with [[Iran]] to delay the release of American hostages past Election Day.[^2]

The Task Force's January 1993 final report found "no credible evidence" of such a deal. However, the investigation has been criticized on several grounds. A U.S. Embassy Madrid cable indicating [[William J. Casey]] was in Madrid in summer 1980 "for purposes unknown" was withheld by Bush administration officials and never provided to the Task Force. The Russian intelligence report confirming Casey's Madrid and Paris meetings with Iranians arrived in January 1993 after the Task Force had closed, and was buried in raw files until journalist [[Robert Parry]] discovered it later.[^2]

In 1992 Hamilton selected E. Lawrence Barcella, Jr. as chief counsel. Hamilton selected him in March 1992, saying then that he sought "someone that had a large amount of international experience." Asked in a May 29, 1992 *Hartford Courant* account about a 1985 legal opinion on a contra arms shipment that the paper attributed to the lawyer, Hamilton said he would "investigate all aspects of criticisms of staff, including Larry or anybody else."[^3]

Barcella told Parry that substantial new evidence arrived in the final month of the inquiry and that he asked Hamilton to extend it three months. Barcella said Hamilton, recognizing how nasty the Republican reaction would be, turned down the request.[^4] In Meldon's 2008 article, Barcella is quoted as saying that Hamilton cited the complexity of getting the task force reauthorized in a new Congress.[^5] Hamilton told Parry in 2010 that he might have explained the problem of the authorization running out at the end of the session: "All I could have done is go before the next Congress and request reauthorization."[^6] Hamilton told Parry in 2010 that he had no recollection of such a proposal, and in 2011 that he had no recollection of the request.[^7][^8] Parry also wrote that Barcella recommended the extension to Hamilton, and that Hamilton did not extend the task force.[^5]

House Resolution 585 of October 2, 1992 authorized the task force to file its final report after adjournment and before noon on January 3, 1993; Representative [[Anthony C. Beilenson]] (D-California), introducing it, said the investigation "may continue after we adjourn, but no later than the end of this year."[^9] A Russian report addressed to Hamilton, requested by him on October 21, 1992, reached the task force on January 11, 1993. Parry reported that Hamilton told him, "I don't recall seeing it."[^10] Hamilton held a press conference on January 13, 1993, dismissing the allegations.[^5]

Hamilton's January 24, 1993 New York Times op-ed, "Case Closed," stated that the staff "conducted over 230 formal interviews and depositions" and that the task force found "virtually no credible evidence" of the accusations.[^11]

Parry wrote that Barcella attributed the refusal to Hamilton's recognition of the Republican reaction, and Hamilton has denied recalling the request.[^4][^8]

### 9/11 Commission

Hamilton co-chaired the National Commission on Terrorist Attacks Upon the United States (the 9/11 Commission) alongside Republican former New Jersey Governor Thomas Kean from 2002 to 2004. The Commission's report, released July 22, 2004, became one of the best-selling government documents in American history. Hamilton advocated for the Commission's intelligence reform recommendations, many of which were enacted in the Intelligence Reform and Terrorism Prevention Act of 2004.[^1]
