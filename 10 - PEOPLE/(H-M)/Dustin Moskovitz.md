Dustin Moskovitz (born May 22, 1984) cofounded [[Facebook]] with [[Mark Zuckerberg]], his roommate at [[Harvard University]], where he studied economics. From February 2004 to November 2008 he held senior roles at Facebook, including chief technology officer and vice president of engineering. He then cofounded [[Asana]] with [[Justin Rosenstein]], and with his wife [[Cari Tuna]] established [[Good Ventures]], the foundation that supplies nearly all of the grant money of [[Open Philanthropy]], renamed Coefficient Giving in 2025.[^1][^2] *[[Semafor]]* wrote that its founders and many early employees "were sympathetic to the utilitarian philosophical movement known as [[Effective Altruism|effective altruism]]."[^3] [[Peter Thiel]] was Facebook's first outside investor.[^4] Moskovitz grew up in a small city in inland Florida; his father was a psychiatrist and his mother a teacher and artist, and as a boy he wanted to be an Air Force pilot. He was Facebook's third employee. In 2009 he met Tuna, then a *[[The Wall Street Journal|Wall Street Journal]]* reporter, on a blind date arranged by her colleague, and they married in 2013; shortly before, he wrote on Quora: "Cari and I are stewards of this capital. It's pooled up around us right now, but it belongs to the world. We intend not to have much left when we die."[^5]

### Asana

Moskovitz has been an Asana director since December 2008 and its chairman since December 2019. He was chief executive from October 2010 to July 2025, and also chief financial officer from 2009 to 2017. Asana went public by direct listing on the [[New York Stock Exchange]] in September 2020.[^4] In 2026 he held 74.2 percent of the company's voting power through his own shares, the Dustin A. Moskovitz Trust, a Roth IRA and shares held by Good Ventures; he bought 19,273,127 shares in a private placement on September 7, 2022.[^1] In 2016 [[Sam Altman]] led a 50-million-dollar investment in Asana.[^6] [[Adam D'Angelo]], Facebook's chief technology officer from 2006 to 2008 and founder of [[Quora]], sat on Asana's board from December 2008 to 2026 while also a director of [[OpenAI]]; [[Matthew Cohler]] of [[Benchmark Capital]], another Facebook alumnus, was an Asana director from 2009 to 2026. Asana's 2026 proxy statement reported, as related-person transactions, 1.1 million dollars in AI services bought from [[Anthropic]], noting that Moskovitz "has a minority ownership interest in Anthropic," and 300,000 dollars bought from OpenAI, noting D'Angelo's seat on its board.[^1]

### Good Ventures and Open Philanthropy

Good Ventures, whose directors are Moskovitz, Tuna and [[Divesh Makan]], the controlling partner of [[ICONIQ Capital]], held 10.1 billion dollars in assets in mid-2025 and received 1.4 billion dollars in contributions that year.[^2][^7] Open Philanthropy had given away about 7 billion dollars by September 2026, most of it from the couple's fortune.[^3] Of this, more than 580 million dollars went to building the field of AI-risk research; the chief executive, [[Alexander Berger]], wrote in 2024 that without its AI grantees "fewer people with AI experience would have been positioned to help, and policymakers would have been slower to act."[^8] Its grants include 30 million dollars to OpenAI in 2017, with a board seat for [[Holden Karnofsky]], whose grant page disclosed that the OpenAI researchers [[Dario Amodei]] and [[Paul Christiano]] were Open Philanthropy advisers who lived in Karnofsky's house;[^9] 55 million dollars in 2019 to launch the [[Center for Security and Emerging Technology]] at [[Georgetown University]] under the former IARPA director [[Jason Matheny]]; 16 million dollars in 2023 to the [[RAND Corporation]] after Matheny became its chief executive, "to be spent at the discretion of" Matheny; 5.16 million dollars in 2022 to the [[Center for AI Safety]]; and 36 million dollars in 2025 to [[Redwood Research]].[^10]

### Anthropic

Moskovitz took part in Anthropic's first funding round of 124 million dollars in 2021, and in 2023 told *[[Politico]]* that "my goal in investing [in Anthropic] stems from the exact same place as our non-profit work" and that any returns "will be entirely redirected back into our philanthropic work." The Open Philanthropy grantmaker [[Luke Muehlhauser]] was then one of Anthropic's four directors, and Karnofsky had married Anthropic's president, [[Daniela Amodei]].[^6] Muehlhauser resigned from the board in May 2024, writing that it had become complicated to be both an AI governance grantmaker at Open Philanthropy and a director of a frontier AI company.[^11] In April 2026 the *[[Washington Examiner]]* reported that in 2025 Moskovitz had transferred his Anthropic stake, "which had reportedly grown to roughly $500 million, to his charity to avoid the appearance of conflicts of interests."[^12] *[[Fortune]]* described the same 500-million-dollar stake as moved "into a nonprofit vehicle to reinvest its returns."[^13] Asana's proxy statement of April 20, 2026 described Moskovitz as having "a minority ownership interest in Anthropic."[^1] *Semafor* called Coefficient Giving "a favorite of Anthropic executives"; its chief executive, Alexander Berger, said in 2026: "We're definitely not the Anthropic Foundation."[^3]

### Washington

Open Philanthropy set aside nearly 3 million dollars in 2022 for the first fellows of the [[Horizon Institute for Public Service]], which pays technology fellows placed in congressional offices, federal agencies and think tanks, among them the offices of Senators [[Martin Heinrich]], [[Mike Rounds]] and [[Todd Young]], whom [[Chuck Schumer]] had put in charge of AI legislation; *Politico* wrote that Open Philanthropy had "effectively created" Horizon.[^6] Matheny served in the Biden [[White House]] from March 2021 to June 2022. According to documents the *Washington Examiner* obtained under the [[Freedom of Information Act]], he hired [[Ben Buchanan]], director of CSET's CyberAI project, under the Intergovernmental Personnel Act, with Georgetown continuing to pay Buchanan's salary and the government reimbursing it; the paper noted that CSET had received 97 million dollars from Open Philanthropy between 2019 and 2021, and that Buchanan became an architect of the Biden export controls on advanced chips that Anthropic later urged the Trump administration to keep. Buchanan said he "scrupulously complied with all legal and ethics rules." Senator [[Ted Cruz]], who named Moskovitz in a 2024 letter, wrote that "large AI technology companies are influencing the Biden administration's AI policy from the inside."[^12]

The lobbying arm of the Center for AI Safety stated that it secured 10 million dollars from Congress in 2024 to establish the [[U.S. AI Safety Institute]], where Christiano became head of AI safety.[^14] The 501(c)(4) [[Americans for Responsible Innovation]], founded in late 2023 by the former congressman [[Brad Carson]], lists Coefficient Giving among its funders with the [[Omidyar Network]], and reported in-house lobbying expenses of 1.51 million dollars in 2025. The [[Open Philanthropy Action Fund]] has paid outside lobbyists every year since 2021, the [[Kyle House Group]] since 2023.[^15]

### The Press

By 2025 Coefficient Giving supplied most of the funding of the [[Tarbell Center for AI Journalism]], which pays journalists placed at *[[Time (magazine)|Time]]*, [[Bloomberg]], *[[The Verge]]* and other newsrooms and publishes the newsletter *Transformer*; Coefficient's director of communications, [[Naina Bajekal]], was a former executive editor of *Time*.[^16] In September 2026 the publication *Effort*, founded by [[Brian Chau]], wrote that "Coefficient pays the salaries of two dozen journalists at major news outlets," that Open Philanthropy had pursued a "placement" strategy "to put their hand-picked employees in US and UK government offices," and that Moskovitz was the third-largest Democratic donor in 2020 and the second-largest in 2024.[^17]

### Political Giving

On September 8, 2016, Moskovitz and Tuna announced 20 million dollars in contributions to defeat [[Donald Trump]] and elect [[Hillary Clinton]], calling Trump's promises to voters who felt left behind "quite possibly a deliberate con." They named 5 million dollars each to the [[LCV Victory Fund]] and [[For Our Future PAC]], and wrote that "we have reservations about anyone using large amounts of money to influence elections."[^18] [[Federal Election Commission]] records itemize contributions from Moskovitz of about 18.0 million dollars in the 2015-2016 cycle, led by 6 million to [[Priorities USA Action]], 4.5 million to the LCV Victory Fund, 2.5 million to For Our Future and 1.65 million to [[Color of Change PAC]]; 5.7 million in 2017-2018, including 3 million to [[MoveOn]] and 2 million to the [[Senate Majority PAC]]; 51.8 million in 2019-2020, including 46.89 million to the Democratic super PAC [[Future Forward]] and 830,600 dollars to the [[Biden Victory Fund]]; 5.9 million in 2021-2022, including 3.3 million to Future Forward and 1.9 million to its affiliate FFPA; and 50.9 million in 2023-2024, of which 50 million went to Future Forward in eight payments between August 28 and October 22, 2024, 30 million of it in October, and 929,600 dollars to the [[Harris Victory Fund]]. None had been itemized for the 2025-2026 cycle by September 2026.[^19] In March 2024 *[[Puck]]*'s [[Theodore Schleifer]] wrote that Moskovitz had spent "up to $100 million to defeat Donald Trump last cycle" and was "leaning into his power... and his checkbook" to beat back "the Andreessens and Musks of the world."[^20]
