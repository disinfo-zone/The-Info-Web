Richard Bruce Cheney was born in [[Lincoln, Nebraska]] on January 30, 1941, and grew up in [[Casper, Wyoming]]. He was a congressional fellow in 1968, worked at the [[Office of Economic Opportunity]] and the [[Cost of Living Council]], and was a vice president of the investment firm [[Bradley, Woods & Co.]] from 1973 to 1974. He was deputy assistant to President [[Gerald R. Ford]] in 1974 and 1975 and his chief of staff from 1975 to 1977. He represented Wyoming in the [[United States House of Representatives]] from January 3, 1979, until March 17, 1989, was [[Secretary of Defense]] from 1989 to 1993, was chief executive of [[Halliburton]] from 1995 to 2000, and was Vice President under [[George W. Bush]] from January 20, 2001, to January 20, 2009.[^1]

### The Rockefeller Commission Report

In June 1975, as deputy to the chief of staff, [[Donald Rumsfeld]], Cheney led the editing of the draft report of the [[Rockefeller Commission 1975|Rockefeller Commission]] on [[Central Intelligence Agency|CIA]] activities in the [[United States]]. His handwritten comments survive. The [[National Security Archive]], which published the White House and commission files on February 29, 2016, reported "numerous edits to the report by then-deputy White House Chief of Staff Richard Cheney," and said that Cheney "led the way in 'editing' the Rockefeller report."[^2] The section "Alleged Plans to Assassinate Certain Foreign Leaders," 86 pages long, was removed and replaced with a note that the commission had lacked time to finish the subject; language calling CIA activities "unlawful" was softened to "improper," except for the drug experiments, which were labeled "illegal"; and a recommendation that CIA employees be able to report improper activities was narrowed to orders "outside of established channels."[^3] The archive's account of the same edit says it replaced the commission's language on unlawful activity with language about actions that merely exceeded the agencies' statutory authority, and that the Cheney-inspired edit added recommendations the panel had not voted.[^2] In the working copy of the report in the Ford Library file (Document 15), the chapter on mail openings keeps the conclusion that the CIA's domestic mail opening programs "were unlawful," strikes through a sentence stating that the mail-cover aspects of the projects "were not unlawful," and strikes "This was improper under the National Security Act," with handwriting added beneath it. The chapter on Operation CHAOS keeps "Some domestic activities of Operation CHAOS unlawfully exceeded the CIA's statutory authority" and strikes the sentence "These activities were improper."[^3]

The archive reported that Cheney and White House Counsel [[Philip Buchen]], citing concerns raised by [[Henry Kissinger]], intervened to induce the commission's staff director, [[David W. Belin]], to cancel a press conference on the assassination findings.[^2] The June 5, 1975 commission section on CIA plots against foreign leaders, which covered plots against [[Fidel Castro]] and [[Rafael Trujillo]], was not released with the final report.[^3] Strategy papers for the report prepared in Cheney's office in June 1975 argued that giving the [[Church Committee]] the minutes and records of covert action groups "would be an unprecedented act."[^3]

### The Holystone Memoranda, May 1975

On May 25, 1975, the [[New York Times]] published an article by [[Seymour Hersh]] on an American undersea intercept operation. Memoranda from the Cheney files show the White House response over the following week.[^4] On May 30, Donald Rumsfeld, then in [[Brussels]], cabled Cheney asking to be told when the secretary of defense had made his recommendation to the [[National Security Council]] on whether the operation should continue. Rumsfeld wrote that the FBI investigation of the disclosure should begin soon, unless starting it would adversely affect the operation, in which case he directed that it not be initiated.[^4]

The same day, [[Robert McFarlane]] of the National Security Council staff sent Cheney a memorandum marked "Holystone" giving the Defense Department position. The Navy's tentative view was that planning for continuation should go forward, and the memorandum states that "to the extent that an investigation could generate publicity leading to a Soviet reaction, an investigation is considered a bad idea." It adds that [[James R. Schlesinger]] had concurred in the Navy's position.[^5] Cheney's memorandum to Rumsfeld the same day, summarizing the McFarlane report, recommends: "Based on the above, I recommend that no investigation be launched for the time being."[^6]

A May 31, 1975 memorandum in the Cheney files records that [[Edward Levi]] had instructed the criminal division and the [[Federal Bureau of Investigation|FBI]] to prepare a proposal for an investigation. It states that "we should not have an FBI investigation if there is no intention to prosecute," and that "we believe the Hersh story of May 25 is based on papers filed with the court in the [[Victor Marchetti|Marchetti]] case."[^7] An undated memorandum from [[Rex E. Lee]] and [[John C. Keeney]] set out the steps needed for a possible prosecution under 18 U.S.C. 793 and 798 and for a contempt citation tied to the Marchetti protective order, and named an interview with Hersh as the initial step.[^8]

### Iran-Contra Minority Views

As a House Republican on the [[Iran-Contra Affair]] investigating committees, Cheney signed the minority views in the committees' report of November 1987 (H. Rept. 100-433, S. Rept. 100-216). The minority views were drafted principally by the committee staffer [[Michael J. Malbin]], whom the staff list names as minority editor and writer.[^9] They quoted [[Federalist No. 70]] on the executive: "energy in the executive is a leading character in the definition of good government."[^9] They argued that the Constitution protects the President's ability "to persuade U.S. citizens to engage voluntarily in otherwise legal activity to serve what they consider to be the national interest. That includes trying to persuade other countries to contribute their own funds for causes both countries support. To whatever extent the [[Boland Amendment|Boland Amendments]] tried to prohibit such activity, they were clearly unconstitutional."[^9]

On December 10, 1986, Cheney questioned Robert McFarlane before the [[House Permanent Select Committee on Intelligence|House Intelligence Committee]] about the Boland Amendment. McFarlane testified that in the summer of 1985 he "went to considerable length to determine whether" [[Oliver North]] had violated it, and that a document search "turned up two or three inches of paper, that reported on contacts that did occur between Colonel North and myself, indeed the President and Contra leaders." The majority report wrote that in this testimony McFarlane withheld his concerns about the documents and asserted that they proved North had fully complied with the Boland Amendment.[^10]

### Secretary of Defense, 1989 to 1993

Cheney recalled in the [[Miller Center]] oral history of the [[George H. W. Bush]] presidency that "Panama was a great training experience for us as an administration. We had the opportunity to have an objective, have to use force, coordinate with the Congress, deal with the public, all of those things that come with using the force. It was really the first time we'd done it as an administration, and we learned from that." The invasion of [[Panama]] in December 1989 removed [[Manuel Noriega]]. [[Colin Powell]], then Chairman of the Joint Chiefs of Staff, said in the same project: "Nobody even remembers the Panama war. You don't ever hear of it. But I viewed it as the template of how we should do things."[^11]

In 1990 Cheney announced additional base closures and realignments. Public Law 101-510 provided for the [[Defense Base Closure and Realignment Commission]] to meet in 1991, 1993 and 1995, and the commission's 1993 report discusses the 165 bases in the Secretary's list, which was submitted to the commission on March 12, 1993, after Cheney had left office.[^12]

In February 1992 the Pentagon produced a draft [[Defense Planning Guidance]] for fiscal years 1994 to 1999, dated February 18, 1992. The New York Times published excerpts in March 1992. The National Security Archive's 2008 release of declassified drafts records that the draft aimed to "prevent the reemergence of a new rival," and that [[I. Lewis Libby]] sent Cheney cover memoranda on March 20 and March 26, 1992 with revised drafts. Colin Powell and [[Paul Wolfowitz]] approved the guidance in May 1992.[^13] PBS reported that the White House ordered Cheney to rewrite the document after the leak.[^14] The archive records that Cheney issued a revised version in January 1993 as the "Regional Defense Strategy."[^13]

### Halliburton

Cheney was chief executive of Halliburton from October 1995 to 2000. The company's FY1998 annual report, filed as an amendment, lists him as chief executive officer "since October 1995."[^15] The House biographical directory gives "chairman and chief executive office of the Halliburton Company 1993-2000" and, in the same entry, "senior fellow, American Enterprise Institute 1993-1995."[^1] [[Ray Hunt]] of [[Hunt Oil Company|Hunt Oil]] joined its board in 1998 (the 10-K/A lists a power of attorney he signed in October 1998 as a director) and went at once onto its compensation committee, which that year gave Cheney a bonus of 1.1 million dollars and restricted stock worth 1.5 million on a salary of 1.18 million.[^16] Halliburton, which acquired [[Dresser Industries]] in 1998, reported in the same filing that since 1976 approximately 190,000 claims had been filed against current and former Dresser divisions and subsidiaries alleging injury from inhaled asbestos fibers.[^15]

Cheney and [[William E. Bradford]], chairman and chief executive of Dresser Industries, met briefly on August 25, 1997, when the meeting concerned Halliburton's then pending acquisition of [[NUMAR Corporation]], and met again in Dallas on October 2, 1997. On January 17, 1998, during a customer's quail hunt in South Texas, they agreed to discuss the matter further in Dallas. At a Dallas meeting on January 27, 1998, Bradford said that Dresser was not for sale. Cheney then proposed a merger at an exchange ratio of 0.95 Halliburton share for each Dresser share, with Bradford as chairman of the combined company and Cheney and [[David J. Lesar]] keeping their Halliburton posts. Bradford said he would expect an improved exchange ratio.[^17] The merger agreement was dated as of February 25, 1998, and the final ratio was one Halliburton share for each Dresser share. The merger was completed on September 29, 1998.[^18]

On August 3, 2004, the [[Securities and Exchange Commission]] announced enforcement actions against Halliburton, its former chief financial officer, Gary V. Morris, and its former controller, Robert C. Muchmore Jr., over the company's failure to disclose a 1998 change to its accounting practice. The commission said Halliburton's public statements about its 1998 and 1999 income were materially misleading. Halliburton agreed to a cease-and-desist order and to a $7.5 million penalty. The release stated that "the company's former Chief Executive Officer, Vice President Richard B. Cheney, provided sworn testimony and cooperated willingly and fully in the investigation."[^19]

Halliburton's former subsidiary [[Kellogg, Brown & Root]] (KBR) pleaded guilty in February 2009 in federal court in [[Houston, Texas|Houston]] to a five-count criminal information and agreed to pay $402 million in criminal fines. Halliburton and KBR jointly agreed to pay $177 million in forfeited profits to the SEC in a concurrent civil action, for a combined $579 million. The bribes were paid to officials of the [[Nigeria|Nigerian]] government from 1995 until 2004 to win contracts to build liquefied natural gas facilities on [[Bonny Island]], valued at more than $6 billion.[^20] The payments began in 1995, the year Cheney became chief executive, and continued until 2004.

The Army's [[Logistics Civil Augmentation Program]] (LOGCAP) had been created in 1985; in 1992 its concept was changed to a single, centrally managed worldwide planning and services contract, and on August 3, 1992, during Cheney's tenure as Secretary of Defense, Brown and Root Services Corporation of Houston was awarded the contract competitively, a cost-plus-award-fee contract for one year with four option years. The [[United States Army Corps of Engineers]] has administered it since 1992. Four companies competed, and a Commerce Business Daily notice drew 37 requests for the solicitation.[^21]

On December 11, 2003, the [[Defense Contract Audit Agency]] issued a draft audit concluding that KBR had overcharged by up to $61 million for fuel imported into Iraq from Kuwait between May and September 2003 under the Restore Iraqi Oil contract; on January 15, 2004, the agency asked the Pentagon's inspector general to consider a formal investigation after finding "suspected irregularities."[^22] [[Bunnatine Greenhouse]], the Army Corps of Engineers' senior procurement executive, told the Senate Democratic Policy Committee on June 27, 2005 that "the abuse related to contracts awarded to KBR represents the most blatant and improper contract abuse I have witnessed during the course of my professional career." She testified that the Office of the Secretary of Defense controlled the award of the Restore Iraqi Oil contract, that she had written on the justification and approval document a caution against extending the noncompetitive effort beyond one year, that the commander of the Corps waived KBR's requirement to provide cost and pricing data about a week after the December 2003 audit without her approval, and that on October 6, 2004 she was notified that she would be removed from the Senior Executive Service and from her contracting post.[^23]

### Continuity of Government

In 2008 [[Jane Mayer]] wrote in [[The Dark Side (Mayer book)|The Dark Side]] that during the 1980s Cheney, then a Republican congressman from Wyoming, took part in one of the classified continuity-of-government programs of the [[Ronald Reagan|Reagan]] administration. "Every year, usually during congressional recesses, Cheney would disappear in the dead of the night." Along with "some four or five dozen federal officials," Cheney "would pretend for several weeks to be chief of staff to a designated substitute 'president,' bivouacked in some remote location in the United States."[^24]

Mayer wrote that a secret executive order signed by Reagan established "a means of re-creating the executive branch without informing Congress," and that Cheney "was evidently unperturbed by this oversight." She wrote that "Mann and others have suggested that these doomsday drills were a dress rehearsal for Cheney's calm, commanding performance on 9/11," citing [[James Mann]]'s account of the neoconservatives in [[The Vulcans]]. Her excerpt describes an eyewitness diary that recorded Cheney's conduct in the [[Presidential Emergency Operations Center]] on September 11, 2001.[^24] [[Andrew Cockburn]] wrote in his 2007 book on Donald Rumsfeld that Rumsfeld "loved these games" and that "[t]here were others who were frequent players in the exercises, notably Dick Cheney," quoting a former Pentagon official: "Cheney and the others often had other priorities. Rumsfeld always came."[^25]

### Vice President, 2001 to 2009

On September 16, 2001, Cheney appeared on [[Meet the Press]] with [[Tim Russert]], broadcast from [[Camp David]]. The official White House transcript records: "We also have to work, though, sort of the dark side, if you will. We've got to spend time in the shadows in the intelligence world. A lot of what needs to be done here will have to be done quietly, without any discussion, using sources and methods that are available to our intelligence agencies, if we're going to be successful."[^26]

The [[9/11 Commission]] records that the Secret Service ordered Cheney to the shelter beneath the White House after American Airlines Flight 77 turned south at 9:34 a.m., and that he entered the tunnel to the shelter at 9:37.[^27] The commission concluded that Cheney "arrived in the room shortly before 10:00, perhaps at 9:58." Cheney told the commission that he called the President to discuss the rules of engagement for a combat air patrol over Washington, and that the President "signed off" on the concept that pilots could shoot down a plane that would not divert. The report states that the President "emphasized to us that he had authorized the shootdown of hijacked aircraft."[^27] When a military aide reported an aircraft 80 miles out, between 10:10 and 10:15, the commission reports that the Vice President "authorized fighter aircraft to engage the inbound plane." Scooter Libby described the reaction as quick and decisive, "in about the time it takes a batter to decide to swing." White House deputy chief of staff [[Joshua Bolten]] suggested that Cheney confirm the order with the President, and the report logs the call at 10:18.[^27]

On January 29, 2001, President Bush established the National Energy Policy Development Group with Cheney as chairman. The [[Government Accountability Office]] (then the General Accounting Office) demanded its records on July 18, 2001, reported to Congress on August 17, 2001 that the Vice President had persisted in refusing, and on February 22, 2002 sued for them, the first time it had used its statutory authority to sue an executive branch entity.[^28] [[Judicial Watch]] and the [[Sierra Club]] also sued under the Federal Advisory Committee Act, and the case reached the Supreme Court as [[Cheney v. United States District Court]]. The group had issued its final report five months after its creation. On June 24, 2004, Justice [[Anthony Kennedy]] delivered the opinion of the Court, which vacated the decision of the [[United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit]] and remanded the case, reported at 334 F.3d 1096.[^29]

On August 26, 2002, in a speech to the [[Veterans of Foreign Wars]] convention in [[Nashville, Tennessee|Nashville]], Cheney said that during the spring of 1995 inspectors were on the verge of declaring Iraq's chemical weapons and missile programs accounted for, "[t]hen Saddam's son-in-law suddenly defected and began sharing information," and that "[t]hat should serve as a reminder to all that we often learned more as the result of defections than we learned from the inspection regime itself."[^30] The son-in-law was [[Hussein Kamel]]. The record of his debriefing by [[Rolf Ekeus]] of [[UNSCOM]] and the [[International Atomic Energy Agency]] on August 22, 1995 has Kamel saying: "All weapons - biological, chemical, missile, nuclear were destroyed."[^31]

I. Lewis Libby, Cheney's chief of staff, was indicted on October 28, 2005, in the CIA leak investigation of the disclosure of the identity of [[Valerie Plame]], a CIA officer. At the press conference announcing the indictment, special counsel [[Patrick Fitzgerald]] said: "We make no allegation that the Vice President committed any criminal act."[^32] Libby was sentenced on June 22, 2007 to 30 months' imprisonment, two years of supervised release and a $250,000 fine. On July 2, 2007, President Bush commuted the prison term to expire immediately and left the supervised release and the rest of the sentence in effect.[^33]

The Senate Select Committee on Intelligence's report of June 5, 2008, on prewar public statements, found that "statements by the President and the Vice President indicating that [[Saddam Hussein]] was prepared to give weapons of mass destruction to terrorist groups for attacks against the United States were contradicted by available intelligence information." It also found that statements "by President Bush and Vice President Cheney regarding the postwar situation in Iraq . . . did not reflect the concerns and uncertainties expressed in the intelligence products."[^34]

The [[Senate Armed Services Committee]]'s inquiry into the treatment of detainees, dated November 20, 2008, records that the [[Office of Legal Counsel]]'s opinions of August 1, 2002 were issued "after consultation with senior Administration attorneys, including then White House Counsel Alberto Gonzales and then-Counsel to the Vice President David Addington."[^35]

The Inspectors General of the Justice, Defense, Central Intelligence Agency, [[NSA|National Security Agency]] and Office of the Director of National Intelligence reviewed the President's Surveillance Program under Title III of the FISA Amendments Act of 2008. Their unclassified report of July 10, 2009 states that "[c]ertain senior officials either declined or did not respond to our requests to be interviewed for this review, including Counsel to the Vice President David Addington."[^36]

### Allegations

[[Cathy O'Brien]], who describes herself as a survivor of [[Project Monarch]] mind control, named Cheney among officials she said abused her, in *Trance Formation of America* (1995, with Mark Phillips). Her claims about named officials are uncorroborated.[^37]

### Death

Cheney died on Monday, November 3, 2025, at the age of 84, according to a statement from his family.[^38] The family wrote that "his beloved wife of 61 years, Lynne, his daughters, Liz and Mary, and other family members were with him as he passed," and that "the former vice president died due to complications of pneumonia and cardiac and vascular disease."[^39] George W. Bush called his death "a loss to the nation and a sorrow to his friends."[^38]
