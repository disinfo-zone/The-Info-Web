Agustin Edwards Eastman (died April 24, 2017, aged 89) owned [[El Mercurio]], the principal newspaper of [[Chile]]. In September 1970, after [[Salvador Allende]] won the presidential vote, he went to Washington, where [[Donald Kendall]], the president of [[PepsiCo|Pepsi-Cola]], arranged for him to see [[Henry A. Kissinger|Henry Kissinger]], [[United States Attorney General|Attorney General]] [[John Mitchell]], and the director of central intelligence, [[Richard Helms]]. The [[40 Committee]] later voted his newspaper 1,665,000 dollars. He spent the Allende years in the [[United States]] as a vice president of PepsiCo.[^1][^2][^3]

### September 1970

Kissinger and Mitchell had breakfast with Kendall and Edwards on the morning of September 15, 1970, according to Mitchell's calendar. Helms testified in 1975: "Prior to this meeting with the President the editor of El Mercurio had come to Washington and I had been asked to go and talk to him at one of the hotels here, this having been arranged through Don Kendall, the head of the Pepsi Cola Company." He added: "I have this impression that the President called this meeting where I have my handwritten notes because of Edwards' presence in Washington and what he heard from Kendall about what Edwards was saying about conditions in Chile." At the meeting that afternoon President [[Richard Nixon]] instructed Helms to organize a military coup, the operation that became [[Project FUBELT]]. An ethics tribunal of the Chilean journalists' guild found in 2015 that Edwards had briefed Helms on the disposition of Chilean politicians and senior officers and on the equipment and logistical capacity of the navy, air force, and army.[^1][^3]

Two days after the breakfast, the [[ITT Corporation|ITT]] representatives [[Hal Hendrix]] and [[Robert Berrellez]] recommended that American companies "pump some advertising into Mercurio" and help revive propagandists formerly supported by "the Matte and Edwards groups."[^4]

### The Subsidies

The 40 Committee authorized 700,000 dollars for El Mercurio on September 9, 1971, and 965,000 dollars more on April 11, 1972. A [[National Intelligence Estimate]] of August 1971 had concluded that the paper "had managed to retain its independence" under Allende. The [[Church Committee]] described the vote a month later as "at some variance" with that finding. From 1970 to 1975 Edwards lived in the United States as a vice president of PepsiCo and president of its [[Foods International]] division. He resumed the direction of El Mercurio in 1982 and held it until 2008.[^2][^3]

### Expulsion

On April 21, 2015, the ethics tribunal of the metropolitan council of the [[Colegio de Periodistas de Chile]] expelled Edwards on a petition filed by members in November 2014. It cited his receipt of CIA money "aimed at destabilizing Salvador Allende's government through communications operations," and his paper's part in April 1987, during the visit of Pope [[John Paul II]], in a staged operation with the security services that accused two young men of subversion and was used to justify their torture. He did not appeal. The researcher [[Peter Kornbluh]] called him "the most important collaborator according to the CIA in creating the conditions for a coup d'état."[^3][^5]
