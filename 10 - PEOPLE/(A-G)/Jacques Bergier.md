Jacques Bergier was a journalist, former French resistance spy, and author with strong ties to the intelligence community and an interest in the supernatural. He is known for bringing secret government [[Extrasensory Perception|ESP]] programs to public attention[^1].

In December 1959, Bergier published an article in the French magazine *Constellation* titled "La transmission de pensée, arme de guerre" (thought transfer, weapon of war). The article reported that ESP tests had been conducted aboard the [[The Nautilus|USS Nautilus]], the world's first nuclear-powered submarine. The purpose of the experiment was to determine if long-distance telepathic communication could be achieved through barriers like thousands of miles of seawater, thick polar ice, and the metal walls of a submarine[^1].

According to Bergier, the ESP experiment involved simple sender-receiver trials using [[Zener Cards]]. He identified [[William H. Bowers]], director of the Biological Department of the Air Force Research Institute, as the man overseeing the joint-service ESP experiment. Bergier's story claimed that the sender and receiver communicated telepathically over a sixteen-day period; Bergier and [[Louis Pauwels]] give July 25, 1959 as the date the submarine embarked and August 10, 1959 as its return to Groton.[^1]

In Le Matin des magiciens, Bergier and Pauwels wrote that the [[Rand Corporation]] laid a report before [[Dwight D. Eisenhower|President Eisenhower]] at the beginning of 1957 proposing telepathic communication with submarines, and listed parapsychology laboratories at Rand, [[Westinghouse Electric Corporation|Westinghouse]], [[General Electric]], Bell Telephone and [[Redstone Arsenal]]. A footnote in the passage cites a Rand Corporation report of August 31, 1958.[^2]

An expanded version of his story, "The Secret of the Nautilus," was published in France's top science journal, *Science et Vie*, in February 1960. While no authors were identified, editor [[Gerard Messadie|Gérald Messadié]] stated, according to [[Annie Jacobsen]], that multiple sources had confirmed the story; [[Martin Ebon]] recorded in 1980 that Messadié named Bergier as his source. [[J. B. Rhine]] of the [[Duke University Parapsychology Laboratory]] was identified as the civilian scientist assigned to the project, with reports of "about 75% of the telepathic tries are said to have been successful"[^1].

The Soviets used the news story to their strategic advantage, with [[Leonid Vasiliev|Leonid L. Vasilev]], Russia's leading ESP researcher, claiming that Soviet parapsychology research was stimulated by the *Nautilus* reports[^1].

In 1963 the Sunday supplement [[This Week]] printed excerpts of the book as "The Great Nautilus ESP Hoax" and, according to the Parapsychology Foundation, reported that Bergier and Pauwels admitted they "had elaborated on reports they had heard, but not verified" and had "given the submarine a name."[^3] Ebon, who visited Messadié in 1980, wrote that Bergier concocted the story, quotes a New York Times obituary of November 24, 1978 describing Bergier as a part-time consultant for French intelligence after the war, and quotes Messadié as saying that Bergier claimed to have extracted the information from intelligence sources.[^4] [[Jim Schnabel]] wrote that Bergier "apparently had connections to French intelligence services."[^5] In 1992 the Soviet defense newspaper [[Trud]] named Bergier as the author of the French article that had caused considerable commotion in December 1959.[^6]
