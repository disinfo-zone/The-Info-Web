Marcos Aguado, recorded in the official files as Marco Antonio Aguado Arguello and in the Justice Department glossary as Marcus Aguado, was a Nicaraguan pilot who served as air operations chief of [[Eden Pastora]]'s [[ARDE]] forces in [[Costa Rica]] from 1984. [[Central Intelligence Agency]] cables, Senate subcommittee hearings and a [[Department of Justice Office of the Inspector General|Justice Department Inspector General]] review recorded allegations that he was involved in drug trafficking, and the reporter [[Gary Webb]] named him in the [[Dark Alliance]] series. He later worked at [[Ilopango Airbase|Ilopango Air Force Base]] in [[El Salvador]].

### Early service

The CIA inspector general's account of Aguado's background states that he was a [[Sandinistas|Sandinista]] Air Force pilot from 1979 to 1980 and then a commercial pilot for [[Aeronica]] until he defected in 1983 and joined the [[Contras]].[^1] Webb's cast of characters calls him a "CIA-trained pilot" and a business associate of [[Norwin Meneses]].[^2] In his October 31, 1987 videotaped deposition, played at the April 1988 hearing, Aguado said that he became head of the southern air force and then second in command to Pastora.[^3]

In an April 1983 cable the agency recorded that Aguado had been offered a job flying an aircraft for the Southern Front. By September 1984 it recorded him as Chief of Air Operations for the [[Sandino Revolutionary Front]] (FRS) and ARDE and as Pastora's personal pilot. According to Pastora, Aguado was also his son-in-law.[^1]

### Air operations for ARDE, 1984 to 1986

A December 1984 cable reported that Pastora had designated Aguado "one of his 18 commandantes, to be in charge of air operations and logistics at Ilopango." The same cable said that Aguado had not flown any missions inside [[Nicaragua]] since September 1983, that he "is closely associated with" Pastora, [[Adolfo Chamorro]] and [[Gerardo Duran]], and that "as such he may very well be actively participating in alleged narcotics activities . . . or at least aware of such activities."[^1]

The [[Kerry Committee|Senate subcommittee]] described Aguado in its December 1988 report as "Eden Pastora's air force chief." In his deposition Aguado said that traffickers "took advantage of the anti-Communist sentiment which existed in Central America" and "undoubtedly used it for drug trafficking," and that "They fooled people."[^3][^4] Referring to the Contra resupply operations, he said the traffickers used "the same connections, the same air strips, the same people," and that the drugs "would later on go to the U.S."[^4]

### The Morales aircraft

The subcommittee report reproduces a notarized purchase order dated October 1, 1984, under which a McDonnell-Douglas DC-3, the civilian designation for a C-47, was transferred to Aguado for one dollar, signed by [[George Morales]] as seller and by Aguado as purchaser.[^4] The subcommittee found that $10,000 had been given to Chamorro to cover the cost of moving the aircraft from [[Haiti]] to Ilopango.[^4] The CIA inspector general records a C-47 flown from Haiti to El Salvador by Aguado on October 16, 1984, under an agreement that Chamorro reached in Miami with an unidentified Cuban trafficker.[^1] The subcommittee identifies the trafficker as Morales.[^4]

Between October 18, 1984 and February 12, 1986, the subcommittee reported, some 156,000 pounds of material were moved from Ilopango to airfields in Costa Rica on 24 flights, eleven of them to [[La Penca]] on the Nicaraguan side of the San Juan River.[^4] A [[U.S. Customs Service|Customs]] document furnished to the subcommittee showed that Morales entered the United States from the [[Bahamas]] on October 13, 1984 with Aguado, [[Octaviano Cesar]] and Chamorro, and that the three declared $400,000 in cash and checks. They said the money had been returned to Morales after clearing Customs.[^4]

Webb quotes [[Jonathan Kwitny]]'s 1987 reporting in [[The Nation]] that the C-47 was "repeatedly piloted into and out of Ilopango Air Force Base in El Salvador by Marcos Aguado."[^2] Webb also reports that when Aguado quit the war in May 1986 he sold the aircraft for $30,000, to Meneses, and that in 1997 Aguado said the plane was still being used by Meneses in [[Venezuela]].[^2]

### Where Morales met the Contra leaders

The subcommittee report says that [[Marta Healey]] introduced Morales to Chamorro, Aguado and Cesar at a meeting in [[Miami]], and that Morales was then under indictment for marijuana smuggling. According to the report, each of the three Contra leaders denied knowing of the indictment when they first met Morales at Healey's house in Miami.[^4] Webb places a March 1984 meeting at Morales's offices in [[Fort Lauderdale]].[^2] The Justice Department Inspector General records Morales as saying that after Healey's May 1984 approach he met with Cesar, Chamarro and Aguado in Miami.[^5] [[Celerino Castillo]] writes that Healey, the former wife of Chamorro, approached Morales in 1984 after his second arrest and arranged a meeting with Chamorro, "Southern group air force chief Marcos Aguado" and Cesar, and that Aguado and Cesar told Morales they were CIA agents and asked for financial and military assistance.[^6] Morales told the subcommittee that he agreed to help the Contras "with their needs," and that "they in exchange would help me with my objective, which was solving my indictment."[^4]

The same debriefing places a June 1983 meeting at [[Opa Locka]] airport, attended by members of the [[Medellin Cartel]], which Morales said Aguado and Gerardo Duran had flown a group into. Morales said that Aguado refused to fly the cocaine shipments because of his "CIA connections."[^5]

[[Alan Fiers]], then chief of the agency's Contra program, told the [[Senate Select Committee on Intelligence]] on July 31, 1987 that Morales had seen [[John Hull]] in Morales's office with Aguado in 1983, but that Morales had not met Hull.[^7]

### Morales's testimony that they were CIA agents

In the July 15, 1987 hearing Morales testified that the Contra leaders "represent themselves as CIA agents," and that he "heard about they being CIA agents," naming Cesar and Aguado.[^8] At the April 7, 1988 hearing he gave the fuller account that "they told me that they were CIA agents. Two of them said they were," followed by the names of Cesar and Aguado.[^3]

### Cables from 1985 to 1988

A May 1985 cable stated that Aguado, then an FRS/ARDE pilot, could "easily" provide concrete evidence linking [[Karol Prado]], Pastora and other Southern Front Contras to drug trafficking.[^1] A January 1986 cable said that Aguado "should have knowledge of the recent arrest" of Gerardo Duran, which the cable tied to a 600-kilogram shipment of cocaine from Morales.[^1]

In April 1986, Adolfo Chamorro planned to denounce Carol Prado, Eden Pastora and Marco Aguado as being the ones involved in drug trafficking, and said he had the evidence to prove it. A DEA debriefing of Chamorro on April 25, 1986 produced no narcotics information, and the cable reported that "all Chamorro wanted to talk about were politics and war."[^1]

In April 1987 a station reported that an embassy officer had been told that Aguado, Prado, [[David Mayorga]], Chamorro and Duran "are presently involved in shipping cocaine from Nicaragua via Costa Rica to the United States."[^1] In July 1987 a message from Octaviano Cesar said that Aguado wanted to contact the CIA to provide information tying Pastora to "past drug trafficking." The station declined to meet Aguado because of counterintelligence concerns and passed the information to the local DEA office.[^1]

On July 31, 1987, Fiers gave the Senate Intelligence Committee an account of the narcotics allegations concerning Aguado, Cesar, Mayorga, Adolfo and Roberto Chamorro and Duran.[^9]

In March 1988 a station cable reported that [[Reyner Veliz Cruz]], a Guatemalan described as a suspected drug trafficker, had been traveling with Aguado, and that the two had flown twin-engine aircraft into Ilopango in February 1988 and again in March 1988, after customs officers had left. The cable said that Aguado "has bragged that he still works for CIA," that customs personnel at Ilopango "assume that Aguado has connections with drug trafficking," and that Aguado could not obtain a United States visa.[^1][^9]

The agency's own assessment reads: "No information has been found to indicate that CIA ever attempted to develop additional independent information that would confirm or refute the allegations against Aguado."[^1]

### Iran-Contra testimony

[[Robert Owen]], a courier for [[Oliver North]], testified in his [[Iran-Contra Affair|Iran-Contra]] deposition that Aguado was Pastora's pilot: "He was Pastora's pilot. There was concern about him."[^10] The subcommittee report says that Owen told the Iran-Contra committees he had told North that Prado was involved in trafficking drugs out of [[Panama]], and that Pastora's pilot, Marco Aguado, "was also involved." The subcommittee was unable to validate Owen's claims. Prado denied the allegations and said he believed the drug trafficking allegations against Pastora were the result of a CIA effort to discredit him.[^4]

### Costa Rica and the Hull ranch

Webb says Aguado was "formally accused" of cocaine trafficking in Costa Rica and was no longer welcome there.[^2] The CIA inspector general reports that in a March 7, 1989 summary, a Costa Rican judge had charged Hull with "hostile acts" and "drug trafficking." Costa Rican court documents claimed that planes piloted by Duran and Aguado had landed at Hull's airstrips carrying military supplies and, with Hull's knowledge, left loaded with drugs.[^7] Hull has said that the allegations of [[Gary Wayne Betzner]] are "not true, not true!"[^7]

### Ilopango

The Mercury News reported in 1996 that Meneses described Aguado as one of his oldest friends and said he worked for the Salvadoran air force high command.[^11] Webb states that Aguado made arrangements for Pastora's planes to be hangared at Ilopango from 1983 under an agreement with the [[Salvadoran Air Force]].[^2] Soon after North set up his resupply operation at the base, Webb writes, Aguado moved there permanently as an aide to a high-ranking Salvadoran air force commander.[^2] The [[Christian Science Monitor]] reported in October 1986 that the Contras had used Ilopango "at least since 1984," and that General [[Juan Rafael Bustillo]] "has been a major contact person for aiding the contra shipments, according to diplomats and other informed sources."[^12]

The Justice Department Inspector General glossary lists "AGUADO, Marcus" as "Contra pilot at Ilopango Airport, alleged drug trafficker, and reportedly Eden Pastora's representative in El Salvador."[^13]

### Enrique Miranda's account

Webb describes [[Enrique Miranda]] as a former Sandinista intelligence officer who became a CIA double agent during the Contra war. Webb dates Miranda's testimony to a 1992 trial in Nicaragua and quotes it: "Norwin was selling drugs and tunneled the benefits to the Contras with help of high-ranking military officials of the Salvadoran Army, especially with the help of the head of the Salvadoran Air Force and a Nicaraguan pilot named Marcos Aguado."[^2] Webb's notes date the testimony to a Nicaraguan case against Meneses filed in 1991.[^14] The Justice Department Inspector General dates the Nicaraguan trial to 1991 and records Miranda's sentence of fourteen years in August 1992.[^15]

The CIA inspector general summarizes Miranda's allegation that Aguado flew weapons to Medellín, Colombia, during the 1980s and returned with cocaine that he stored at Ilopango.[^1] Miranda told the Justice Department Inspector General's investigators in 1997 that Aguado had said he flew Salvadoran military planes from Ilopango to a military airport in [[Texas]], that Aguado frequently described flying to [[Colombia]] with weapons and returning with cocaine stored at Ilopango, and that American planes then came to El Salvador with weapons and went back to the United States with drugs.[^15] Webb quotes Miranda as saying that Aguado "became a colonel in the Salvadoran Air Force, sharing all the privileges of a high-ranking military officer in El Salvador and was accepted by subordinate ranks as Deputy Commander of the Salvadoran Air Force," and that "The flights he directed went as far as Colombia, where they were loaded with cocaine and then redirected to the United States, to a U.S. Air Force base located in the state of Texas."[^2] Webb reports that Miranda said Aguado and Meneses supervised the loading of cocaine at Ilopango onto U.S.-bound aircraft owned by the Salvadoran Air Force and, on occasion, by [[Southern Air Transport]], a Miami-based CIA contractor.[^2]

Webb reports that Miranda testified he had dined with Aguado at Meneses's mansion in [[Managua]] in 1991, where Aguado described flying for the Colombian cartels. Aguado is quoted as saying that he once took a Salvadoran Air Force bomber and leveled a warehouse of Medellín cartel cocaine on behalf of the rival Cali cartel, and that he planned to bomb the prison where [[Pablo Escobar]] had been briefly held.[^2] Webb reports that Miranda doubted Aguado's boast about flying weapons to the cartels until a Salvadoran Air Force colonel and his associates were arrested in 1992 for selling bombs and high explosives to Colombian drug dealers.[^2]

The inspector general's assessment is that "the failure to report such important information in 1996 makes it difficult to credit Miranda's account in 1997."[^15] The same chapter records that Miranda said Meneses had invited him to watch the Texas operation, but that when pressed he said he had not seen it occur, and that most of what he told the investigators came second-hand, mostly from Meneses.[^15] The CIA inspector general's summary states: "No information has been found to support Miranda's allegations."[^1]

Webb reports that Miranda and former Nicaraguan antidrug official [[Roger Mayorga]] placed the Texas base near [[Fort Worth]], without knowing its name. Webb says that the only air force base near Fort Worth at the time of the alleged flights was the now-closed [[Carswell Air Force Base]], home of a [[Strategic Air Command]] bomber squadron, and that the [[U.S. Air Force]] told a requester in a 1997 Freedom of Information Act case that the flight records for Salvadoran military aircraft in the mid-1980s had been destroyed years ago.[^2]

### The Meneses trailer and the 1991 seizure

The San Jose Mercury News reported on August 18, 1996 that during Meneses's 1991 trial in Nicaragua, Meneses testified that Aguado sold him an auto transport trailer that Meneses's men were stuffing with cocaine when Nicaraguan police arrived. Aguado was not charged, and the newspaper could not reach him for comment.[^11] Webb gives the price as $125,000, and writes that in an interview Aguado denied knowing how Meneses would use the trailer, although Miranda said he was present when Aguado and Meneses discussed drug trafficking one evening at Meneses's mansion.[^2] The Justice Department Inspector General records that Miranda and Meneses were arrested by Nicaraguan authorities in November 1991 in connection with the seizure of 764 kilograms of cocaine bound from Managua to the United States.[^15]

### The 1992 bomb case

In April 1992 [[Semana]] reported that Salvadoran authorities had uncovered a plan to kill Escobar in the [[Envigado]] prison. An immigration officer at San Salvador's airport noticed two Colombians who had changed their names to re-enter El Salvador, and detectives followed them to a February 22, 1992 meeting with [[Roberto Antonio Leiva Jacobo]], whom Semana identified as an army colonel. The Colombians were lost to surveillance on February 25. Semana reports that five 500-pound bombs were taken from a battalion at the San Salvador airport on February 26, that Leiva and his men were arrested on March 4 with one of the bombs, after a small aircraft they had used left with part of the cargo, and that Leiva and his associates confessed that they had been hired by members of the Cali cartel, which paid four million dollars for the bombs.[^16]

### Later record

Webb's cast of characters lists Aguado as "Currently in San Salvador," and describes John Hull as having worked closely with suspected drug traffickers Aguado and [[Dagoberto Nunez]] on Contra projects in Costa Rica.[^2] Webb also writes that Aguado was a close associate of Hull, and that Morales said in testimony that Cesar and Aguado made clear that if he assisted them they could make his legal problems disappear.[^2]
