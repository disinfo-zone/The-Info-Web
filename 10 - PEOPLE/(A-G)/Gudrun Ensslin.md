Gudrun Ensslin (born August 15, 1940, in Bartholomä; died October 18, 1977, at [[Stuttgart-Stammheim Prison]]) was a [[West Germany|West German]] student activist and co-founder of the [[Red Army Faction]] (RAF) with [[Andreas Baader]] and [[Ulrike Meinhof]].[^1][^2] The daughter of a Protestant pastor, she left university without a degree and was drawn into the militant milieu by the student protests of 1967 and 1968. The Frankfurt court psychiatrist [[Reinhard Redhardt]] called her the "backbone of the group."[^3] She was convicted in 1977 as a co-perpetrator of the group's May 1972 bombings and was found hanged in her cell on the morning of October 18, 1977, hours after [[GSG 9]] stormed the hijacked [[Lufthansa Flight 181]] at [[Mogadishu]], [[Somalia]].[^2][^4]

### Early life and education

Ensslin was born in [[Bartholomä]], in the former district of Schwäbisch Gmünd in [[Württemberg]], the fourth of seven children of the Protestant pastor [[Helmut Ensslin]] (1909 to 1984), who was born in [[Ulm]].[^1][^5] Her father was pastor in Bartholomä from 1936 to 1948, moved to [[Tuttlingen]] in 1948, and from 1958 worked in [[Stuttgart-Bad Cannstatt]].[^1][^5] In June 1958 she went for a year to the [[Warren High School]] in [[Warren, Pennsylvania]], as an exchange student of the International Christian Youth Exchange, and lived with a host family of the local Methodist congregation.[^1][^5]

She took the Abitur in March 1960 at the Königin-Katharina-Stift in [[Stuttgart]] and from 1960 to 1963 studied German, English and philosophy at the University of [[Tübingen]], without a degree.[^1][^5] At Tübingen she attended a colloquium on contemporary literature given by [[Walter Jens]].[^5] Her application for a scholarship from the [[Studienstiftung des deutschen Volkes]] was at first unsuccessful. From 1963 to 1964 she trained at the [[Pädagogische Hochschule Schwäbisch Gmünd]] and passed the state examination for primary-school teachers in March 1964.[^1][^5] From the summer of 1964 she studied at the [[Freie Universität Berlin]] and prepared a doctoral thesis on the writer [[Hans Henny Jahnn]] with a scholarship from the Studienstiftung. She did not complete the thesis.[^1][^5]

In 1963 she and [[Bernward Vesper]] (1938 to 1971), her partner and later fiance, founded the [[Verlag studio neue literatur]] in Tübingen, which published two books, and in 1964 she co-edited the anthology "Gegen den Tod. Stimmen gegen die Atombombe" as its first volume.[^1][^5] Ensslin also handled distribution of the collected works of [[Will Vesper]] (1882 to 1962), Bernward's father, a National Socialist literary functionary.[^1][^5] The poet [[Günter Maschke]], who contributed to the press as a young author, later said that the two mostly sold the old Vesper's books, among other things through advertisements in the Deutsche National- und Soldatenzeitung.[^5] She translated English texts for Vesper's [[Voltaire Flugschriften]] pamphlet series.[^1] The partnership is dated 1962 to 1968.[^1]

A son, [[Felix Ensslin]], was born on May 13, 1967.[^1][^2] In a report to the Studienstiftung she wrote that he was born "a little early, so very small and delicate."[^5] Ensslin ended the engagement with Vesper two months before the birth and left their shared flat in January 1968. She refused to consent when Vesper wished to adopt the child. Vesper died on May 14, 1971, in Hamburg, eleven days after naming the boy his sole heir.[^5] Felix Ensslin, later a curator and professor, co-edited a volume of his mother's letters, published as "Zieht den Trennungsstrich, jede Minute" (2005).[^6]

### Berlin and the student movement

In 1965 the couple went to West [[Berlin]]. During the 1965 Bundestag campaign Ensslin worked through the [[Wahlkontor Deutscher Schriftsteller]] for the [[Social Democratic Party of Germany]] (SPD), compiling campaign material in an unused office of [[Egon Bahr]] while Vesper wrote speeches for the Berlin senator [[Karl Schiller]].[^5] The formation of the Grand Coalition in 1966 was, in her father's description, "the great shock" for the campaign volunteers. Ensslin later said that the SPD leaders were "prisoners of the system," who had to take account of the economic and extra-parliamentary powers in the background.[^5] From 1967 she took part in the [[Außerparlamentarische Opposition]] (APO).[^1] She increasingly characterized West German society as irredeemably fascist and conventional political opposition as futile.[^7] She and Baader first met in the autumn of 1967.[^3]

The killing of [[Benno Ohnesorg]] at a demonstration in Berlin on June 2, 1967, against the visit of the [[Shah of Iran]] is the event the Neue Deutsche Biographie names as the start of her rapid radicalization.[^1] The judgment identifies the shooter as the policeman [[Karl-Heinz Kurras]], who shot Ohnesorg in a courtyard.[^8] Kurras was acquitted in November 1967 and again at a retrial in 1970. Reporting on the Stasi records in May 2009, [[Time]] said that Kurras had been "an active Stasi agent" who spied for the Stasi "under the cover name of Otto Bohl," and that the Stasi file described the shooting as an "unlucky accident."[^9] After Ohnesorg's death she reported to the Studienstiftung that apart from her child the months since June had been almost entirely taken up by events at the university and in West Berlin, and that she had taken part "actively in numerous actions."[^5]

On the evening of June 2, 1967, according to the account [[Stefan Aust]] gave in his book on the RAF, a group of shocked demonstrators met at the SDS centre on the [[Kurfürstendamm]] and argued about the shooting. In the discussion, Ensslin said: "This is the generation of Auschwitz; one cannot argue with them."[^10][^11] The 2008 article by [[Christian Schneider]] in [[Vorgänge]] repeats this version under Aust's name and notes that Ensslin's authorship of the statement is now disputed, while the fear that the argument expressed is not in dispute.[^10]

### The Frankfurt arsons

On April 2, 1968, Ensslin, Baader, [[Thorwald Proll]] and [[Horst Söhnlein]] set fires in two Frankfurt department stores, the Kaufhof on the Zeil and the M. Schneider store.[^1][^12] The judgment records considerable property damage and no injuries, and records that the arsons were motivated by a campaign of [[Kommune 1]], whose members used a 1967 fire in a [[Brussels]] department store, which killed more than 200 people, to criticize the [[Vietnam War]].[^12] The trial at the [[Frankfurt Regional Court]] opened in October 1968, with the Berlin law professor [[Ernst Heinitz]], her friend since her Berlin years, as her defence counsel. On October 31, 1968, the court sentenced each of the four to three years' Zuchthaus for endangering human life by arson.[^1][^5][^12]

Ulrike Meinhof, who reported on the trial for the magazine [[konkret]], criticized the arson in her column. She wrote that an attack of this kind on the capitalist consumer world does "not lift that consumer world off its hinges, does not even injure it."[^13] Ensslin was released from pretrial detention on June 13, 1969. The [[Federal Court of Justice]] rejected the appeal in November 1969, and she fled to [[Paris]] and Italy. She returned to West Berlin in February 1970 and lived underground.[^1] The accounts of Aust and of the historian [[Jeremy Varon]] hold that during the release pending appeal in 1969 she and Baader built the network of contacts that became the RAF.[^7]

### Underground and the liberation of Baader

On May 14, 1970, Ensslin took part in the liberation of Baader from custody in Berlin. The judgment describes the operation as carried out by a group around Ensslin and Meinhof.[^1][^2] From June to August 1970 she trained in weapons and explosives at an [[Al Fatah]] training camp in [[Jordan]]. From 1970 to 1972 she took part in bank robberies, car thefts, burglaries, explosive attacks and the group's "May offensive" (Mai-Offensive).[^1] The police who pursued the group believed that Meinhof supplied the theory while Ensslin gave practical help, arranging lodgings, managing the cash box and assisting the preparation of group operations.[^5]

### The 1972 bombings and arrest

The judgment records six explosive attacks in 1972, in [[Frankfurt]], [[Augsburg]], [[Munich]], [[Karlsruhe]], [[Hamburg]] and [[Heidelberg]], and attributes them to the three defendants as originators.[^14] On May 11, 1972, three devices exploded at the headquarters of the 5th US Corps in Frankfurt, and one person was killed. On May 24, 1972, bombs in two vehicles at the US Army headquarters in Heidelberg killed three American soldiers.[^14] The RAF declaration of May 25, 1972, justified the Heidelberg attack with the words "That is genocide, that would be the 'final solution,' that is Auschwitz"; the judgment attributes the declaration to the RAF, not to Ensslin personally.[^14]

On Wednesday, June 7, 1972, Ensslin was arrested in Hamburg. The court places her in the Linette fashion boutique on the Jungfernstieg at about 1 p.m. She had taken off her suede jacket at the back of the shop while trying on a sweater. A member of staff noticed an unusual weight in the jacket, suspected a firearm and called the police. When an officer entered the shop at about 1:30 p.m., Ensslin turned toward him and reached into the right outer pocket of her jacket for a .38 Special revolver loaded with hollow-point ammunition. The court found that she intended to shoot him and accepted that his death was likely.[^15] A police patrol car stopped near the shop at 1:26 p.m., and a second officer standing behind her seized her as she turned. Her handbag held a second pistol, a Belgian 9 mm FN, 830 marks, and an identity card in the name of Margarethe Reins together with a Hamburg address, where police then detained five members of a commune who were soon released. She was flown by police helicopter to Essen.[^5]

Der Spiegel reported that a leading investigator told it: "My impression is that she provoked her arrest herself," and that former friends of hers suspected she had "deliberately set out to be recognized." The magazine added that Baader had been arrested six days earlier with [[Holger Meins]] and [[Jan-Carl Raspe]] in Frankfurt and that her handbag contained newspaper clippings on his arrest and wounding.[^5]

The judgment quotes a note, attributed by the court to Ensslin and written in [[Essen]] prison, that describes the shop: "the idea was other clothes, a taxi into the city, covering the trail," and that otherwise "a saleswoman would now be dead (hostage)."[^16] The circumstances of her arrest, in a high-street shop, were used to mock the RAF's stated rejection of consumer society.[^17] Ensslin was held in the Essen prison until February 1974.[^18]

### Stammheim trial

The trial at Stammheim opened on May 21, 1975, before the [[Higher Regional Court Stuttgart]] (file 2 StE (OLG Stgt) 1/74) and ended with the judgment of April 28, 1977.[^1][^2] The court found the three defendants jointly guilty of three murders combined with six attempted murders, and of a further murder combined with one attempted murder. It also found them guilty of attempted murders and explosions in the other attacks. Ensslin was separately convicted of one attempted murder combined with resistance to officers. The court found that the three had formed a criminal association, and it sentenced each of them to life imprisonment.[^19] For the completed murders in Frankfurt and Heidelberg, the court imposed two life sentences.[^20]

In her statement of May 4, 1976, Ensslin accepted responsibility for the attacks without qualification. The judgment quotes her: "Put simply, we are also responsible for the attacks on the CIA headquarters and the headquarters of the 5th US Corps in Frankfurt and on the US headquarters in Heidelberg, insofar as we were organized in the RAF since 1970, fought in it and took part in shaping its politics and structure."[^21]

The court identified notes signed "Smutje" as Ensslin's and found that she organized the group's work from prison. One draft, it found, was to be put to Ulrike Meinhof and Baader for approval.[^22] Her defence counsel [[Otto Schily]] filed an appeal after the judgment.[^1] According to the reporting of the time, the Baden-Württemberg Justice Minister [[Traugott Bender]] and the Interior Minister [[Karl Schiess]] had defence conversations with RAF defendants secretly overheard in April 1975 and around the turn of 1976 to 1977, contrary to law and the constitution; Bender later called the admission of the unlawful action a "psychological-cosmetic" matter. The senate chairmanship went to Judge [[Theodor Prinzing]] in 1974 after a rotation of posts between the Higher Regional Court and the state ministry of justice.[^23]

### The last weeks of 1977

On September 30, 1977, the [[Bundestag]] passed the Contact Ban Act (Kontaktsperregesetz), which allowed authorities to cut all contact between prisoners linked to terrorist groups and the outside world, after the kidnapping of the employers' president [[Hanns Martin Schleyer]]. The anonymous 1978 leaflet on the events of October 18, 1977 reproduces the text of the law and the restrictions imposed at Stammheim.[^24]

On October 9, 1977, a [[Federal Criminal Police Office]] (BKA) official spoke with Ensslin at Stammheim. The government's documentation on the terror attacks, as described by Der Spiegel, showed that the isolated prisoner knew exactly of a seven-minute conversation the official had held with Baader the day before. In the individual talks all three prisoners told him that they wished to take the decision out of Chancellor [[Helmut Schmidt]]'s hands, "as a decision about us" (Ensslin).[^25]

Der Spiegel reported that in the days before her death she telephoned Bonn offices and tried to win the head of the [[Federal Chancellery (Germany)|Chancellery]], [[Manfred Schüler]], or the minister of state [[Hans-Jürgen Wischnewski]], for a "political conversation" in the prison, without result. On the evening before her death she received the two prison chaplains, evangelical and Catholic, for a conversation of an hour and a half that the clergy described as not a pastoral talk. The chaplains wrote afterwards to her parents that she had asked them to see that three letters addressed to the head of the Chancellery, kept in her cell in a folder marked "Anwalt," reached him.[^3]

According to the same report, the letters could not be found: no one at the Chancellery, the BKA, the Federal Ministry of Justice or the Stuttgart prosecutor's office said they had seen them, and a prosecutor at the scene first confirmed the seizure of a letter and then called it a mistake. Officials of the Chancellery were convinced that Ensslin had misled the chaplains to stage mysterious circumstances of death. The magazine also reported that she had written to her court-appointed counsel [[Manfred Künzel]] months before: "I am certain that we will be liquidated in the shadow of Buback's end."[^3]

### Deaths at Stammheim

On October 18, 1977, the GSG 9 unit stormed the hijacked aircraft at Mogadishu. Raspe was found with a head wound in his cell at about 7:40 a.m., during the distribution of food, and died in the Katharinenhospital at 9:40 a.m.; Baader was found dead in his cell, a pistol beside him; Ensslin was found hanged at the window grille in cell 720; [[Irmgard Möller]], the surviving defendant, was found with knife wounds and survived.[^4][^26][^27] A witness report reproduced by [[Karl-Heinz Weidenhammer]] in his 1988 documentary describes her body as "hanging dead from the window grille."[^27]

The autopsy was performed in Tübingen in the presence of three experts from Liège, Vienna and Zurich. The protocol of Ensslin's body, reproduced in the Weidenhammer documentary, records all typical signs of death by hanging: a ligature mark on the neck, asphyxial haemorrhages under the eyelid lining and the lining of the heart, and the tip of the tongue caught between the jaws. Its secondary injuries, the protocol says, "are not suspicious of the action of another's hand."[^3][^28]

Der Spiegel wrote that Ensslin was last checked at 4 p.m. on the previous afternoon, that the cell doors of the wing had been fitted with foam-rubber sound insulation, that the prison doctor, the prison director and the security officer did not enter her cell when it was opened but pronounced "death by suicide" from the threshold, and that she "possibly" was still alive. In her cell the cable on which she hung had been left behind when electrical appliances were collected after the contact ban took effect.[^3] A week later the magazine reported that, according to the prosecutors, the two guards on the floor had not, as first assumed, been in the glass "cockpit" at the end of the corridor but in the watch officers' room behind double doors on the same floor.[^23]

### Official findings and dissent

The Baden-Württemberg state government's preliminary report of October 26, 1977 stated, in section 5, that "on the basis of the investigation results to date as assessed by the public prosecutor, there is no indication that the deaths of the prisoners Baader, Ensslin and Raspe, or the injuries to the prisoner Möller, were caused by the fault of others."[^24] The [[Landtag of Baden-Württemberg]] committee of inquiry heard evidence in 19 sessions on the basis of 53 evidentiary orders, and, according to the state government's later account, came unanimously to the conviction that the prisoners had killed themselves (Landtag Drucksache 7/3200, p. 47 f.).[^29] According to Weidenhammer, the trace-evaluation report of February 28, 1978 was prepared after the Landtag committee had ended its work, and Prosecutor Christ signed the closing decision on April 18, 1978. The Neue Deutsche Biographie states that the allegation that the three deaths were not suicides "is regarded as refuted."[^1][^30]

Her death was disputed by RAF supporters on the same grounds they contested Baader's death: that the maximum-security facility made access to the means of hanging impossible without guard knowledge.[^17] The surviving prisoner Möller's criminal complaint, filed through her lawyers, quotes her: "at no time was there an agreement between Gudrun Ensslin, Jan-Karl Raspe, Andreas Baader and me to commit suicide together."[^24] Der Spiegel reported that Möller told her lawyer that around 4:30 a.m. she had heard "bangs and squeaking," called through the door to Raspe and received a calming reply, and that doctors found no sedatives in her blood and urine and found longitudinal cuts on both wrists from a round-tipped serrated knife and four stabs into the left breast.[^23] Schily, Ensslin's counsel, recalled the earlier eavesdropping affair in Stammheim, in which a "separate access" to the special floor had come into view, and said that it was "not outside the conceivable" that an intelligence service had had a hand in it this time as well.[^3]

Weidenhammer, who was defence counsel for Raspe, argues that the cord used for the hanging was a loudspeaker cable from Ensslin's cell. The trace report of February 28, 1978 stated that the two loudspeaker boxes in her cell were connected by a green electric cable which, "at least in outward appearance, also constitutes the hanging instrument." No criminal-technical comparison of the cable ends was carried out, and Weidenhammer records that the investigator's request for a prosecutorial order for one was never issued.[^31] Weidenhammer also wrote that the [[Federal Intelligence Service]] (BND) installed listening devices in the high-security wing, that the [[Bundesamt für Verfassungsschutz|Verfassungsschutz]] and the BND had worked on the seventh floor without control since March 1975, and that on the official version the prisoners would have agreed on their suicides under the supervision of the intelligence services.[^32]

Helmut Ensslin openly doubted the official account of collective suicide. He wished to preach at his daughter's funeral, and the [[Evangelical Church in Württemberg]] (Evangelischer Oberkirchenrat) did not grant the request. Minister-President [[Hans Filbinger]] pressed for only the Swabian Ensslin to be buried in Stuttgart, while Mayor [[Manfred Rommel]] permitted a common grave for the three at the [[Dornhaldenfriedhof]]. The funeral took place on October 27, 1977. According to the Stuttgarter Zeitung, the father was urged to stop making accusations about the deaths of the three prisoners.[^4] Filbinger attributed the failures at Stammheim to the federal government, the [[Federal Public Prosecutor General|Federal Prosecutor's Office]] and the lawyers; Justice Minister Bender had resigned.[^23]

### State informants in the Berlin milieu

[[Wolfgang Kraushaar]]'s 2005 book on the bomb intended for the Jewish community house in Berlin in November 1969 describes [[Peter Urbach]] as a West Berlin Verfassungsschutz informant from 1967. The book describes Urbach as a locksmith and agent provocateur who supplied militant circles of the APO with weapons and drugs of every kind. Kraushaar argues that the bomb came from the state office's stocks. In the book, Urbach, asked about the role of the state, told the Kommune member [[Rainer Langhans]]: "Rainer, if you only knew."[^33]
