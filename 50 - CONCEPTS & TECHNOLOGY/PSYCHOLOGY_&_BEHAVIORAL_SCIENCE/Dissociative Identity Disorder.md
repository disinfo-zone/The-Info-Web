Dissociative identity disorder (DID) is a dissociative disorder that the DSM-5 defines by "the presence of two or more distinct personality states or an experience of possession" together with "recurrent episodes of amnesia."[^1] Before the 1994 rename the clinical literature called it [[Multiple Personality Disorder]]. The condition sits at the center of three bodies of material on this site: the Belgian [[Marc Dutroux|Dutroux]] affair and the testimony of survivors such as [[Regina Louf]], the [[Project MKUltra]] files, and the therapy cases of the 1980s and 1990s that produced the [[False Memory Syndrome]] debate. Its clinical history runs back to the nineteenth century, and its diagnostic validity remains contested in the literature.

### Nineteenth-century origins

[[Pierre Janet]] published *L'Automatisme psychologique* in Paris in 1889 through [[Félix Alcan]]. Its principal subjects were four persons whom Janet identified by the first names Léonie, Lucie, Rose and Marie.[^2] In the book Janet wrote of "le dédoublement simultané de la personnalité," the simultaneous splitting of the personality, which he presented as a little known alteration of human consciousness.[^2]

[[Morton Prince]], a professor at the [[Tufts University School of Medicine|Tufts College Medical School]], published *The Dissociation of a Personality: A Biographical Study in Abnormal Psychology* in 1906 through [[Longmans, Green and Co.]] The patient at the center of the book appears under the pseudonym Miss Beauchamp, and her secondary personality, named Sally, is the subject of much of the text. Prince wrote that "the disintegration resulting in multiple personality is only a functional dissociation of that complex organization which constitutes a normal self."[^3] He proposed that the material of the secondary group could be called a co-consciousness: "Sally may therefore be termed a co-consciousness, or a subconsciousness."[^4]

### Diagnostic history

The fourth edition of the [[Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders]], published in 1994 by the [[American Psychiatric Association]], renamed the condition from multiple personality disorder to dissociative identity disorder.[^5] The third edition (1980) had listed multiple personality among its dissociative disorders, together with psychogenic amnesia, psychogenic fugue, depersonalization disorder and atypical dissociative disorder.[^6] The DSM-5 places it among the dissociative disorders and gives its criteria in two parts: the identity disruption described above, and recurrent amnesia.[^1] The same section lists "dissociative states secondary to brainwashing or thought reform" among the examples of other specified dissociative disorder.[^7] A separate passage in the DSM-5 section on conditions for further study describes an "identity disturbance due to prolonged and intense coercive persuasion," with brainwashing, thought reform, and recruitment by sects or terror organizations among its examples.[^8]

### Early clinical studies and the Chicago cases

A 1986 study by [[Frank W. Putnam]] and colleagues reviewed 100 recent cases in which clinicians completed a 386-item questionnaire about patients treated for multiple personality disorder. The authors reported "a clinical syndrome characterized by a core of depressive and dissociative symptoms and a childhood history of significant trauma, primarily child abuse."[^9]

[[Bennett Braun]], a psychiatrist at [[Rush-Presbyterian-St. Luke's Medical Center]] in [[Chicago]], treated patients for multiple personality disorder. The [[American Bar Association|ABA Journal]] reports that two patients sued Braun and the hospital after his treatment led them to believe in memories of satanic ritual abuse, and that one case settled for $10 million and the other for $7.5 million. Rush did not admit wrongdoing as part of either settlement. According to the same article, Braun lost his medical license for two years after hearings before the Illinois Department of Professional Regulation, and he died in March 2024 at the age of 83.[^10]

The FMSF Newsletter of December 1997 reported, citing a front-page story by Pam Belluck in [[The New York Times]] of November 6, 1997, that the family in *Burgus v. Braun* (Circuit Court of Cook County, Illinois, Nos. 91L8493) accepted $10.6 million on the day trial was expected to begin, six years after filing. The newsletter gave the division as $3.5 million from Rush-Presbyterian-St. Luke's, based on charges concerning the treatment of the patient's children, and $7.3 million from Braun and the psychiatrist [[Elva Poznanski]]. It reported that the defendants made no admission of negligence, and that Braun called the settlement a "travesty" reached over his objections and said any hypnosis he used was for pain control.[^11] The psychologist and lawyer [[R. Christopher Barden]], one of the plaintiffs' attorneys, wrote in [[Psychiatric Times]] in 2014 that a family accepted a $10.6 million settlement for treatment with Braun in November 1997.[^12] The ABA Journal gives $10 million for the same case.

The FMSF Newsletter of January/February 1998 listed the closing of the Rush-Presbyterian dissociative disorders unit among the events of the preceding months.[^13] The December 1999 issue reported that Braun surrendered his license for two years in a plea agreement with the Illinois Department of Professional Regulation on a nine-count complaint that included implanting memories of satanic ritual abuse, with a five-year probation if he returned to practice, and that 11 former patients had sued him.[^14]

### Sybil

[[Flora Rheta Schreiber]]'s 1973 book *Sybil* was a widely read account of a patient treated by the psychiatrist [[Cornelia Wilbur]].[^15] In 2011 the journalist [[Debbie Nathan]] published *Sybil Exposed* with the [[Free Press]], which argued that the account was substantially fabricated.[^16] [[Laura Miller]], reviewing the book in [[Salon]], described Wilbur's treatment as a heavy regimen of drugs and electroshock, followed by hypnosis, and wrote that the patient had sent her doctor a letter saying the multiple personality material was invented, which Wilbur treated as a defensive maneuver.[^17]

### The false memory debate and the FMSF

The [[False Memory Syndrome Foundation]] (FMSF) was formed in 1992 by [[Pamela Freyd]] and [[Peter Freyd]], according to a 2020 history in [[International Society for the Study of Trauma and Dissociation|ISSTD]] News by [[Kate McMaugh]] and [[Warwick Middleton]].[^18] The article lists the foundation's Scientific and Professional Advisory Board as including [[Ralph Underwager]], [[Hollida Wakefield]], [[Aaron T. Beck]], [[Elizabeth Loftus]], [[James Randi]], [[John Hochman]], [[Rosalind Cartwright]] and [[Jennifer Freyd]]. It states that Underwager was forced to resign from the board after a *Paidika* interview he gave with his wife, and that the foundation announced its closure at the end of 2019.[^18]

The foundation's own newsletter printed the board in each issue. The list dated December 1, 1997 includes [[Martin Orne]], [[Emily Carota Orne]], [[David F. Dinges]] and Rosalind Cartwright, the last with the affiliation Rush Presbyterian St. Luke's Medical Center, Chicago.[^19] The March/April 2000 issue reported Martin Orne's death on February 11, 2000, called him a member of the Scientific Advisory Board, and stated that he and his wife Emily had been instrumental in founding the foundation and had conceived the professional advisory board; it recorded that in 1991 he had brought together several families who had turned to him.[^20] An issue of February 5, 1993 thanked "members of the FMSF Advisory Board, M. Orne and E. Loftus" for comments.[^21]

The 1984 paper by Martin Orne, David F. Dinges and Emily Carota Orne addressed the differential diagnosis of multiple personality in the forensic context.[^22] The 2000 newsletter also reported that in the Hillside Strangler case Orne demonstrated that [[Kenneth Bianchi]] was feigning multiple personalities.[^20]

### Belgian cases

The writer [[S. Dovey]] wrote that Louf was publicly discredited as a fantasist who suffered from false memory syndrome after her identity as X1 was leaked to the press.[^23] Louf gave up her anonymity as witness X1 in January 1998.[^24] Dovey wrote that Louf's accounts described alternative personality states emerging through prolonged abuse, and that the X-witness accounts were dismissed as a hoax.[^23] Dovey also wrote that a media narrative built around Dutroux as a lone serial killer led to the X-witness accounts being dismissed, and that the X witnesses were prevented from testifying at the trials of Dutroux and [[Jean-Michel Nihoul]] in 2004.[^23]

The Dutch broadcaster [[Zembla]] reported on March 11, 2004 that most media had depicted Louf as a disturbed fantasist, as the Belgian judiciary had also done. The same programme reported that the Belgian gendarmerie had been able to confirm much of what witness X1 said.[^25]

Dovey wrote that both DID and false memory syndrome explanations agree that [[Hypnotism|hypnotism]] can be used to access repressed memories, or implant false ones, within splits of the human personality. Dovey wrote that proponents of the false memory explanation argued that recovered memories required validation with real world evidence, while advocates for survivors pointed to corroborating evidence gathered for the X-witness accounts.[^23] Dovey wrote that the dismissal of DID as an explanatory framework allowed the investigation to focus on individual pathology rather than on the organized networks that survivors had described.[^23] Dovey wrote that the clinical literature distinguishes trauma-induced dissociation from iatrogenic dissociation, and that the cases may have involved both, with organized abuse networks inducing dissociative states and later therapy shaping the content of recovered memories.[^23]

### Intelligence research and dissociation

Three [[Central Intelligence Agency|CIA]] documents of August 1961 concern MKUltra Subproject 136. A memorandum to the Comptroller dated August 23, 1961 states that the subproject "has been approved" and that "$8,579.00 of the over-all Project MKULTRA funds have been obligated," under the authority of the Director of Central Intelligence memorandum of April 13, 1953.[^26] A memorandum for the record of the same date gives the purpose as support for an investigator's research on an "Experimental Analysis of Extrasensory Perception," which would move beyond whether extrasensory perception exists to its relation to personality factors and its use "as a method of communication." The investigator's name is redacted, and the memorandum states that the funds would be passed through a redacted organization for security and cover purposes.[^27] The attached proposal states: "In working with individual subjects, special attention will be given to disassociative states, which tend to accompany spontaneous ESP experiences. Such states can be induced and controlled to some extent with hypnosis and drugs." It adds that "The experimenters will be particularly interested in disassociative states, from the abaissement de niveau mental to multiple personality in so-called mediums, and an attempt will be made to induce a number of states of this kind, using hypnosis," and that "drugs and psychological tricks will be used to modify his attitudes." Its data sources include "special groups such as psychotics, children and mediums."[^28]

A statement of January 20, 1964 from the redacted recipient itemized $8,579 received for 1961-1962, and an accounting form for September 1, 1961 to January 20, 1964 was approved on March 10, 1964 over the name of [[Sidney Gottlieb]] of the Technical Services Division.[^29]

The writer S. Dovey wrote that Subproject 136 sought to induce dissociative states in children through drugs and hypnosis for the purpose of creating multiple personalities, and that researchers of the period understood that trauma could produce similar dissociative states in children, which could be compartmentalized and activated independently, and that this understanding formed the basis for experiments applying chemical and psychological methods to behavioral modification.[^23] The 1961 documents describe inducing dissociative states and multiple personality in mediums with hypnosis and drugs, and list children among the groups whose existing data would be analyzed.[^28]

A CIA proposal titled "Studies in the Military Application of Hypnotism" is undated in the reading room copy. It describes twenty somnambulists drawn from a military setting, a message that only one hypnotist could recover, and a budget of approximately $10,000. The proposal states: "In deep hypnotism or somnambulism, the subject has complete amnesia; he remembers nothing of what has happened."[^30] A CIA report dated October 25, 1955 separates the use of hypnosis with "witting, willing subjects," for purposes such as age regression, cover story build-up and post-hypnotic suggestion, from its use with unwitting and unwilling subjects.[^31]

A 1951 CIA progress report on [[Project Bluebird]] refers to meetings of May and June 1951 and lists the appointment of a coordinator, the establishment of a research panel, and the planned development of research projects.[^32]

The 1980s and 1990s therapy controversy shares its subject with these programs. [[Cathy O'Brien]] described claims of trauma-based programming under [[Project Monarch]] in her 1995 book *Trance Formation of America*, written with [[Mark Phillips]].[^33] Colin A. Ross published *The CIA Doctors: Human Rights Violations by American Psychiatrists* in 2006 through [[Manitou Communications]].[^34]

### Ritual abuse and the FBI

In 1992 the [[Federal Bureau of Investigation]] published *Investigator's Guide to Allegations of "Ritual" Child Abuse* by [[Kenneth V. Lanning]], a supervisory special agent in the Behavioral Science Unit. Lanning wrote that when he first heard these allegations in 1983 and 1984 he tended to believe them, and that the number of cases then began to grow, with "hundreds of victims alleging that thousands of offenders are abusing" people as part of organized satanic cults, "and there is little or no corroborative evidence."[^35] He wrote that in February 1985 the FBI Academy hosted the first national seminar on "these kinds of cases."[^36]

Lanning described the adult survivor cases in terms that bear on dissociative diagnosis: "They are frequently hypnotized, intentionally or unintentionally, as part of the therapy and are often diagnosed as suffering from Multiple Personality Disorder."[^37]

### Critiques of the diagnosis

[[Nicholas Spanos]]'s 1996 book *Multiple Identities & False Memories: A Sociocognitive Perspective* was published by the [[American Psychological Association]]. Its contents include hypnosis, false memory, seduction theory, spirit possession across cultures, and a social learning account of multiple personality disorder.[^38]

In 1999 [[Scott O. Lilienfeld]], [[Steven Jay Lynn]] and colleagues published in [[Psychological Bulletin]] a reply to [[David Gleaves]]'s 1996 critique of the sociocognitive model, which had argued for the posttraumatic model. They wrote that "the treatment literature suggests that iatrogenic factors play an important role in the etiology of DID," and that the evidence linking child abuse to DID was more problematic than Gleaves had implied.[^39] In 2022 Lynn and colleagues wrote that "neither perspective provides a satisfactory account" of dissociation, and proposed a transtheoretical framework that treats dissociative disorders as failures of normally adaptive systems.[^40]

### Clinical research and diagnostic validation

In 2003 A. A. Reinders and colleagues used functional neuroimaging to study patients with DID in two identity states. They reported "specific changes in localized brain activity consistent with their ability to generate at least two distinct mental states of self-awareness," involving the medial prefrontal cortex and the posterior associative cortices.[^41]

A 2012 study by Reinders and colleagues compared 11 DID patients with 18 simulating controls, 10 high in fantasy proneness and 8 low. The authors wrote that the identity states "were not convincingly enacted by DID simulating controls," and concluded that the findings "indicate that DID does not have a sociocultural (e.g., iatrogenic) origin."[^42] The paper describes "a competing view" holding that DID is due to fantasy proneness, suggestibility, suggestion and role-playing.[^42]

A 2019 study by Reinders and colleagues applied pattern recognition to structural brain images of 75 participants, 32 with DID and 43 controls. The classifiers discriminated between the two groups with 72 percent sensitivity and 74 percent specificity.[^43]

The 2016 paper by [[Bethany L. Brand]] and colleagues in the [[Harvard Review of Psychiatry]] listed six beliefs about DID: that it is a fad, that it is primarily diagnosed in North America by experts who overdiagnose it, that it is rare, that it is iatrogenic rather than trauma-based, that it is the same entity as borderline personality disorder, and that treatment is harmful. The authors wrote that the absence of research to substantiate these beliefs, and the existence of research that refutes them, "confirms their mythical status."[^44] The ISSTD published the third revision of its treatment guidelines for DID in 2011.[^45]
