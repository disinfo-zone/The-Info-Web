[[Synchronicity]] is the term [[Carl Jung]] used for "a coincidence in time of two or more causally unrelated events which have the same or a similar meaning," set against "synchronism," which he defined as the simultaneous occurrence of two events.[^1] In the essay that gave the concept its fullest statement, Jung wrote that synchronicity means "the simultaneous occurrence of a certain psychic state with one or more external events which appear as meaningful parallels to the momentary subjective state," and, in certain cases, the reverse.[^1] He tied these phenomena to the archetypes, which he called "formal factors responsible for the organization of unconscious psychic processes," and he described the unconscious that produces them as "psychoid," a psyche "identical with itself in all individuals."[^1]

### Kammerer and the 1930 Wilhelm address

Jung wrote that "the problem of synchronicity has puzzled me for a long time, ever since the middle twenties."[^1] Among the earlier works he cited is [[Paul Kammerer]]'s *Das Gesetz der Serie: Eine Lehre von den Wiederholungen im Lebens- und Weltgeschehen* (Stuttgart and Berlin: [[Deutsche Verlags-Anstalt]], 1919).[^2] Jung wrote that Kammerer had dealt, "not altogether convincingly," with the question of the "countereffect of the succeeding state on the preceding one."[^3]

Jung's own first public use of the term came in a memorial address for the sinologist [[Richard Wilhelm]], delivered in [[Munich]] on May 10, 1930. The address was later printed as an appendix to the English edition of *[[The Secret of the Golden Flower]]* (London and New York, 1931), where Jung wrote: "The science of the I Ching is not based on the causality principle, but on a principle (hitherto unnamed because not met with among us) which I have tentatively called the synchronistic principle."[^4]

### Koestler and Thompson

[[Arthur Koestler]] published *[[The Roots of Coincidence]]* in 1972 (New York: [[Random House]]).[^5] [[Annie Jacobsen]] wrote that the book explored Jung's concepts and their possible connection to [[Extrasensory Perception|ESP]] and [[Psychokinesis]], and that [[Edmund Thompson]], the Army's assistant chief of staff for intelligence, developed an interest in psychic research after reading it.[^6] Jacobsen describes synchronicity as differing from mere coincidence in that it carries a profound personal meaning for the individual experiencing it, often feeling like a sign or a message, and as suggesting a connection between the inner and outer worlds that goes beyond conventional cause and effect, hinting at a collective unconscious or a universal field of information.[^6] In her account Jung believed that synchronicity was an acausal connecting principle, a phenomenon where events in the external world mirror an individual's internal psychological state, suggesting a deeper, underlying order to the universe.[^6]

### Volumes of 1952 and 1955

The essay first appeared in German as "Synchronizität als ein Prinzip akausaler Zusammenhänge," printed together with a monograph by [[Wolfgang Pauli]] in *Naturerklärung und Psyche* (Studien aus dem C. G. Jung-Institut, IV; [[Zürich]]: [[Rascher Verlag]], 1952).[^7] A shorter version, "Über Synchronizität," was printed in the [[Eranos]] yearbook for 1951.[^7]

The English edition, *The Interpretation of Nature and the Psyche*, was published as [[Bollingen Series]] LI in 1955 by [[Pantheon Books]] for the [[Bollingen Foundation]]. Pauli's contribution is his essay "The Influence of Archetypal Ideas on the Scientific Theories of Kepler."[^8] Jung's foreword is dated August 1950 and thanks [[Liliane Frey-Rohn]] "for her help with the astrological material."[^9]

### The Bollingen Foundation

[[Paul Mellon]] enlisted as an Army private when the United States entered the war, joined the cavalry, and rose to the rank of major in the [[Office of Strategic Services]], according to the obituary distributed in February 1999.[^10] The [[Frick Collection]] research directory gives his Army service as 1941 to 1943 and his work overseas with the Office of Strategic Services as 1943 to 1945, and the [[Yale Center for British Art]] states that after two years at [[Fort Riley]], Kansas, he served in the Office of Strategic Services in England.[^11] The obituary calls Carl Jung his friend and former analyst, and states that he paid for the publication of Jung's collected works.[^10] The *Public Domain Review* essay on his collecting of [[William Blake]] states that wartime service "forced an end to the relationship with Jung."[^12] The same essay describes an earlier scheme, from early 1945, "for Mellon to enlist Jung's help with psychological propaganda against the retreating German army." Germany's surrender made the mission redundant, and Mellon was back in [[Virginia]] by August 1945.[^13]

The essay states that "almost immediately" afterward the Mellons established the Bollingen Foundation, under the direction of their friend [[Jack Barrett]], with the goal of publishing Jung's collected works in English translation.[^13] Paul and [[Mary Mellon]] had returned hastily to the United States shortly before the occupation of Denmark, Norway and France in May.[^14] Mellon also established the Bollingen Prize for poetry, the first of which went to [[Ezra Pound]] while Pound was under indictment for treason.[^10] The 1955 volume states on its title leaf that it is the fifty-first in a series of works "sponsored by and published for Bollingen Foundation."[^7]

### The astrological marriage study

Chapter two of the essay describes a set of marriage horoscopes. Jung wrote that they were "obtained from friendly donors in Zurich, London, Rome, and Vienna," and that "the sample was a random one."[^15] The first batch of 180 married pairs was used as a pilot. The study grew to 483 marriages, or 966 horoscopes, in three batches of 180, 220 and 83.[^16] Jung compared the 180 married pairs with the 32,220 unmarried pairs formed by pairing each husband with each woman he was not married to, and he tabulated the conjunctions and oppositions among the planets and the ascendant and descendant for both groups.[^16]

Jung's own conclusion, in the essay, was: "For the present, at any rate, we have insufficient grounds for believing that the astrological results are more than mere chance, or that statistics involving large numbers yield a statistically significant result."[^17] He reported that "calculations undertaken by a mathematician showed unmistakably that the average frequency of 10% in the first batch and subsequently in all three batches is far from representing a significant figure," and that "there is no ground for assuming that our maximum frequencies are more than mere dispersions due to chance."[^18] He also recorded an astrologer's objection that his statistical method was "too arbitrary and too clumsy" to evaluate the aspects of marriage.[^19]

Jung then argued that a chance result could still be meaningful: "It is nothing but a chance result from the statistical point of view, yet it is meaningful on account of the fact that it looks as if it validated this expectation. It is just what I call a synchronistic phenomenon."[^20]

### Rhine's ESP data in the essay

Jung wrote that "decisive evidence for the existence of acausal combinations of events has been furnished, with adequate scientific safeguards, only very recently, mainly through the experiments of [[J. B. Rhine]] and his fellow-workers."[^21] He cited Rhine's *Extra-Sensory Perception* (Boston, 1934) and *New Frontiers of the Mind* (New York, 1937), along with the [[Journal of Parapsychology]] paper by Rhine and Betty Humphrey on a transoceanic experiment.[^21]

The figures Jung reproduced include a subject who "once guessed all 25 cards correctly, which gives a probability of 1 : 298,023,223,876,953,125." When the distance was increased "in one case to 250 miles," the average was 10.1 hits for 25 cards. In another series the scores were 11.4 with experimenter and subject in one room, 9.7 in the next room and 12.0 two rooms away. Jung also cited experiments between [[Durham, North Carolina]], and [[Zagreb]], about 4,000 miles apart, and a time experiment in which "the reading of a series of cards to be turned up in the future" produced "a probability of 1 : 400,000."[^22] Jung drew from these results the conclusion that "we have no alternative but to assume that distance is psychically variable," and he described "a psychic relativity of time."[^22] He also described the PK dice experiments as positive.[^22]

Jung's note on the Rhine material records that "Professor Pauli was kind enough to draw my attention to this paper, which appeared in The Scientific Monthly (London), LXIX (1949), No. 2."[^23]

### Pauli and the 1948 letter

Schücking's 2002 review of the letters edition, *[[Atom and Archetype]]*, gives 80 letters between Pauli and Jung from 1932 to 1958, 39 of them by Pauli.[^24] The review also describes an unpublished essay, "Modern Examples of Background Physics" ("Hintergrundsphysik"), found with the correspondence, which it says "addresses itself to the principal theme of their exchanges." Schücking wrote: "What Pauli had in mind was a description of nature integrating both physics and psyche."[^24]

In a letter to Jung dated June 16, 1948, from [[Zollikon]], Pauli wrote: "When that amusing 'Pauli effect' of the overturned vase occurred, on the occasion of the founding of the [[C. G. Jung Institute]], I had the immediate and vivid impression that I should 'pour out water inside'."[^25] For the incident see [[Pauli Effect]].

### Remote viewing and the Stargate literature

In 1976 [[Hal Puthoff]] and [[Russell Targ]] published "A Perceptual Channel for Information Transfer over Kilometer Distances: Historical Perspective and Recent Research" in the [[Proceedings of the IEEE]]. They wrote: "Currently, we have no precise model of this spatial and temporal remote-viewing phenomenon. However, models of the universe involving higher order synchronicity or correlation have been proposed by the physicist Pauli and the psychologist Carl Jung."[^26] A copy is held in the [[Central Intelligence Agency]]'s document series on the [[Stargate Project]].[^26]

In August 1974 the [[Parapsychology Foundation]] held its 23rd annual conference at the Hotel La Réserve in [[Geneva]], on the subject "Quantum Physics and Parapsychology," which a conference report describes as "a topic selected at the instigation of Arthur Koestler." The report, dated October 28, 1974, records that experiments with gifted subjects were presented by Puthoff and Targ.[^27]

### Critics

[[Martin Gardner]], quoted in *[[Time (magazine)|Time]]* in March 1974, listed "Uri Geller, The Secret Life of Plants, telepathy, ESP, the incomplete conclusions of Koestler" and wrote that these "all seem part of a new uncritical enthusiasm for pseudo science." In Gardner's words, "The claims are immense, the proof nonexistent."[^28]

In 1926 [[Gladwyn K. Noble]], of the [[American Museum of Natural History]], published a letter in *[[Nature]]* arguing that the nuptial pads of the midwife toads in Paul Kammerer's experiments had been altered, and finding artificial black ink in them.[^29] The Embryo Project account of Kammerer's work reports that he died by suicide that year, and that [[William Bateson]] had criticized the experiments in *Nature* in 1919 and 1923.[^29]

### Later research

[[Harald Atmanspacher]] and [[Wolfgang Fach]] published "A structural-phenomenological typology of mind-matter correlations" in the [[Journal of Analytical Psychology]] in 2013 (vol. 58, no. 2, pages 219 to 244). Their abstract presents a typology of mind-matter correlations within a dual-aspect monist framework that they attribute to Pauli and Jung, in which the mental and the material arise as two complementary aspects of one underlying psychophysically neutral reality, and states that the typology agrees with an empirically based classification of mind-matter correlations observed in exceptional human experiences.[^30]
