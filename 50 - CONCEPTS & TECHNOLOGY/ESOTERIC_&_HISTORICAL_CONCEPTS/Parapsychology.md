[[Parapsychology]] is the scientific study of psi, the claimed faculties of [[Extrasensory Perception|extrasensory perception]] (ESP) and [[Psychokinesis|psychokinesis]] (PK). Its institutions descend from the psychical research societies of the 1880s. From the early 1970s much of its record is that of government programs which paid for psi research, tested it, and in 1995 closed the [[Stargate Project|Star Gate]] program.

### Psychical research before 1900

Mesmeric trance, and the psi abilities observed in hypnotized subjects, drew scientific attention from the late eighteenth century. The nineteenth century brought a craze over "spiritism" and "spiritualism," in which mediums claimed to communicate with the dead, and with it considerable psychological and psychiatric research into those claims.[^1]

The [[Society for Psychical Research]] was formed in London in 1882. A preliminary conference convened by [[William Fletcher Barrett|Professor Barrett]] met on January 5 and 6, 1882, and a committee of sixteen was appointed. The society was constituted on February 20, 1882, under the presidency of [[Henry Sidgwick]], and its first council meeting was held on March 3, 1882. In a presidential address of April 24, 1885, delivered after Sidgwick's resignation by his successor [[Balfour Stewart]], Barrett was called "our founder," and the society's membership was given as 150 at the end of 1882, 288 at the end of 1883, 520 at the end of 1884 and 586 at the time of the address.[^2] Sir William Fletcher Barrett (1844 to 1925) was a vice-president of the society in December 1885, listed as of the Royal College of Science, Dublin.[^3][^4]

The [[American Society for Psychical Research]] followed. At a meeting in Boston on September 23, 1884, the question of forming a society was placed in the hands of a committee of nine: [[G. Stanley Hall]], [[Edward C. Pickering]], [[Henry P. Bowditch]], [[Charles S. Minot]], [[Samuel H. Scudder]], [[Alpheus Hyatt]], [[William James]], [[William Watson]] and N. D. C. Hodges. The committee met in October and November and invited scientific men throughout the country to join, and about eighty replied favorably. The first meeting of the society was held in Boston on December 18, 1884, when a council was elected that included Hall, [[George S. Fullerton]], James, Pickering and [[Simon Newcomb]]. The organization was completed at an adjourned meeting on January 8, 1885, and Newcomb was the president for 1884-85. The society's first circular records that the Boston meeting appointed a committee with full powers "for the purpose of considering the advisability of forming a Society for Psychical Research in America," and describes the English society as "formed in the beginning of 1882, for the purpose of making an organized and systematic attempt to investigate that large group of debatable phenomena designated by such terms as 'mesmeric,' 'psychical,' and 'spiritualistic.'"[^5]

The Geneva psychologist [[Theodore Flournoy]] studied the medium [[Helene Smith|Hélène Smith]] and published *From India to the Planet Mars* in 1900, with an English translation issued the same year in New York and London.[^6] Flournoy used [[Cryptomnesia|cryptomnesia]] for the return of memories buried beneath the waking state, and applied the explanation to Smith's Hindu-language material as a question of memories from her present life. He wrote that he saw "nothing of the supernormal in that," while adding that he had not yet found the key to the enigma.[^6][^7]

### Rhine at Duke, 1930 to 1965

[[J. B. Rhine]] began card-guessing experiments for ESP at [[Duke University]] in the fall of 1930, in a program conducted under [[William McDougall]] within the psychology department, after McDougall invited Rhine and [[Louisa Rhine]] to Durham.[^8][^9] Rhine's colleague [[Karl Zener]] suggested the five symbols of the test cards used in the first series, in 1930-31, and the deck became known as [[Zener Cards]].[^8] Rhine's *[[Extra-Sensory Perception]]* was published in 1934 by the [[Boston Society for Psychic Research]], with a foreword by McDougall and an introduction by [[Walter Franklin Prince]].[^10] The work at Duke was carried on as the [[Duke University Parapsychology Laboratory]], which left the psychology department in 1950.[^11]

The *[[Journal of Parapsychology]]* began in March 1937. Its first editorial introduction traces the enterprise to a plan of some fourteen years earlier, when [[Harry Houdini]] "conspired with one of the editors of this journal to bring psychical research to the notice of the universities and to secure for it a place among their recognized fields of study."[^12] Rhine's program moved the field away from the theatrical displays of mediums toward card guessing and dice throwing, with statistical evidence as its basis.[^1] The laboratory's method rested on counting hits against chance. [[Martin Gardner]], in *[[Fads and Fallacies in the Name of Science]]* (1952), devoted a chapter to Rhine's work and judged it "an enormous self-deception," a verdict Jacobsen reports.[^7]

Money came mostly from private benefactors. The laboratory's records also list a grant from the [[Rockefeller Foundation]] from 1950 to 1954 and a contract with the [[Office of Naval Research]] from 1953 to 1959 (contract NR 160-244, under which pigeon homing research was conducted).[^13] The [[U.S. Army]] paid for a different line of work. From late 1951 the [[Engineer Research and Development Laboratories]] at [[Fort Belvoir]] funded Rhine's investigation of mine-detecting dogs, under contract DA-44-009 eng-1039, on the premise that dogs could detect landmines through ESP. Rhine's funding ended in 1953, and the contract records were destroyed in 1969 after declassification.[^14]

In 1962 Rhine founded the [[Foundation for Research on the Nature of Man]] in Durham, North Carolina. The laboratory moved to it as the Institute for Parapsychology in 1965, when Rhine retired from the Duke faculty.[^11][^13]

### The Parapsychological Association and AAAS affiliation, 1957 to 1969

The [[Parapsychological Association]] was formed at Duke on June 19, 1957, on a proposal made by Rhine at a workshop in the laboratory. It held an annual convention from 1958.[^13] [[E. Douglas Dean]], who was elected president of the association in 1966 and was its secretary in 1968 and 1969, gave an account of its attempts to affiliate with the [[American Association for the Advancement of Science]] in 2015.[^15] According to Dean, the AAAS Council of 530 delegates decided on the affiliation of societies. The association's first application, made in 1963 by its president Carroll B. Nash after the required five years of existence, was refused. Dean's 1967 application passed the AAAS committee on new affiliations in November 1967, but the AAAS board of directors, meeting on December 26, did not let it proceed to the third stage, and on December 30 a stormy controversy produced a board motion that the matter be put to a yes-or-no vote in 1968, after which the board dissolved the entire committee on new affiliations on stated grounds unrelated to the application. In 1968 the AAAS president, [[Walter Orr Roberts]], spoke for the application in the committee on council affairs, was outvoted, and the application failed at the first stage; the criteria for new affiliations were also to be rewritten. In 1969 [[Robert A. McConnell]] advised Dean to apply again, and the fourth application went in with four pounds of papers for each of the twelve scientists on the committee. It listed nine association members as AAAS fellows, though a typing error on the agenda gave four. Dean wrote that Roberts told him in December that the board had accepted the application.[^15]

Dean recorded that the AAAS Council met on December 30, 1969, in the ballroom of the Statler-Hilton Hotel in Boston, with [[Bentley Glass]] in the chair because the scheduled chairman, [[Athelstan Spilhaus]], and Roberts were absent. Item 7 combined the applications of the American Orthopsychiatric Association and the Parapsychological Association. The first was carried without discussion. On the second there was a silence before a motion and a second were heard. One delegate said that the phenomena of parapsychology did not exist and that he would vote against; a woman delegate said the members were not qualified to vote on a society they did not know. Glass stated that the committee on council affairs had concluded that the association investigated controversial or nonexistent phenomena, was open to critics and agnostics, and used scientific methods, and that nine fellows, not four, belonged to it. [[Margaret Mead]] then spoke, saying that the council had spent ten years discussing what science and scientific method are, that the association used statistical methods, blinding, placebo controls and double-blind procedures, and that the whole history of scientific progress was full of scientists who had studied phenomena the scientific establishment believed did not exist. Glass called a show of hands. Dean estimated 160 to 180 hands in favor and 30 to 35 opposed, and the motion was carried.[^15] An English typescript of Dean's account of the session is held in the STARGATE collection.[^16]

The affiliation was later contested. In 1979 [[John Archibald Wheeler]] wrote to the AAAS asking that a committee review the parapsychology section, and he recalled that "the views of our late and beloved Margaret Mead were strong in getting parapsychology admitted to the AAAS." He also described the affiliate label as giving solicitations "an air of legitimacy."[^17] [[Jim Schnabel]] wrote that the field continued to face an "informal system of scientific apartheid," in which parapsychologists were permitted to call themselves scientists but were largely kept out of the mainstream.[^1]

### Army, CIA and DIA remote viewing, 1972 to 1995

The government's psi work in the 1970s began with a contract. In 1972 the [[Central Intelligence Agency|CIA]] funded [[Stanford Research Institute]] to study [[Remote Viewing|remote viewing]], the description of distant or hidden targets by mental means. [[Russell Targ]] and [[Hal Puthoff]] ran the early work, and [[Pat Price]] was one of the subjects. The CIA's program was discontinued in 1977, and the [[Defense Intelligence Agency]] and the Army carried on under other names: Gondola Wish, which became [[Grill Flame]] in 1978, [[Center Lane]] in 1983, and [[Sun Streak]] under the DIA. Congress directed a new DIA program in the defense authorization act for fiscal 1991, which DIA named Star Gate, and the program was moved to the CIA in 1995.[^18]

The *Advanced Threat Technique Assessment*, a final report of October 1978 by Puthoff, Targ, [[Edwin C. May]] and [[Ingo Swann]], is among the SRI reports in the STARGATE collection.[^19] A Grill Flame Scientific Evaluation Committee, chaired by [[Manfred Gale]], reported in December 1979.[^20] DIA issued a research and peer review plan for the program in June 1994.[^21]

The Army's own review began in 1984, when the U.S. Army Research Institute asked the National Academy of Sciences to form a committee to examine techniques proposed to enhance human performance. The [[National Research Council]] committee, chaired by [[John A. Swets]], first met in June 1985, had fourteen members, and gathered six times over the following two years. Its parapsychology subcommittee was chaired by Ray Hyman, with Lloyd G. Humphreys as the other member and Paul Horwitz as consultant. The subcommittee visited [[Robert Jahn]]'s laboratory at [[Princeton University]] in November 1985 and [[Helmut Schmidt]]'s laboratory at the Mind Science Foundation in San Antonio in June 1986; the committee visited the laboratory of Cleve Backster in San Diego in February 1986; and the subcommittee chair also visited SRI International and attended the association's convention at Sonoma State College. Army staff who assisted the committee included Colonel [[John Alexander]].[^22]

The committee reported in 1988. Its parapsychology section concluded that "the committee finds no scientific justification from research conducted over a period of 130 years for the existence of parapsychological phenomena," and that the Army had no reason for direct involvement at that time. The committee recommended monitoring of Soviet research and of selected American laboratories, among them those of Jahn, [[Charles Honorton]], Schmidt and Edwin C. May.[^23] The report's evaluation chapter reviewed remote viewing and ended that review with the sentence "By both scientific and parapsychological standards, then, the case for remote viewing is not just very weak, but virtually nonexistent."[^24] A 1990 article by Swets and [[Robert A. Bjork]] in *[[Psychological Science]]* reported that little or no scientific evidence supported remote viewing or psychokinesis as performance techniques.[^25]

Parapsychologists replied. [[John A. Palmer]], Charles Honorton and [[Jessica Utts]], writing for the association's board, argued that "Obviously the Committee's conclusion far outstrips the scope of its investigation," and that the conclusion discouraged future research and funding.[^26]

In 1995 the CIA's Director of Research and Development in the [[Directorate of Science and Technology]] wrote in memorandum ORD-0245-95 that in late January the Executive Director had approved an approach to the congressionally directed action transferring Star Gate to the CIA; that the approach was transmitted to Congress and briefed to Richard D'Amato of the Senate Appropriations Committee staff on March 27, 1995; and that funds had been transferred to the Army Research Institute as executive agent for an NRC panel review. The memo stated that the NRC had declined on May 5, 1995, citing its 1988 report and quoting its conclusion that "the case for remote viewing is not just very weak, but virtually nonexistent." It proposed that the CIA consider going back to Congress with a recommendation not to accept the program from DIA, noted that neither the Directorate of Operations nor the Directorate of Intelligence had wanted it, and set out an alternative plan, recommended for raising at the Executive Director level, to conclude without further review that the program was not likely to be of value to the CIA. It also stated that the agency was prepared to proceed with a panel review under the [[American Institutes for Research]] instead.[^18]

The American Institutes for Research panel reported on September 29, 1995, as *An Evaluation of Remote Viewing: Research and Applications*, by [[Michael D. Mumford]], [[Andrew M. Rose]] and [[David A. Goslin]]. The panel included Utts, a statistician who had published articles viewing paranormal interpretations positively, and [[Ray Hyman]], a psychologist chosen to represent the skeptical side. Each wrote a separate review, and [[Lincoln Moses]] gave statistical advice.[^27] The panel found that a statistically significant effect had been obtained in the laboratory, and that "it is unclear whether the observed effects can unambiguously be attributed to the paranormal ability of the remote viewers." Utts argued that the results reflected psychic functioning. Hyman wrote that if her claim that methodological flaws were "soundly refuted" was correct, "I would have to agree with her bottom line that 'psychic functioning has been well established,'" but that he did not find all alternative explanations eliminated.[^27]

The operational review found that the intelligence end users valued concrete, specific information, that the remote viewing reports did not provide it, and that in no case had the information guided an intelligence operation. The authors wrote that these observations "provide a compelling argument against continuation of the program within the intelligence community."[^27]

### Soviet and Warsaw Pact programs

The Soviet record that reached the United States came largely through Defense Intelligence Agency studies. A 1975 DIA study, *Soviet and Czechoslovakian Parapsychology Research*, placed the start of the field in a 1924 initiative by [[Anatoly Lunacharsky]] for a Soviet committee on psychical research. It reported that Bekhterev's enthusiasm led to extensive work financed at the University of Leningrad Institute for Brain Research, and that [[Leonid Vasiliev]], a former student of Bekhterev, demonstrated to his own satisfaction that telepathic influence at a distance might occur. His *Experiments in Mental Suggestion* reached the West in an English translation in 1963.[^28] The same study reported that research funds were put at 20 million rubles in 1973, and "are believed to be primarily from military sources."[^28]

The 1975 study also described a 1973 article in *Questions of Philosophy* by four Moscow psychologists, which denounced "militant parapsychologists" and which the DIA read as an effort to discredit as myth any idea of a "parapsychological movement" in the Soviet Union.[^28] Vasiliev's 1960 arguments for Soviet psi research cited the telepathy reports from the [[The Nautilus (Telepathy Project)|Nautilus]], which had appeared in the French press in 1959 and 1960 and which the [[U.S. Navy]] denied.[^29]

A 1972 DIA report by [[John LaMothe|John D. LaMothe]], *Controlled Offensive Behavior: USSR*, stated that the impetus behind the Soviet effort in telepathic communication, telekinetics and bionics was said to come from "the Soviet military and the KGB," that the USSR then had twenty or more centers for parapsychological study, with an annual budget estimated in 1967 at over 12 million rubles, and that Soviet knowledge in the field was superior to that of the United States.[^30]

A DIA report of March 30, 1978, *Paraphysics R&D - Warsaw Pact* (DST-1810S-202-78), was prepared by the Foreign Technology Division of the Air Force Systems Command and approved by the DIA Directorate for Scientific and Technical Intelligence, with an information cutoff date of October 1, 1977. [[Annie Jacobsen]] wrote that [[Dale Graff]] was its author.[^7][^31] The report treated "paraphysics" as the investigation of unusual mental functioning and noted that the Soviet term "psychoenergetics" had been gaining popularity. It rated the telepathy experiments of I. M. Kogan, begun in 1965, as the best documented and controlled: in a 1967 series attested by at least five members of the Popov Society, 105 of 135 randomly selected digits were received correctly, a result the report called the most statistically significant ever reported in the psychic research literature. It identified the Kazakh State University in Alma-Ata as in all probability the largest Soviet center, with at least 20 researchers, and gave Table III totals of 75 cities, 213 facilities and 657 persons for Warsaw Pact paraphysics research.[^31]

On Soviet funding the 1978 report recorded that an estimate of 12 to 20 million rubles had survived for at least a decade without a known derivation, that the RAND Corporation had found "prima facie evidence that the figure of 12 to 20 million rubles is, if anything, grossly inflated," and that the study identified a considerably larger number of persons than RAND's analysts had known and had no reason to disagree with their assessment. It stated that official support was highly variable and that the total effort had never reached the inflated levels sometimes claimed.[^31]

### China

A SECRET report in the STARGATE collection lists in its contents a section on a China psychoenergetics tour, with subsections on the Institute of High Energy Physics in Beijing, a Shanghai meeting with the editorial staff of *Ziran Zazhi* (Nature Journal) and the representation of the People's Republic of China at the Cambridge conference.[^32] A chronology of Chinese research, also in the collection, gives the sequence in which the work moved from open publication to restriction.[^33] Research on exceptional human body functions began in 1978 with a twelve-year-old boy in Sichuan, Tang Yu. *Sichuan Daily* reported on March 11, 1979 that he could "read" with his ears; in May 1979 *People's Daily* criticized the report as unscientific, and in September 1979 *Ziran Zazhi* carried a report confirming popular accounts of nonvisual pattern recognition. A parapsychology conference was held in Shanghai in February 1980 with participants from over twenty institutes. In October 1981 an American and Canadian group organized by [[Stanley Krippner]] visited the People's Republic of China.[^33]

The sequence reversed in 1982. On February 24 the Chinese Academy of Sciences held a public hearing in Beijing, attended by 4,000 scholars, which considered most of the cases unfounded. A joint trial at Beijing Teacher's College in April gave mostly negative results, with the exception of those of the subject [[Zhang Baosheng]]. On May 13, 1982, after both sides appealed to the Party, the decision was announced by [[Hu Yaobang]], the Party's general secretary: exceptional functions of the human body were rejected as an approved subject of scientific research, a minority group of scientists was to be allowed to continue and report in an internally circulated publication, the media were asked not to publish on the topic, and formal university research and government funding were halted. In 1983 nineteen researchers led by Lin Shuhuang of Beijing Teacher's College ran PK experiments with Zhang Baosheng. In 1984 Zhang became a full-time subject of the military-run psi team at the 507 Institute, the Institute of Space Medico-Engineering in Beijing, where the chronology records that psi research was official and funded. In 1987 the Institute's team produced a film of Zhang as a PK subject which was awarded a second-class Scientific Research Achievement Prize by the Spacefight Department.[^33] In a March 1985 interview in *Psi Research*, Paul Dong told Larissa Vilenskaya that the government had ordered scientists not to refer publicly to their work.[^33]

At the 1985 convention of the Parapsychological Association, H. E. Puthoff of SRI reported an attempt to repeat Chinese experiments, which succeeded in part with a smaller magnitude than the Chinese results. A May 1985 final report, *Photon Production (Chinese Replication)*, examined the claim that light is emitted near correctly identified remote viewing targets. Across 22 sessions with four viewers it recorded a significant correlation (p < 0.035), with a signal-to-noise ratio far below the 100 to 1000 to 1 reported by the Chinese.[^34]

### Academic chairs and teaching

The [[University of Edinburgh]] holds a chair in parapsychology endowed by the bequest of [[Arthur Koestler]]. The Koestler Parapsychology Unit in the university's psychology department gives 1985 as its year of establishment, and [[Robert L. Morris]] was appointed the first holder of the Koestler Chair in 1985.[^35][^36] A paper reproduced in the DIA's 1994 peer review plan gives Morris's affiliation as occupying the chair in the Department of Psychology at Edinburgh.[^37] [[James E. Alcock|Alcock]] described the chair as "endowed by a bequest from the late Arthur Koestler, a long-time supporter of parapsychology."[^38]

By 1987 the critic James E. Alcock of [[Glendon College]] counted courses in parapsychology for academic credit at about fifty colleges and universities in the United States. He added that Ph.D.s had been awarded for parapsychological research at Cambridge, Edinburgh, Surrey, Purdue, the University of the Witwatersrand and the City University of New York.[^38] At the [[New School for Social Research]], the spring 1975 bulletin listed a course in experimental parapsychology and a course titled "Paramechanics," taught by [[Gilbert Roller]].[^39]

### Dissent

Alcock's critique, published in *Behavioral and Brain Sciences* in 1987, argued that "parapsychologists have clearly failed to produce a single reliable demonstration of 'paranormal,' or 'psi,' phenomena," and that the controlled character of the experiments "does not by itself make a science."[^38] Hyman and Honorton debated the ganzfeld results at the association's 1982 convention. Their arguments appeared in the *Journal of Parapsychology* in March 1985, and a joint article followed in December 1986.[^40]
