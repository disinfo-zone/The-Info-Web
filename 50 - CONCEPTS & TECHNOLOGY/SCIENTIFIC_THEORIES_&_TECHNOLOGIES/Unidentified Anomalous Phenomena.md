Unidentified anomalous phenomena (UAP) is the term used in United States federal law since December 23, 2022 for three classes of objects: airborne objects that are not immediately identifiable; transmedium objects or devices; and submerged objects or devices that are not immediately identifiable and display behavior or performance characteristics suggesting a relation to the airborne objects. It succeeded "unidentified aerial phenomena," the term of the 2021 [[Office of the Director of National Intelligence]] preliminary assessment, and the older "unidentified flying objects" and "flying saucers."[^1][^2]

### CIA Scientific Advisory Panel, January 1953

A panel convened by the [[Central Intelligence Agency|CIA]] Office of Scientific Intelligence met January 14 to 18, 1953 (the [[Robertson Panel]]). Its members were [[H. P. Robertson]] of the California Institute of Technology (chairman), [[Luis W. Alvarez]] of the University of California, [[Lloyd V. Berkner]] of Associated Universities, [[Samuel Goudsmit]] of Brookhaven National Laboratory and [[Thornton Page]] of Johns Hopkins University; its associate members were [[J. Allen Hynek]] of Ohio State University and Frederick C. Durant of Arthur D. Little, Inc. Its signed report of January 17, 1953 concluded that the evidence showed "no indication that these phenomena constitute a direct physical threat to national security," that there was no "residuum of cases" attributable to hostile foreign artifacts, and recommended that national security agencies "strip the Unidentified Flying Objects of the special status they have been given and the aura of mystery they have acquired" through a program "designed to reassure the public."[^3]

The longer report of the meetings described the proposed educational program as having "two major aims: training and 'debunking,'" the second to reduce "public interest in 'flying saucers'" through "mass media such as television, motion pictures, and popular articles," and to reduce "the current gullibility of the public and consequently their susceptibility to clever hostile propaganda." It recorded that Hynek suggested using Walt Disney animated cartoons. A section on unofficial investigating groups named the "Civilian Flying Saucer Investigators" (Los Angeles) and the "Aerial Phenomena Research Organization" (Wisconsin), stating that "such organizations should be watched."[^4]

### ODNI preliminary assessment, June 25, 2021

The Office of the Director of National Intelligence assessment of June 25, 2021, drafted by the Unidentified Aerial Phenomena Task Force ([[UAP Task Force]]) and the National Intelligence Manager for Aviation with input from the Under Secretary of Defense for Intelligence and Security, [[Defense Intelligence Agency]], [[Federal Bureau of Investigation]], National Reconnaissance Office, National Geospatial-Intelligence Agency, [[National Security Agency]], the Air Force, Army, Navy, Office of Naval Intelligence and [[DARPA]], reviewed incidents from November 2004 to March 2021. Of 144 reports from U.S. government sources, 80 involved observation with multiple sensors; one was identified "with high confidence" as a large deflating balloon, and "the others remain unexplained." The assessment stated that some UAP "appeared to remain stationary in winds aloft, move against the wind, maneuver abruptly, or move at considerable speed, without discernable means of propulsion," that the task force held "a small amount of data that appear to show UAP demonstrating acceleration or a degree of signature management," that there were 11 reports of documented near misses between pilots and UAP, and that "sociocultural stigmas" impeded reporting. It stated that UAP "probably lack a single explanation," proposed five explanatory categories (airborne clutter, natural atmospheric phenomena, U.S. government or industry developmental programs, foreign adversary systems, and "other"), and stated that the task force lacked data to indicate any UAP were part of a foreign collection program or a breakthrough by a potential adversary.[^2]

### Public Law 117-263, December 23, 2022

Section 6802 of the Intelligence Authorization Act portion of the National Defense Authorization Act for Fiscal Year 2023 (Public Law 117-263) rewrote section 1683 of the fiscal 2022 Act to create the [[All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office]] (AARO) to carry out the duties of the task force "including those pertaining to unidentified anomalous phenomena," reporting to the Deputy Secretary of Defense and the Principal Deputy Director of National Intelligence. It added the statutory definitions of "transmedium objects or devices" (objects observed to transition between space and the atmosphere, or between the atmosphere and bodies of water, and not immediately identifiable) and of unidentified anomalous phenomena. The same section required the AARO director to submit a written report on the historical record of the United States government on UAP, focusing on the period beginning January 1, 1945, including "any program or activity that was protected by restricted access that has not been explicitly and clearly reported to Congress" and "any efforts to obfuscate, manipulate public opinion, hide, or otherwise provide incorrect unclassified or classified information about unidentified anomalous phenomena or related activities."[^1]

Section 1673 of the same Act (50 U.S.C. 3373b) directed the Secretary of Defense, through the AARO head, to establish a secure mechanism for authorized reporting of any event relating to UAP and of "any activity or program by a department or agency of the Federal Government or a contractor of such a department or agency relating to unidentified anomalous phenomena, including with respect to material retrieval, material analysis, reverse engineering, research and development, detection and tracking, developmental or operational testing, and security protections and enforcement." The mechanism was to prevent "unauthorized public reporting or compromise of classified military and intelligence systems, programs, and related activity," including special access and compartmented programs, and required notice to the congressional defense and intelligence committees and congressional leadership within 72 hours of a disclosure relating to a restricted access program not explicitly and clearly reported to them.[^1]
