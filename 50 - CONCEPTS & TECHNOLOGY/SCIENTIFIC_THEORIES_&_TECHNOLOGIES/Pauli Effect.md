The Pauli effect is the anecdotal tendency of technical equipment to encounter critical failure in the presence of certain people. The term is named for the Austrian-born theoretical physicist [[Wolfgang Pauli]] (1900-1958), whose reputation for disrupting laboratory apparatus was passed among physicists as a body of stories.[^1] [[Annie Jacobsen]]'s account of the subject describes Pauli as having an uncanny ability to cause experimental equipment to malfunction simply by being in its vicinity.[^2]

### Stories told by colleagues

A 2001 memoir by the physicist [[Engelbert Schücking]] records several of these stories. Schücking had met Pauli in [[Zürich]] and at the [[Hamburg Observatory]] in the late 1950s. He wrote that after a talk he gave in Zürich he stood beside Pauli when two streetcars collided in front of them. Pauli turned to him, his face flushed, and exclaimed "Pauli effect!" Schücking glossed the remark: Pauli "had a reputation for being so disastrously clumsy with laboratory equipment that it was assumed that any mishap within kilometers of his presence was somehow a manifestation of a 'Pauli effect.'"[^1]

Schücking also recorded that Pauli had visited the Hamburg Observatory in the 1920s to see his friend [[Walter Baade]], and that "The Pauli effect, on that occasion, had almost destroyed the observatory's great refractor telescope."[^1] Schücking's memoir places a further visit by Pauli to the observatory in November 1958, the month before Pauli's death. Pauli told Schücking that he reached Baade by postcard, timed to arrive at the full moon, when astronomers would not be observing.[^1]

### Göttingen and the railway station

The incident most often cited is a failure in the laboratory of [[James Franck]] at the [[University of Göttingen]]. [[George Gamow]] told it in 1966: "Early one afternoon, without apparent cause, a complicated apparatus for the study of atomic phenomena collapsed." Franck wrote humorously about it to Pauli in Zürich and, after some delay, received an answer in an envelope with a Danish stamp. Pauli wrote that he had gone to visit [[Niels Bohr]] and that at the time of the mishap his train had been stopped for a few minutes at the Göttingen railroad station.[^3] Gamow introduced the subject by saying that Pauli was famous for three things, the Pauli principle, the Pauli neutrino and the Pauli effect, "a mysterious phenomenon which is not, and probably never will, be understood on a purely materialistic basis," and added that "there are many other observations concerning the reality of the Pauli Effect!"[^3]

### Otto Stern's laboratory

[[Otto Stern]] gave an interview to the Swiss physicist [[Res Jost]] in Zürich on December 2, 1961. Stern said that Pauli "was not allowed to enter our laboratory, because of the Pauli effect": when Pauli called, Stern was fetched, and Pauli knocked at the door without entering. "Oh yes, we were very superstitious at that time." Asked by Jost whether anything had ever happened, Stern answered: "Alas, many things did happen. The number of Pauli effects, the guaranteed (verbürgten) Pauli effects, is enormously large." Stern kept a wooden hammer by his apparatus in [[Frankfurt]]; when it vanished the apparatus failed for three days until the hammer was found. During bad runs he invited Pauli to dinner and "bewailed my sufferings, and that helped."[^4]

### Colleagues' accounts, 1928 to 1956

[[Erwin Panofsky]], speaking at the Princeton celebration of Pauli's Nobel Prize, said the citation had "inexcusably omitted" the celebrated Pauli effect. He recalled that at an outdoor restaurant in [[Hamburg]] in 1928 he and a friend had been sitting in whipped cream for about three hours, but not Pauli, and called it the Pauli effect in reverse.[^5] On a postcard from the steamer Grusia on August 25, 1930, to [[Paul Ehrenfest]], Pauli and [[Arnold Sommerfeld]] wrote: "The Pauli effects so far have been quite harmless. Many greetings from God's whip. Sommerfeld."[^6] When Pauli broke his shoulder in a fall at Ann Arbor in 1931, Sommerfeld called it an inverse Pauli effect.[^7] Pauli wrote to [[Victor Weisskopf]] on September 5, 1934, that his former car had suffered "a genuine Pauli effect, i.e. without my direct intervention or visible exterior causes," which had cost him a lot of money and cut his vacation short.[^8]

On May 26, 1955, before Pauli's lecture on Einstein in Zürich, the Lambretta of the physicist Speiser caught fire at a filling station, the bicycle of [[Felix Thellung]] had flat tyres and [[Ralph Kronig]] missed his tram stop. Pauli appeared highly amused and considered them typical Pauli effects; [[Charles Enz]] recorded that a defining feature of a Pauli effect was that Pauli himself never experienced any harm.[^9] At Varenna in the summer of 1956 a taxi stalled, Enz said "Pauli effect!", and Pauli did not appreciate it.[^10]

### Pauli on his own effect

[[Markus Fierz]] wrote that Pauli "thoroughly believed in his effect." Pauli told him that he sensed the mischief beforehand as a disagreeable tension, and that when the anticipated misfortune struck he felt strangely liberated and lightened. Fierz added: "It is quite legitimate to understand the 'Pauli effect' as a synchronistic phenomenon as conceived by Jung."[^11] Pauli wrote to Jung on June 28, 1949 that a synchronistic phenomenon occurs when the pairs of opposites keep the balance among each other as much as possible, a moment the I Ching characterizes by the sign Dschen (shaking, thunder).[^11]

### The overturned vase in Zürich

Pauli's own account is a letter to [[Carl Jung]] dated June 16, 1948, written from [[Zollikon]], near Zürich. It opens with the incident: "When that amusing 'Pauli effect' of the overturned vase occurred, on the occasion of the founding of the [[C. G. Jung Institute]], I had the immediate and vivid impression that I should 'pour out water inside'."[^12] Pauli described the phrase as symbolic language that he had acquired from Jung.[^12] Pauli wrote that when the connection between psychology and physics took up a large part of Jung's talk, "it became even more clear to me what I was to do. The outcome of all this is the enclosed essay."[^12]

A later essay on the Jung and Pauli relationship describes the object as a china vase that fell and shattered for no discernible reason as Pauli entered the meeting hall, and names the paper Pauli wrote in response as "Background-Physics."[^13] The letter itself does not name the enclosed essay. The appendix of the letters edition prints an essay, "Modern Examples of Background Physics" ("Hintergrundsphysik"), which Schücking described in a 2002 review as an unpublished essay found with the correspondence and addressing its main theme. In its introduction Pauli wrote that the physicist, "in having recourse to these facts, is of necessity bound to move from this background and end up in the world of psychology."[^14]

### The letters and the joint volume

The correspondence between Pauli and Jung ran from 1932 to 1958. The letters edition, [[Atom and Archetype]], contains 80 letters, 39 of them by Pauli.[^14] The English-language volume [[The Interpretation of Nature and the Psyche]] ([[Bollingen Series]] LI, 1955) carried Jung's essay "Synchronicity: An Acausal Connecting Principle" alongside the physicist's contribution.[^15] The principle the essay develops is Jung's concept of [[Synchronicity]].

### Use in remote-viewing literature

In 1976 [[Hal Puthoff]] and [[Russell Targ]] published, in the [[Proceedings of the IEEE]], a survey of the history of inquiry into paranormal perception together with recent experiments. Their discussion of the experiments states: "Currently, we have no precise model of this spatial and temporal remote-viewing phenomenon. However, models of the universe involving higher order synchronicity or correlation have been proposed by the physicist Pauli and the psychologist Carl Jung."[^16] A copy of the paper is held in the [[Central Intelligence Agency]]'s document series on the [[Stargate Project]] (CIA-RDP96-00787R000200080007-7).[^16]

Jacobsen wrote that at [[SRI International]] equipment malfunctioned around [[Uri Geller]] (computers crashed, magnetic tape became demagnetized), that scientists called it the Pauli effect, and that "in homage to the Pauli effect" the SRI researchers began calling the occurrences the [[Geller Effect]].[^2] She also wrote that in February 1950, during a visit to [[Princeton University]], the cyclotron Pauli had come to observe inexplicably caught fire.[^2] The term appears in [[Psychokinesis]] research as an example of the mind influencing physical systems.[^2]

### Correspondence with a Pittsburgh physicist

Jacobsen places a paper by [[Robert A. McConnell]] titled "ESP: Fact or Fancy?" in the early 1950s discussion between Pauli and Jung on [[Extrasensory Perception|ESP]] and psychokinesis. Jung remarked that some age-old mysteries never change and that attempts to explain away seemingly miraculous results often fail against the facts.[^2]

The archive of Pauli's papers at [[CERN]] holds the correspondence directly. McConnell wrote from [[Pittsburgh]] to Pauli on September 28, 1950, sending a reprint on ESP and mentioning his work on psychokinetic effects.[^17] Pauli wrote to McConnell on October 14, 1954; the archive holds a typed copy made by Pauli. According to the archive's description, the letter refers to the ideas of [[G. D. Wassermann]] on "mental patterns", which resemble Jung's archetypes, suggests that a wider significance of the archetype concept may help in understanding ESP and its connection with evolution, and asks McConnell a specific question about ESP experiments.[^18] McConnell replied on October 22, 1954, giving Wassermann's address in England and answering the question about the tests.[^19]

### Dreams and the alchemy volume

Jung analyzed Pauli's dreams after a nervous breakdown Pauli suffered while working in Zürich. According to [[Ian F. Roberts]], who cites [[David Lindorff]], Jung first placed Pauli with one of his pupils for fear of influencing the analysis, and took over the treatment after ten months. Pauli shared about 1,000 dreams. Jung's 1935 paper on dream symbols included 74 of them, and the paper grew into [[Psychology and Alchemy]].[^20]

### Interpretations by later writers

The physicist [[Harald Atmanspacher]] and [[H. Primas]], writing on Pauli, quote the early interpretation of the failures as "synchronistic manifestations of a deep conflict between his rational and non-rational side."[^21] After Pauli's death, the physicist Markus Fierz described him in a 1980 biographical entry as "the conscience of theoretical physics."[^22]

### Death in 1958

Pauli died of cancer at the [[Red Cross Hospital]] in Zürich on December 15, 1958, aged 58, according to the description of his papers at CERN.[^23] The stomach pains began during a lecture on December 5; on December 6 he was taken to the Red Cross Hospital on Gloriastrasse. On the Monday evening Pauli asked Enz whether he had seen the room number: "137!" Enz replied that it must mean good luck, and Pauli "was not convinced."[^24] His widow, [[Franca Pauli]], assisted by his last assistant, Charles Enz, began sorting and administering the collection after his death.[^23]
