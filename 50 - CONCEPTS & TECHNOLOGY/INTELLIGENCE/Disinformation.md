Disinformation is false information deliberately and often covertly spread in order to influence public opinion or obscure the truth. In accounts of Cold War [[Parapsychology|psi]] research it figures in the origins of the [[Soviet Union|Soviet]] research program.[^1]

The French accounts of 1959 and 1960 describing telepathy tests between a shore laboratory and the USS Nautilus ([[The Nautilus (Telepathy Project)]]) were cited by [[Leonid Vasiliev]] in 1960, in a lecture at Leningrad and, according to [[I. V. Tsykunov]], in a report to Defense Minister [[Rodion Malinovsky]], when he argued for renewed Soviet psi research.[^2][^3] The [[U.S. Navy]] denied the reports, and the submarine's first captain, [[William R. Anderson]], said in 1963 that the Nautilus was in dock on the date given.[^4] [[Martin Ebon]] recorded that some Americans entertained the hypothesis that the story was a "Soviet plant," a piece of "disinformation" launched in France to provide a reason for a start-up of Soviet psychic studies, and that [[Gerard Messadie|Gérald Messadié]] told him in 1980 that the whole thing was probably a hoax and that [[Jacques Bergier]], who had ties to French intelligence, was his source.[^5] [[Jim Schnabel]] wrote that the story may have been Bergier's private fraud or a disinformation ploy designed to stir up valuable data about American psi research or to encourage Russia to undertake wasteful psi research.[^6] A 1975 [[Defense Intelligence Agency|DIA]] study described the Nautilus "experiments" as "probably mythical," while saying the claims had "one tangible consequence" for Soviet research.[^7]
