[[Coordinate Remote Viewing]] (CRV) is a [[Remote Viewing|remote viewing]] method in which the viewer receives only geographic coordinates for a site and produces a sequence of responses, beginning with a kinesthetic drawing called an ideogram. The coordinate targeting was proposed by [[Ingo Swann]] at [[Stanford Research Institute|SRI]] in 1973.[^1] The staged training course was developed at SRI under joint [[Defense Intelligence Agency|DIA]] and [[United States Army Intelligence and Security Command|INSCOM]] funding, with a September 1981 contract task to "Continue development and evaluation of CRV training program," and Army personnel began training under it in July 1982.[^2][^3]

### Coordinate targeting, 1972 to 1975

The SRI chronology in the 1979 evaluation of the program by [[Harry L. Snyder]] and [[Arlene G. Snyder]] records that Swann wrote to [[Hal Puthoff]] on March 30, 1972 and visited him in June 1972.[^4] In [[Jim Schnabel|Schnabel's]] account, Swann proposed the coordinate method in April 1973. Swann believed that coordinates would let a psychic be directed to a target precisely and economically, without being told what the target was, so that conscious imagination would not interfere with the impressions. Puthoff and [[Russell Targ]] were initially skeptical, arguing that coordinate systems were arbitrary human creations and that a psychic's success might be attributed to eidetic memory or cryptomnesia. Swann persisted, and after informal tests produced results the protocol was tightened so that coordinates were chosen by a third party unknown to the researchers and the psychic.[^1]

The Snyder evaluation gives an independent account of the same beginning. It quotes [[Mind-Reach|Targ and Puthoff's 1977 book]] for Swann's remark that he could "look anywhere in the world if you just gave me some coordinates like latitude and longitude." The evaluation says that the first experiments "were done largely to placate Swann; however, when these were considered to be successful, more controlled experiments and a 'standard' protocol were developed." Its chronology lists Swann's return to SRI for eight months in 1973 and a series of [[SCANATE]] readings on a "Virginia site," first by Swann and then by [[Pat Price]], followed by readings on Kerguelen Island and on an Urals site.[^4]

The Sugar Grove episode is recorded in two accounts. Schnabel wrote that Swann and Price accurately described a secret [[National Security Agency|NSA]] facility although they had been given coordinates for a different location.[^1] [[Roger A. Beaumont]], writing in the January 1982 issue of [[SIGNAL (magazine)|SIGNAL]], summarized the SRI report on the tests, which the experimenters called "Project Scanate," as describing Price's detailed reading of a supposedly secret installation in Virginia. Beaumont listed among the explanations offered by the sponsors for the later reversal of their assessment that the coordinates had been confused, and/or that the subject had described the nearby [[Sugar Grove]] station.[^5] Both explanations are Beaumont's summary of what the sponsors said, not his own finding.

### The name and the first stages

The Army's chronology of the program records that in July 1982 [[Grill Flame]] personnel began training at SRI in the new Coordinate Remote Viewing (CRV) method. The same chronology lists the [[Gondola Wish]] plan and the establishment of the Grill Flame program before that entry.[^3] The term was in use among Army staff and the SRI program by 1983. A memorandum of October 1984 credits Swann with the name: "Ingo Swann, who coined the term CRV."[^6] A Center Lane training and applications procedures document submitted on June 6, 1984 defines CRV as "A Remote Viewing technique that requires the use of coordinates as targeting information," and lists CRV training as a contracted service of SRI with a duration of 12 to 18 months at Menlo Park, New York and Washington, D.C.[^7] A Defense Intelligence Agency memorandum records that the project's new nickname was STAR GATE, effective January 31, 1991, the name that gives the [[Stargate Project]] its title.[^8] Later overview memoranda use the name Project STAR GATE.[^9]

In September 1980 an Army information paper recorded that Swann had said SRI would not be ready for advanced training for one year, while Puthoff told an Army officer that SRI was ready and that Swann, as a consultant, could not speak for SRI.[^10] In a December 1982 memorandum to the Army, Puthoff described the FY 1981 decision: at the start of the joint services program with DIA, SRI "made a decision to go forward with developing and codifying the most Promising RV enhancement procedure that had emerged from earlier work, a six-stage training procedure developed by SRI consultant I. Swann."[^11]

### The eight-stage draft of 1983

The draft report, titled on its cover "Co-ordinate Remote Viewing (CRV) Technology 1981-1983 Three-Year Project," dated August 30, 1983 and prepared for Puthoff under the number SRI/GF-0253, is the fullest account of the training course in the record. It describes training as a regime: "'Training' implies a prefigured regime that will, if correctly applied, lead to predictable performance." The draft says that psychic signals "offer themselves up to interpretative consciousness through a predictable series of 'signal impulses.'" The series "starts with 'greatest' meaning, and evolves into 'specific' components."[^12]

The draft's figure lists eight stages. Stage 1 produces ideograms and ideogram responses, Stage 2 registers sensations from the distant site, Stage 3 covers motion and mobility, producing sketches and "trackers," and Stage 4 covers quantitative and qualitative assessments of the site. Stage 5 (methods of interrogating the signal line) and Stage 7 (sonics) are marked as still in research and development, Stage 6 (creating three-dimensional models) as consolidated, and Stage 8 (human to human interfaces) as in research and development for 1984 and 1985. The text states the status of the first six: "Stages 1 through 3 have been confirmed and delivered to trainees. Stage 4 and Stage 6 have been confirmed and are ready to be delivered to trainees upon their completion of Stage 3. Stage 5 is understood, but has not yet been solidified into a training package."[^12]

The draft describes two trainee groups. The first group, in its words, was "composed of persons who had had psi experience and had acted as experimental subjects in several other kinds of parapsychological experiments." The second group was made up of professionals, "none of which had acted before as an overt psychic-type of person in parapsychological experiments." The draft says that all the viewers "have responded with near similarity to each other."[^12]

The August 4, 1983 briefing on the program reproduces the stage table and calls the ideogram "perhaps the most significant occurrence in all the CRV work."[^13]

### The six stages in the 1985 working paper

The December 1982 final report by Puthoff lists six stages on one table, with the labels "Major gestalt," "Sensory contact," "Dimension, motion, mobility," "Qualitative aspects," "Significant analytical aspects" and "Specific quantitative aspects." The February 1985 working paper, titled on its cover "Coordinate Remote Viewing Stages I-VI and Beyond" and marked with the project officer's title and the name withheld, gives the most complete definitions of those stages in the record. Its purpose, in its words, is "to provide an overview of Coordinate Remote Viewing (CRV) Training Stages I through VI." It defines the method as "the process by which a person is capable of 'perceiving' information concerning a site remote from him in location and/or time given only the geographic coordinates of that location." It adds: "One cannot expect to learn RV simply by reading this document. CRV must be learned by doing."[^14]

Stage I is "a quick-reaction response to the reading of geographic coordinates by the interviewer." The response "takes the form of an immediate, primitive 'squiggle' on paper. This 'squiggle' is known as an ideogram." The ideogram "captures the overall feeling/motion of the gestalt of the site (e.g., fluid/wavy for water). This response is kinesthetic and not visual."[^14]

Stage II trains the viewer "to become sensitive to sensations associated with the site." The paper says that Stage II signals "are essentially nonvisual in nature."[^14]

Stage III is the stage in which "we observe the emergence of a broader concept of the site." The paper calls the wider contact "a 'widening of the aperture'" and the overall appreciation "an 'aesthetic impact'." After that impact "the urge to draw the site begins," and the viewer produces sketches, trackers and additional ideograms.[^14]

Stage IV is the point of analytical data. The paper says that "Stage IV is therefore the point where the viewer begins to become operational." It gives as examples of what Stage IV reports the ambience of a site ("military, religious, technical, or educational") and its function ("power generation, BW research, or human research").[^14]

Stage V is the interrogation of the signal line. The paper says that a viewer who probes the significance of Stage IV data "usually results in the production of AOL," the [[Analytical Overlay|analytical overlay]] described below. "In Stage V however, special techniques are used to produce the more detailed information without triggering AOL."[^14]

Stage VI is three-dimensional contact and modeling. The viewer "uses various materials to produce three dimensional representations of the site," and the modeling "is done with 'feeling'." The paper states that the kinesthetic activity "appears to both quench AOL formation associated with purely cerebral processes, and to act as a trigger to produce further analytical content of the site."[^14]

### Ideograms and analytical overlay

The paper defines the ideogram as "the kinesthetic response of the viewer to his perception of the site" and says that "Ideograms are the basis for the CRV training program." An ideogram has three parts: the mark, an "A-" for feeling and motion, and a "B-" for an automatic analytical response. The ideal sequence is written "I+A=B." The paper says that without mastering the ideogramic process "the trainee cannot proceed to subsequent stages." Ideograms are classed as single, double, composite and multiple.[^14]

Analytical overlay, abbreviated AOL, is the viewer's premature interpretation of fragmentary data. The paper's example is that "an impression of an island is immediately interpreted as Hawaii." Stage I and Stage II training are designed to teach the viewer to "grab" incoming data bits while controlling the overlays.[^14]

The paper also disclaims any claim about the trainee's underlying ability: "In teaching CRV we are not teaching the trainee to be psychic. We are not teaching him to receive the signal. We are teaching him the proper format to be used in objectifying the data he perceives upon receiving the coordinate."[^14] Its session classes separate training sessions, which are given feedback and "Used in training sessions," from confirmation sessions, in which the monitor is blind to the site, and from operational sessions.[^14]

### Trainees and units, 1982 to 1985

A memorandum of March 24, 1983, signed by CPT [[Tom McNear|Thomas M. McNear]], MI, as Project Officer, amends a trip report of July 6 to 22, 1982 to SRI that covered Stages One and Two. It records that he was at SRI in [[Menlo Park, California|Menlo Park]] from March 7 to 15, 1983 to learn the Stage Three techniques, that Stage Three would take about two more two-week training periods, and that Stage III "is broken into four parts": aesthetic impact, sketches, trackers and movement at the site.[^15]

A second group began CRV training at SRI in January 1984. A February 1984 information paper gives the dates as "15-27 JAN 84" for the second group's start.[^16] A memorandum of December 6, 1983 records that the [[Center Lane]] office had identified CPT [[Ed Dames|Edward Dames]] as a prospective member of the project, that he "agreed to a future assignment to the project and to participate in the training," and that Major General [[Albert Stubblebine]] commented that Dames was a good choice.[^17] The Center Lane access roster of April 10, 1984 lists Edward Dames of INSCOM with a level 3 access dated August 25, 1983.[^18]

The October 22, 1984 training plan records that four Center Lane personnel were then in Stages I through III and one trainee in Stage V. The plan says of the four personnel in Stages I through III that they "are ICLP initiates and as such have not achieved a sufficient level of expertise to be operationally employed," and that they would begin Stage III on October 22, 1984 and were required by contract to finish it by the end of December 1984. The additional individual, who had completed Stages I, II, III, IV and VI, "is considered operational" and was expected to finish Stage V before the end of November 1984. The plan adds that two Center Lane personnel were in an in-house phase of CRV training that applied "basic principles drawn from the Ingo Swann CRV methodology," and recommends that Swann's training continue with the four trainees in Stages IV, V and VI because they "have adapted well to the Swann training model." It states: "Training is conducted at SRI-International facilities in Menlo Park, CA and New York, NY." The same plan records that the Center Lane project "will be transferred to the Defense Intelligence Agency (DIA)" and that its psychoenergetic collection activities ceased on September 30, 1984. A second copy of the memorandum carries the signature of CPT [[Frederick Atwater|Frederick H. Atwater]], MI, OPS/TNG Officer.[^6][^19]

A July 1984 SRI report by Puthoff on Stage IV, covering February 1983 to April 1984 and prepared under Army contract DAKF27-83-C-0018, describes a trainee identified only as Trainee #059. The trainee began Stage IV in December 1983 and "completed the requirements for S-IV on 22 March 1984." The report says that "Thirty-one (31) S-IV training sessions were conducted with this trainee," and that "the 31 sessions provided a total of twenty-six (26) completed trials." It calls the trainee "a model trainee."[^20]

A December 1984 SRI report on Stages V and VI names the same trainee as the first to complete the six-stage package: "Army INSCOM Trainee #059 is the first individual to complete the six-stage training package described in this report." The report gives the time as "2 1/2 years." Stage VI was delivered before Stage V, because "the purpose of S-V is to correct and elaborate, which is an addition to, rather than a foundation for, the use of S-VI procedures." In six test sessions with no feedback, the trainee correctly described the Padre Bay, Utah, site among other targets. The report states that "Only eight sites were required to declare Trainee #059 complete on S-V," and that after the trainee returned to the client facility, "a series of verification tests under client-controlled conditions" began.[^21]

The reports give no name for Trainee #059. A letter of April 24, 1984, from the Army's security support detachment at [[Fort Meade]] to Puthoff, concerns SRI's contract reporting and refers to "Upon completion of Stage IV training by Tom McNear."[^22] The Stage IV completion in the July 1984 report and the April letter fall within a month of each other. The records found do not establish whether Trainee #059 is Tom McNear.

### Later use

In "Tinker, Tailor, Soldier, Psi," an article by Schnabel in the [[Independent on Sunday]] of August 27, 1995, Schnabel wrote that by the early 1980s Swann "had developed what he claimed was a superior co-ordinate-based remote-viewing technique, or CRV," and that "Swann taught the technique to five new recruits to the Fort Meade unit." An unnamed long-time member of the unit told Schnabel: "We often used CRV for target acquisition, and ERV for in-depth work on the target."[^23] CRV was one of two methods the unit used, alongside [[Extended Remote Viewing]].

### Sun Streak, 1985 to 1989

The Defense Intelligence Agency manual for [[Sun Streak]] of December 1985 defines CRV as "A Remote Viewing technique that requires the use of coordinates as targeting information." It describes the external CRV training as a contracted service provided by SRI, with a duration of 12 to 18 months, divided into stages 1 through 6, and states that "Experience and theory extension indicates that additional increments exist beyond Stage 6."[^24]

The Sun Streak annual report for 1986, forwarded in February 1987, lists training sessions in 1986 by source number: #003 (16), #011 (106), #018 (50), #021 (24), #079 (35) and #101 (24), a total of 255. Its source notes state that Source #003 "received one and one-half years of CRV training and has had two years of operational experience" and "was the primary author/compiler of the CRV training document used by this office"; that Source #021, with the same training and experience, "was co-author/compiler of the CRV training document"; that Source #011, previously an operational ERV source and interviewer, completed CRV Stage 2 on October 10, 1986 and Stage 3 on December 5, 1986; that Source #018 received one year of CRV training followed by six months of cross-training in ERV; and that Source #101 received one and one-half years of CRV training and six months of ERV training. The report's training precis says that the CRV procedure "was developed by an SRI-International (SRI-I) subcontractor in the early 1980s," that it "is fully documented in booklet form" after the technology transfer from the subcontractor, and that the booklet "may not be reproduced or disseminated without permission."[^25]

A memorandum of October 17, 1986 reporting on the third quarter of that year records that, with the formation of two remote-viewing teams, trainees were assigned either to CRV or to ERV, with sources #101 and #018 to be "dual qualified." Of 106 training sessions in the quarter, 82 were CRV. It records that "A new CRV training protocol has been implemented," beginning with Source #011, in which virtually all sessions use Class A or Class B sites and, from Stage 2, "encrypted" coordinates.[^26]

The Sun Streak annual report for 1987 states that procedures were established to cross-train all remote viewers in CRV and ERV and that the time required to become operational was reduced from two years to six months. The annual report for 1988, dated January 10, 1989, repeats the cross-training and the reduction, adds a third method, Written Remote Viewing, and records that one viewer completed CRV Stage IV on January 10, 1989.[^27]

### Later careers

Schnabel's 1995 article reports that Dames left the unit in 1989 and "formed a company, Psi Tech, to make commercial use of his remote-viewing skills." By 1995, according to Schnabel, the company offered "a private remote-viewing service, as well as training courses for people who want to become remote-viewers themselves."[^23]
