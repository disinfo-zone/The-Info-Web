The Senate Select Committee to Study Governmental Operations with Respect to Intelligence Activities, known as the Church Committee after its chairman, [[Frank Church]] of Idaho, investigated the [[Central Intelligence Agency]] (CIA), the [[Federal Bureau of Investigation]] (FBI), the [[NSA|National Security Agency]] (NSA) and the [[Internal Revenue Service]] from January 1975 to April 1976. It issued an interim report on assassination plots on November 20, 1975, a six-book final report dated April 26, 1976, and seven volumes of hearings.[^1][^2] The Senate Historical Office gives April 29, 1976 as the date the final report was published.[^3]

### Creation and membership

The Senate created the committee on January 27, 1975 under Senate Resolution 21.[^2][^4] Church chaired it, and [[John G. Tower]] of Texas was vice chairman. The other members were Philip A. Hart of Michigan, [[Walter F. Mondale]] of Minnesota, Walter D. Huddleston of Kentucky, Robert Morgan of North Carolina, Gary Hart of Colorado, [[Howard H. Baker Jr.]] of Tennessee, [[Barry Goldwater]] of Arizona, Charles McC. Mathias Jr. of Maryland and Richard S. Schweiker of Pennsylvania.[^5] William G. Miller was staff director. [[Frederick A. O. Schwarz Jr.]] was chief counsel, [[Curtis R. Smothers]] was counsel to the minority, and Audrey Hatry was clerk.[^5]

### Triggering events

On December 22, 1974 the [[New York Times]] published on its front page an article by [[Seymour Hersh]], "Huge C.I.A. Operation Reported in U.S. Against Antiwar Forces, Other Dissidents in Nixon Years." Hersh wrote that the CIA "conducted a massive, illegal domestic intelligence operation during the Nixon Administration against the antiwar movement and other dissident groups in the United States," and that intelligence files on at least 10,000 American citizens were kept by a special unit of the CIA that reported directly to [[Richard Helms]]. The article attributes direction of the domestic activities to the counterintelligence department of [[James Jesus Angleton]], names [[Richard Ober]] as the unit's liaison with Helms, and reports that a check of the CIA's domestic files ordered in 1973 by [[James Schlesinger]] had produced evidence of dozens of other illegal activities, among them break-ins, wiretapping and mail inspection. The program it describes is the one the committee later documented as [[Operation CHAOS]].[^6] [[William Colby]] wrote that he dismissed Angleton on December 17, 1974, five days before the article appeared.[^7]

Earlier revelations included the "Family Jewels," a compilation of potentially illegal CIA activities that DCI Schlesinger ordered assembled in 1973 and transmitted to Colby in September 1973. Colby retained the document and later provided it to the committee.[^8] Also earlier, the exposure of [[COINTELPRO]] followed the March 1971 [[Media FBI Office Break-In|Media, Pennsylvania, FBI break-in]], and the [[Watergate]] investigations revealed CIA involvement in domestic political operations.[^9]

### Operation CHAOS and domestic collection

The committee covered the CIA, FBI, NSA, Army intelligence and the IRS. Its account of the program describes a unit run by the Special Operations Group under Ober inside the [[Counterintelligence Staff]] of Angleton. On August 15, 1967, [[Thomas Karamessines]] sent Angleton a memorandum that referred to "Dick Ober (or whatever officer you select)." The committee found no written directive from Helms to Karamessines establishing the program, and treated the August 15, 1967 memorandum as the first recorded authorization. It recorded that Ober testified before the committee on October 28 and 30, 1975 and that his deputy testified under the name "James Eatinger" on October 14, 1975.[^10]

The CHAOS information was held in a computer system called [[HYDRA]], compartmented into layers of increasing sensitivity and restricted access.[^11] The committee also documented [[Project MERRIMAC]] and [[Project RESISTANCE]], which were administered by the [[CIA Office of Security|Office of Security]] rather than by Angleton's staff and which supplied information to CHAOS.[^12] The [[Rockefeller Commission 1975|Rockefeller Commission]] reported about 13,000 CHAOS files, including about 7,200 personality files on American citizens, and an index of more than 300,000 names and organizations. It gave March 15, 1974 as the date the program was terminated.[^13]

The committee documented the CIA's [[HTLINGUAL]] program, which opened first-class mail between the United States and the Soviet Union from 1952 to 1973, as an illegal domestic operation conducted without judicial authorization.[^14]

The committee's NSA section records that from the early 1960s until 1973 the National Security Agency intercepted and disseminated international communications of selected American citizens and groups on the basis of name lists supplied by other agencies, that in 1967 the watch lists were expanded to include individuals and organizations in domestic antiwar and civil rights activities, and that NSA formalized the program under the codename MINARET in 1969. It records that Attorney General [[Elliot Richardson]] and Assistant Attorney General Henry Petersen concluded in October 1973 that the watch lists were of "questionable legality."[^15]

### COINTELPRO and the King investigation

The FBI's programs used infiltration, psychological warfare, fabricated communications, referrals for prosecution and coordination with local police against lawful political activity. Between 1960 and 1974 the Bureau conducted more than 500,000 separate investigations of persons and groups under its "subversive" category.[^16][^17]

Book III of the final report devotes pages 1 to 80 to COINTELPRO and pages 81 to 184 to the FBI's campaign against [[Martin Luther King Jr.]], under the heading "Dr. Martin Luther King, Jr., Case Study."[^17] The case study opens with testimony of [[William Sullivan|William C. Sullivan]], then the Bureau's assistant director for domestic intelligence and, in the report's words, the man in charge of the FBI's "war" against King: "No holds were barred." The report records wiretaps on King's home telephone from October 1963 until mid-1965, initially approved by Attorney General [[Robert Kennedy|Robert F. Kennedy]], wiretaps on the telephones of the [[Southern Christian Leadership Conference]] headquarters for a longer period, and sixteen occasions on which the FBI acknowledged hiding microphones in King's hotel and motel rooms.[^18] The electronic surveillance section begins at page 111, the wiretap account at page 115 and the microphone account (January 1964 to November 1965) at page 120. A later section covers the Bureau's effort in 1964 to discredit King, beginning at page 131, including Sullivan's January 1964 proposal to promote a new Negro leader (page 135).[^19]

The report refers to two of King's advisers as "Adviser A" and "Adviser B." It records that a microphone was installed in the office of Adviser A on March 16, 1962 and a wiretap on his office telephone on March 20, 1962, and that the Attorney General authorized a wiretap on his home telephone in November 1962. It states that the information from the continued coverage of Adviser A "appears to have related solely to his advice to Dr. King concerning the civil rights movement and not at all to the alleged Communist Party origins of that advice."[^20] The committee found that the FBI's justification for surveillance of [[Stanley Levison]] was inadequate to support the scope of the surveillance, and that the surveillance extended beyond counterintelligence into political disruption under COINTELPRO.[^21]

The section on the tape mailed to King begins at page 158. Sullivan testified that [[Alan Belmont]] told him that Director [[J. Edgar Hoover]] wanted one of the King tapes mailed to [[Coretta Scott King]] to precipitate their separation, and that he sent an agent to Tampa, Florida to mail a package. The agent testified that he flew to Miami, telephoned Sullivan, and mailed the package, addressed to King, from a post office near the Miami airport; a travel voucher gives November 21, 1964 as the date of the flight.[^22] [[Andrew Young]] testified that a letter accompanied the tape stating that the tape would be released in 34 days and threatening that "there is only one thing you can do to prevent this from happening," and that the disturbing thing to King was that "somebody was trying to get him to commit suicide." The report discusses the letter's apparent purpose of inducing King to commit suicide at pages 158 to 161.[^22] Sullivan testified that he did not recall ever having seen the letter given to the committee, that it was "possible" he had something to do with it, and that it might have been "planted" in his files; after reading its last paragraph he conceded that it could be interpreted as an invitation to suicide.[^23] Sullivan wrote in his memoir that his division prepared the letter.[^24]

The committee also documented the Bureau's coordination with local police in the December 4, 1969 raid in Chicago that killed [[Fred Hampton]] of the [[Black Panther Party]]. The Black Panther section runs from page 185 to page 224 of Book III.[^25]

### Interim report: assassination plots

The Interim Report, "Alleged Assassination Plots Involving Foreign Leaders," was issued as Senate Report No. 94-465 on November 20, 1975.[^26] The Senate voted its release after [[Gerald Ford]] wrote to Church on October 31, 1975 asking that the report be suppressed and after Church refused that request on November 4.[^27]

The report states that the committee had "concrete evidence of at least eight plots involving the CIA to assassinate [[Fidel Castro]]" in the period 1960 to 1965.[^28] It records that on December 11, 1959 [[Allen Dulles]] approved the CIA's giving "thorough consideration to the elimination of Fidel Castro," following a memorandum of that date from [[J.C. King]], head of the Western Hemisphere Division, and that Dulles told the Special Group the following month that "we do not have in mind the quick elimination of Castro."[^29] The Cuba section describes the poison pills prepared by the [[Technical Services Division]] and the recruitment of underworld figures through [[Robert Maheu]], who brought in [[Johnny Roselli]], [[Sam Giancana]] and [[Santos Trafficante, Jr.]]. The report states that there is some evidence that Giancana or Roselli originated the idea of putting a poison pill in Castro's drink, and that the second batch of pills prepared by the Technical Services Division, which contained botulinum toxin, was tested on monkeys.[^30] [[William Harvey]], to whom [[Richard Bissell]] was asked to hand the gambling-syndicate operation in November 1961, asked in early April 1962 to be put in touch with Roselli, met him in Miami, and told him to maintain his Cuban contacts but not to deal with Maheu or Giancana.[^31][^32] [[Desmond FitzGerald]] and a case officer met the AM/LASH asset [[Rolando Cubela]] in Paris on November 22, 1963, the day of the Kennedy assassination, and the report records their assurances of support to Cubela.[^32] The committee found that the plots had not been disclosed to the [[Warren Commission]].[^32][^33]

The Congo section concerns [[Patrice Lumumba]]. In 1960 DDP Richard Bissell asked [[Bronson Tweedy]] to explore the feasibility of assassinating Lumumba. Bissell also asked a CIA scientist, identified in the report by the pseudonym "Joseph Scheider," to prepare to assassinate or incapacitate an unspecified "African leader."[^34] In late September 1960 Scheider delivered the lethal substances to the Leopoldville station officer, whom the report calls "Hedgman," and instructed him to assassinate Lumumba.[^34] The report records that the toxic substances were never used. [[Larry Devlin]] was the Congo station chief. Scheider testified on October 7 and 9, 1975 that he destroyed the viability of the biological material and disposed of it in the [[Congo River]] before leaving the Congo on October 5, 1960; the station officer testified on August 21, 1975 that he locked the material in the bottom drawer of his safe and disposed of it months later. Each recalled disposing of it in the Congo River, alone.[^35] Devlin wrote that he never used the materials and disposed of them in the river.[^36] The committee examined whether [[Michael Mulroney]] received an assassination assignment after refusing one from Bissell, and it examined whether the CIA was involved in Lumumba's transfer to Katanga.[^37] The committee found that Lumumba was killed in Katanga Province after forces loyal to [[Mobutu Sese Seko]] transferred him there.[^38]

The Dominican section concerns [[Rafael Trujillo]]. The report records that the CIA supplied three pistols and three carbines to dissidents, and that U.S. officials knew the dissidents intended to overthrow Trujillo, probably by assassination.[^39]

The Vietnam section concerns [[Ngo Dinh Diem]]. The report finds that the United States encouraged the coup against Diem but neither desired nor was involved in his death, which appears to have been a spontaneous act by Vietnamese generals.[^40] The Chile section concerns [[Rene Schneider|General René Schneider]], the Chilean army commander who was shot in an attempted abduction in October 1970. The report states that his removal became "a necessary ingredient in the coup plans of all the Chilean conspirators," and that the conspirators decided to kidnap him.[^41]

The Mongoose section records that Robert Kennedy played an active role in [[Operation Mongoose]], and that [[Maxwell Taylor]] was chairman of the Special Group (Augmented) that oversaw it. [[Edward Lansdale]] coordinated the operation, and William Harvey was placed in charge of the CIA's [[Task Force W]].[^42]

### Hearings

The committee published seven hearing volumes under Senate Resolution 21.[^2]

Volume 1, "Unauthorized Storage of Toxic Agents," was held September 16, 17 and 18, 1975, with testimony from Colby (September 16) and Helms (September 17). It concerned the CIA's shellfish toxin stored at [[Fort Detrick]] and the [[MKNAOMI]] program.[^43]

Volume 2, "Huston Plan," was held September 23, 24 and 25, 1975. Its witnesses were [[Tom Charles Huston]] (September 23), Angleton (September 24) and [[Charles Brennan]], former assistant director of the FBI's Domestic Intelligence Division (September 25).[^44]

Volume 3 is titled "Internal Revenue Service." Volume 5 is titled "National Security Agency and Fourth Amendment Rights."[^2]

Volume 4, "Mail Opening," was held October 21, 22 and 24, 1975. Its witnesses included former CIA Inspector General Gordon Stewart and former CIA Director of Security Howard J. Osborn (October 21) and former Postmasters General J. Edward Day, John A. Gronouski and Winton M. Blount (October 22).[^45]

Volume 6, "Federal Bureau of Investigation," was held on November 18 and 19 and December 2, 3, 9, 10 and 11, 1975. Its witnesses included FBI informants [[Mary Jo Cook]] and [[Gary Thomas Rowe]] (December 2), former FBI officials [[Cartha DeLoach]] and [[Courtney Evans]], former Attorneys General [[Nicholas Katzenbach]] and [[Ramsey Clark]] (December 3), FBI Director [[Clarence M. Kelley]] (December 10) and Attorney General [[Edward H. Levi]] (December 11). Exhibit 6 is a July 19, 1966 memorandum from Sullivan to DeLoach on "Black Bag" jobs.[^46]

Volume 7, "Covert Action," was held December 4 and 5, 1975. On December 4 the committee heard staff members William G. Miller, William Bader, Karl Inderfurth and [[Gregory Treverton]], and then Ralph Dungan, Charles A. Meyer and [[Edward Korry]], who was ambassador to Chile from 1967 to 1971. Treverton's statement quotes the handwritten notes of Helms from the September 15, 1970 meeting with [[Richard Nixon]], which include "Make the economy scream." It states that between October 5 and October 20, 1970 the CIA made 21 contacts with key military and police officials in Chile, and that on October 22 CIA officials passed three submachine guns to two Chilean officers; later that day General Schneider was mortally wounded in an abortive kidnap attempt, by a group that the statement says was not the group that received the weapons.[^47] The notes are reproduced as Exhibit 2 (page 96). Treverton wrote to Korry on October 20, 1975 (Exhibit 3, page 97), and Korry's reply to Church of October 23, 1975 is printed as Exhibit 4 (page 100).[^48]

The committee's staff report, *Covert Action in Chile 1963-1973*, was printed in December 1975. It states that the CIA spent three million dollars in an effort to influence the 1964 Chilean presidential election, and that eight million dollars was spent covertly in the three years between 1970 and the military coup of September 1973, in support of opposition to [[Salvador Allende]]. It records that the CIA spent more than thirteen million dollars on covert action in Chile between 1963 and 1974, and that Congress was briefed on projects totaling about 7.1 million dollars of it.[^49]

### Final report

The final report, Senate Report No. 94-755, was issued April 26, 1976 in six books.[^50]

Book I, "Foreign and Military Intelligence," covers the assassination programs and the [[Project MKUltra|MKULTRA]] program. It records approximately 11 grams of shellfish toxin held at a CIA laboratory at Fort Detrick that went undetected for five years, and it records that MKULTRA records were destroyed in January 1973 by Technical Services Division personnel acting on the verbal orders of [[Sidney Gottlieb]].[^51]

Book II, "Intelligence Activities and the Rights of Americans," contains the statistic on FBI investigations cited above and the committee's mandate, which includes "The origin and disposition of the so-called Huston Plan."[^52]

Book III, "Supplementary Detailed Staff Reports on Intelligence Activities and the Rights of Americans," contains the following sections: COINTELPRO (from page 1), the King case study (from page 81), the Black Panther Party (from page 185), informants (from page 225), warrantless FBI electronic surveillance (from page 271), black bag operations (from page 353), FBI domestic intelligence investigations (from page 373), domestic CIA and FBI mail opening (from page 559), the CHAOS program and the Office of Security (from page 679) and NSA surveillance affecting Americans (from page 733).[^53]

Book IV, "Supplementary Detailed Staff Reports on Foreign and Military Intelligence," describes at pages 128 and 129 the CIA's Program Branch 7 (PB/7), headed in the early years of the CIA by Army Colonel [[Boris Pash]], as a "special operations" unit within the [[Office of Policy Coordination]] with jurisdiction over assassinations and kidnappings. The committee staff concluded that the unit was assigned responsibility for assassinations and kidnappings if such operations were authorized, and stated that it had found no evidence that the unit carried out any. [[Howard Hunt]] told an interviewer, as the New York Times reported on December 26, 1975, that he was told in 1954 or 1955 that Pash was in charge of an assassination unit.[^54]

Book V, "The Investigation of the Assassination of President John F. Kennedy: Performance of the Intelligence Agencies," and Book VI, "Supplementary Reports on Intelligence Activities," complete the set.[^50]

### MKULTRA and behavioral research

The committee's inquiry into the CIA's behavioral-control research was hampered because the MKULTRA records had been destroyed in January 1973. Technical Services Division personnel destroyed them on Gottlieb's verbal orders. Gottlieb testified that he was carrying out a verbal order from Helms in doing so, and Helms confirmed that the order was his.[^51]

Gottlieb testified in secret about the assassination programs, under the alias "Joseph Scheider," the name the interim report gives the CIA scientist who took the toxic materials to the Congo. [[John D. Marks]] wrote that Gottlieb professed not to remember most of the behavioral-control details.[^55] Book I describes the program as "concerned with the research and development of chemical, biological, and radiological materials capable of employment in clandestine operations to control human behavior," records that the DCI, Allen Dulles, approved it on April 13, 1953 on a proposal from Assistant Deputy Director for Plans Helms, and records that over its ten-year life many "additional avenues to the control of human behavior" were designated for investigation under its charter, among them "radiation, electroshock, various fields of psychology, psychiatry, sociology, and anthropology, graphology, harassment substances, and paramilitary devices and materials."[^56]

The hearing volume on the storage of toxic agents disclosed the MKNAOMI program and the operations at Fort Detrick.[^57]

John D. Marks wrote that the only time in about twenty-five years that CIA officials sought [[White House]] approval for this work was a 1955 contribution of $375,000 toward a research building at [[Georgetown University Hospital]], which Dulles approved. Marks also wrote that a reference to the program in the Rockefeller Commission report led to the Freedom of Information request that surfaced the surviving boxes of MKULTRA financial records.[^58]

### Rockefeller Commission and Pike Committee

President Ford created the Rockefeller Commission (Commission on CIA Activities Within the United States, chaired by Vice President [[Nelson Rockefeller]]) by Executive Order 11828 on January 4, 1975, and it issued its report in June 1975.[^59] The National Security Archive has published documents showing that the White House removed an 86-page section on assassination plots from the commission's draft before publication, and that [[Richard Cheney]] made handwritten edits to the draft around June 1, 1975.[^60]

The House Select Committee on Intelligence, known as the Pike Committee after [[Otis Pike]], ran in parallel with the Church Committee in 1975 and 1976. The House established the committee by H. Res. 138 on February 19, 1975 and reconstituted it by H. Res. 591 on July 17, 1975.[^61] The papers of Richard Ober at the [[Hoover Institution]] list Pike Committee files alongside the Church Committee and Rockefeller Commission files.[^62]

### Successor committees and executive order

The Senate created the [[Senate Select Committee on Intelligence]] by S. Res. 400 of the 94th Congress, second session, introduced on March 1, 1976 and agreed to on May 19, 1976, to oversee intelligence activities and to make continuing studies of them.[^63] The [[House Permanent Select Committee on Intelligence]] followed as the House counterpart.[^64]

President Ford signed Executive Order 11905 on February 18, 1976. Section (g) provides that "No employee of the United States Government shall engage in, or conspire to engage in, political assassination."[^65] The Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Act of 1978 created a judicial framework for domestic national security surveillance.[^66]
