*Black Mass* was a film of a satanic mass produced and directed by the [[Philadelphia]] filmmaker [[R.C. Horsch|Ray Hoersch]], later known as R.C. Horsch. A midnight run at the [[Arcadia Theatre (Philadelphia)|Arcadia]] on Chestnut Street was cancelled in February 1975 after what the *[[The Philadelphia Inquirer|Philadelphia Inquirer]]* reported as pressure from the [[Roman Catholic Archdiocese of Philadelphia|Philadelphia Archdiocese]], and the film was screened instead at midnight at the [[Bandbox Theater]] in [[Germantown, Philadelphia|Germantown]] on Saturday, March 1, 1975.[^1] The *Inquirer* of February 28, 1975 announced the midnight showing at the Bandbox for that Saturday.[^1] On February 27, 1975, sixteen Christian and Jewish leaders of Greater Philadelphia, among them Cardinal [[John Krol]], issued a joint statement against its showing.[^2]

### Production

Ray Hoersch's earlier screenplays include *Resolved: There Is No Such Thing as Gravity (The Earth Sucks),* registered for copyright in his own name on April 1, 1970.[^3] In *[[Hoersch v. Froehlke]]*, decided by the [[United States District Court for the Eastern District of Pennsylvania]] on October 11, 1974, Hoersch challenged orders to involuntary active duty issued after unexcused absences from his unit of the [[Pennsylvania Army National Guard]]. The opinion records that he alleged the unit failed to consider his request to wear long hair "as required for his employment as a motion picture producer." The court found that he had been properly ordered to active duty and denied his petition.[^4]

The [[Religious News Service]] described the film as "reputed to be the only literal representation ever made of an authentic satanic rite," based on transcripts of the rite "obtained by producer-director Ray Hoersch, reportedly a former producer of pornographic films." The service reported that Hoersch "reportedly attempted to film the actual ceremony" and, failing that, obtained transcripts "including the mystical incantations." It called the film "more of a documentary than a 'scary story.'"[^5] Hoersch's own description, as the service printed it: "The Satanic Mass is the exact inverse of the Catholic ceremony. Every part of it is reversed in blasphemy, for its intention is a deliberate corruption, a parody of the Mass. Starting with the processional, the ritual uses a nude as the altar piece. The second section is a baptism, in which a girl dressed in white renounces her Christianity and seals her pact with the devil. And the third part is the Invocation to lust, you'll have to see that."[^5] The *Inquirer* printed the opening lines of the same description on February 28, calling the rite "the exact inverse of the Catholic ceremony" with "every part of it reversed in blasphemy."[^2]

In a 2026 broadcast of interviews recorded in 2013, Horsch called it "a literal Catholic black mass in Latin" that "kind of bombed," because "not many of the film-going audience at the time spoke fluid Latin." Asked why he chose to film a black mass, he said: "Something to do that was contrary to the law. I mean, something to get me into trouble."[^6] In the 2013 interview he said that he made the film from money earned on [[The Erotic Memoirs of a Male Chauvinist Pig|Erotic Memoirs]] and other films: "I took the money I made from Erotic Memoirs and a couple of the other films, and I made this unfortunate Black Mass film." He said that the film lost its money and left him in a deep depression.[^7]

Horsch said he still held the film rights to [[Anton LaVey]]'s *[[The Satanic Bible]]*, which he had bought for about 150 dollars.[^6][^7]

### The Arcadia Cancellation

On February 28, 1975, the society column of *The Philadelphia Inquirer* printed a notice that "'Black Mass,' the controversial film by Chestnut Hill filmmaker Ray Hoersch, will be shown at the Germantown Bandbox Theater this Saturday at midnight." The notice said that the film was "originally scheduled for a midnight showing at the Arcadia," and that it "was cancelled after reported pressure by the Philadelphia Archdiocese, according to the Bandbox's [[Art Carduner]], who gave the film a new home."[^1] The showing was to benefit the [[Friends of the Bandbox]], and the column reported that Hoersch would attend the opening.[^1]

The *Inquirer*'s news story of the same date, headed "Protest Raised Here On 'Black Mass' Film," reported that the film "reportedly was scheduled to open here this weekend" and that "it was doubtful Thursday night that the film would open." It had reportedly been scheduled in a center-city theater and one in Germantown. The center-city house said the report "was a mistake," and the Germantown theater said it was not yet known whether the movie would be shown there.[^2] The society column of the same date named the Arcadia as the original booking and the Archdiocese as the cause, in Carduner's account.[^1]

The Arcadia was a 500-seat house at 1529-1531 Chestnut Street in [[Center City, Philadelphia|Center City]], recorded by the [[Athenaeum of Philadelphia]] with the [[Sablosky Corporation]] and [[Frank DeLong]] among its clients.[^8]

In 2026 Horsch described the same events from his side: "It had one screening and then the Catholic Archdiocese in Philadelphia actually had the whole run of the film cancelled. They had quite a bit of clout back then."[^6]

### The Protest

The joint statement was issued on Thursday, February 27, 1975, and the *Inquirer* reported it the next morning as an effort by "Christian and Jewish leaders of Greater Philadelphia" to discourage the showing. The statement called the film "a frontal attack on the Sacred itself" that was "offensive to persons of all religious faiths."[^2]

Sixteen Catholic, Protestant, Orthodox and Jewish leaders of Philadelphia, led by Krol, signed the statement, which the Religious News Service printed in March 1975. It said that the film's content, "depicting an authentic satanic rite," was "a blasphemous attack on the religious rites held in sacred reverence by millions of Christian believers," and it urged "believers of every religious conviction to use every moral force to discourage the showing of this film in the Philadelphia area." The signers said the film was "obviously intended not to be viewed as a film, but as a blasphemous ritualistic observance," noted that plans called for it to be shown in one theater every Saturday at midnight, and asked everyone in the local film industry "to recognize the outrage being voiced by religious people of all faiths and to resist and reject every attempt to market or portray this film."[^2][^5]

The signers included Rabbi [[Charles Lacks]], president of the [[Greater Philadelphia Board of Rabbis]], and its executive director, Rabbi [[Harold Goldfarb]]; [[Francis G. Brown]], general secretary of the [[Philadelphia Yearly Meeting]] of the [[Religious Society of Friends]]; the Rev. [[William J. Brown]] of the Donegal Presbytery of the [[United Presbyterian Church in the United States of America|United Presbyterian Church]]; and Bishop [[E. L. Hickman]] of the First Episcopal District of the [[African Methodist Episcopal Church]]. Representatives of the Greek Orthodox community, the [[United Church of Christ]], the Pennsylvania Baptist Association, the Byzantine Ukrainian Rite, the United Methodists and the Metropolitan Christian Council of Philadelphia also signed. In an addendum the Board of Rabbis wrote that the film "not only constitutes an obscene travesty of Catholic sancta, but deeply outrages the religious sensitivities of all God fearing people," and that it saw "no redeeming social or aesthetic value whatever in 'Black Mass.'"[^5] The *Inquirer* attributed the "obscene travesty of Catholic sancta" sentence to Lacks and Goldfarb.[^2]

The Religious News Service dispatch named United Methodist Bishop [[James M. Ault]] of the Philadelphia Area among those opposing the film, and *The United Methodist Reporter* ran it on March 14, 1975, under the headline "U.M. bishop joins those against satanist movie."[^9]

The Archdiocese had used a movie boycott before. On May 23, 1934, [[Dennis Dougherty|Cardinal Dougherty]] called on the area's 800,000 Catholics to stay out of motion picture theaters and declared it sinful to enter one. Over 300,000 Catholics signed pledges to avoid the movies, and ticket sales fell about 20 percent. Dougherty did not release anyone from the pledge and never officially ended the boycott. In June 1934 the [[Motion Picture Association of America]] agreed to stronger self-censorship and set up the [[Production Code Administration]], headed by the Catholic layman [[Joseph Breen]], and the Catholic [[Legion of Decency]] grew in influence.[^10]

### The Bandbox

The Bandbox Theatre stood at 20-22 East Armat Street in Germantown and held 499 seats.[^11] In late 1969 the [[Philadelphia Department of Licenses and Inspections]] closed Hoersch's [[Underground Cinema 16]] at 2021 Sansom Street. The Bandbox then ran the Vietnam documentary [[In the Year of the Pig]], which Cinema 16 had under contract.[^12] The alternative weekly [[The Distant Drummer]] quoted the department's deputy commissioner, [[Leo Goldstein]], as saying: "I first heard about the theatre (Cinema 16) when I read in the BULLETIN that they were going to play that Vietnam film."[^12]

Art Carduner of the Bandbox, who gave the film its new home, booked the screening as a benefit for the Friends of the Bandbox, and Hoersch attended.[^1] The *Inquirer* column of February 28, 1975 announced the midnight showing and reported that Hoersch would attend the opening.[^1] Horsch's account is that the film "had one screening" before the run was cancelled.[^6] The column places the pressure on the Arcadia booking. Horsch places it on the whole run.

### Counterfeiting Arrest

On December 10, 1974, about eleven weeks before the Bandbox booking, [[Hatboro, Pennsylvania|Hatboro]] police apprehended Hoersch, then 31, after the Hatboro Card Shop on Old York Road reported a suspicious ten-dollar bill. The [[The Morning Call|Morning Call]] of [[Allentown, Pennsylvania|Allentown]] reported the next day that [[United States Secret Service|Secret Service]] agents under [[James D'Amelio]], searching his car under a warrant, had found $630 in counterfeit ten-dollar bills there, and that his driver's license and registration were also counterfeit. The paper reported that the printing equipment was in a small building on Taylor Street in [[Doylestown, Pennsylvania|Doylestown]], where [[Charles Griffith]] of [[Wallingford, Pennsylvania|Wallingford]] was arrested. Both men were committed to the [[Philadelphia Detention Center]] in default of bail. Authorities described Hoersch as unemployed, "who said he hadn't worked for 11 years."[^13]

The [[Associated Press]] carried the same arrests under the headline "Phony Bill Suspects Held": "Federal agents have arrested two men on counterfeiting charges and have seized $180,000 in phony $10 bills."[^14] The Morning Call headline gave the same $180,000 figure.[^13]

When Hoersch was sentenced on federal drug charges in 1985, the *Philadelphia Daily News* reported that his record included a 1974 counterfeiting conviction "which drew a two-year sentence" and a 1972 check-forging conviction in North Carolina.[^15]

Horsch later said that after the film he "totally lost interest in the porn business" and became a fashion photographer.[^6]
