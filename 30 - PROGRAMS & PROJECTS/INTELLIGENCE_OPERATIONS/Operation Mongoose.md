Operation Mongoose was the Kennedy administration's covert action program targeting [[Fidel Castro]]'s Cuba, initiated in the aftermath of the failed [[Bay of Pigs]] invasion and operating from approximately November 1961 until the Cuban Missile Crisis of October 1962 forced its suspension. It was the largest CIA covert action program since the Korean War, with Task Force W, the CIA component, employing approximately 400 people at CIA headquarters and its Miami station,[^1] several thousand Cuban exile agents and a budget approaching $50 million annually.[^2]

### Origins

The Bay of Pigs invasion of April 17-19, 1961 was a catastrophic failure: the CIA-trained Cuban exile brigade was captured within three days, the expected popular uprising against Castro did not materialize, and the operation had been run without adequate air support. President [[John F. Kennedy|Kennedy]] publicly accepted responsibility but was furious at CIA misjudgments. Attorney General [[Robert Kennedy]] became the primary administration driver for continuing pressure on Cuba.

In November 1961, Kennedy authorized Operation Mongoose as the successor to Bay of Pigs-era operations. The program was built around a different theory: rather than a direct invasion, Mongoose would use covert operations to create conditions for internal Cuban resistance that could eventually produce an uprising without requiring direct American military intervention.[^2]

### Command Structure

The [[Special Group]] (Augmented), chaired by General [[Maxwell Taylor]] and including [[McGeorge Bundy]] (National Security Adviser), CIA Director [[John McCone]], and senior State and Defense officials, provided policy oversight.[^3] General [[Edward Lansdale]], whose Philippines counterinsurgency success had made him the administration's preferred irregular warfare theorist, was appointed as operational director. The March 2, 1962 program review dates the designation as chief of operations to the November 30, 1961 directive.[^4] Robert Kennedy played an active role in the program, a role unrelated to his position as attorney general.[^3]

The CIA's operational implementation was headquartered at [[JM-WAVE|JM/WAVE]], a large covert station at the University of Miami's South Campus. [[William Harvey]], head of the CIA's [[ZR RIFLE|ZR/RIFLE]] assassination planning program, was the CIA's project officer for Mongoose and chief of [[Task Force W]], the agency's component of the program.[^5][^6] The Senate interim report records that Harvey and the chief of the JM/WAVE station rented the truck used to deliver the Roselli equipment.[^7] The station's cover included a legitimate South Florida business presence and several front companies.[^2]

### Operations

Mongoose operations fell into several categories:

Sabotage: CIA and Cuban exile teams conducted multiple sabotage operations against Cuban economic targets, including sugar mills, oil facilities, and transportation infrastructure. The sabotage program was intended to create economic disruption that would increase popular dissatisfaction with Castro's government.

Resistance development: CIA officers and Cuban exile operatives attempted to recruit and organize internal resistance networks inside Cuba. These efforts were largely unsuccessful; Castro's state security (G2) proved highly effective at detecting and penetrating infiltrated networks.

Psychological operations: Radio broadcasts, leaflet drops, and propaganda campaigns targeted both the Cuban population and Castro's military and political leadership.

Assassination tracks: Parallel to Mongoose's official programs, Harvey maintained ZR/RIFLE operations targeting Castro through the CIA-organized crime connection with [[Johnny Roselli]] and, through him, [[Sam Giancana]] and [[Santos Trafficante, Jr.|Santo Trafficante Jr.]] The assassination track was technically separate from but operationally intertwined with the broader Mongoose program.[^2]

The [[Joint Chiefs of Staff]] were drawn into Mongoose's planning in early 1962. On March 6, 1962, Edward Lansdale wrote to Goodwin that he was asking the Defense representative, General Craig, "to give me a brief but precise description of pretexts which the JCS believes desirable if a decision is ultimately made to use direct military intervention."[^8] The Joint Chiefs' reply of March 13, 1962, signed by Chairman [[Lyman Lemnitzer]], assumed that "a single agency will be given the primary responsibility for developing military and para-military aspects of the basic plan" and recommended that "this responsibility for both overt and covert military operations be assigned the Joint Chiefs of Staff."[^9] The JCS history records that the list covered attacks and sabotage at Guantanamo, a blown-up unmanned ship, faked assassination attempts and bombings in Florida and Washington, a sinking of a boatload of Cuban refugees, a staged filibuster, the downing of a civilian airliner that was in fact a drone, and the apparent shootdown of a U.S. Air Force fighter.[^10]

Lansdale's review of the first phase, dated July 25, 1962, reported that recruitment of third-country nationals and the start of Cuban collection nets had made Mongoose "numerically the largest U.S. intelligence agent effort inside a Communist state."[^11] Taylor's memorandum to the president of August 17, 1962 described the program as generating the largest intelligence effort directed at any Soviet Bloc country.[^12] Harvey's memorandum of July 24, 1962 stated that as of July 23, 477 CIA staff personnel were devoting full time to the effort.[^13] The Church Committee put the total staff of Task Force W at approximately 400 people at CIA headquarters and its Miami station.[^3]

On January 19, 1962 Lansdale added Task 33, a plan to incapacitate Cuban sugar workers during the harvest by chemical means. Lansdale testified that the plan involved nonlethal chemicals meant to keep workers away from the fields for a 24 to 48 hour period. The task was initially approved for planning, with the notation that it would require a policy determination.[^14]

In 1975 Parrott, an assistant to the president's military representative, testified about a plan that he attributed to Lansdale: spreading word that the Second Coming of Christ was imminent, with an American submarine surfacing off Cuba to send up starshells as the sign. Parrott said that somebody dubbed the plan "Elimination by Illumination."[^15]

On February 20, 1962 Lansdale detailed a six-phase schedule designed to culminate in October 1962 with "open revolt and overthrow of the Communist regime." For its "Resistance" phase he listed "attacks on the cadre of the regime, including key leaders," noting that "Gangster elements might provide the best recruitment potential for actions against police G-2 officials." Lansdale testified that the "actions" and "attack" meant killing.[^16] On January 30, 1962 Brig. Gen. William Craig forwarded to Lansdale a concept called Operation Bounty, a scale of rewards for killing or delivering alive known Communists, with 2 cents listed for Castro; Lansdale testified that he tabled it and never brought it before the Special Group (Augmented).[^17]

### The Missile Crisis and Suspension

On April 5, 1962 the Special Group concluded that the time had come to re-examine Mongoose's basic philosophy "with particular reference to the possibility of finding a pretext for early... intervention." Maxwell Taylor put two questions to the Service Chiefs, whether the United States should invade Cuba and whether it had the capacity to do so. Five days later (April 10) the Joint Chiefs answered yes to both.[^10] Two days after that, on April 12, John McCone recorded that a JCS paper on pretext or provocative actions had been discussed at the April 11 Special Group (Augmented) meeting, and wrote that it was "a very thoughtful and useful document."[^18]

The October 1962 [[Cuban Missile Crisis]] fundamentally changed the operational environment. Kennedy's agreement with Soviet Premier [[Nikita Khrushchev|Khrushchev]] to resolve the crisis included a private U.S. commitment not to invade Cuba. This commitment effectively foreclosed the political precondition on which Mongoose was premised: that internal Cuban resistance could ultimately trigger an American-supported uprising.

During the crisis, Harvey gave an order sending agent teams into Cuba to support any conventional U.S. military operation that might occur. Harvey testified that Robert Kennedy "took a great deal of exception" to the order, and that McCone then ordered the agent operations stopped.[^7] Evan Thomas wrote that Robert Kennedy demanded Harvey's removal.[^2] Harvey was appointed chief of station at Rome effective on or about June 30, 1963.[^19]

Mongoose was formally suspended in late 1962. Subsequent anti-Castro covert operations continued under different organizational structures and with reduced resources, but the large-scale coordinated program of 1961-1962 was not reconstituted.

### Significance

Operation Mongoose occupied a significant place in Cold War history for several reasons. It demonstrated the Kennedy administration's willingness to pursue regime change through covert means on a large scale even after the Bay of Pigs disaster had publicly discredited the approach. It institutionalized the CIA-organized crime anti-Castro connection that became entangled with JFK assassination conspiracy theories. And its suspension terms - the no-invasion pledge - provided the diplomatic framework within which Cuba's revolutionary government survived for decades.[^20]

The [[Church Committee]] investigations of 1975-1976 disclosed the assassination planning component of Mongoose, revealing that the CIA had maintained assassination plots against Castro through multiple administrations and had kept this information from the [[Warren Commission]] investigating the Kennedy assassination.
