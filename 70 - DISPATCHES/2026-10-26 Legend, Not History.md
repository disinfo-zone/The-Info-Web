# Legend, Not History

In June 1861 [[Arthur M. Eastman]] bought 5,400 Hall's carbines from the Ordnance Bureau at $3.50 each. He sold 5,000 to [[Simon Stevens]] at $12.50, and Stevens sold them to Major General [[John C. Frémont]] at $22.[^1] The House select committee on government contracts, reporting through [[Elihu B. Washburne]], found that "the arms were placed under the control of J. Pierpont Morgan to secure him the payment of $20,000 he had advanced to Stevens."[^2] Stevens testified: "I got them from J. Pierpont Morgan, to send to General Fremont." He said Morgan was to be refunded the $20,000 with interest "and to be paid for his services and expenses in attending to the sale and collection of the money as trustee for all parties concerned."[^3]

Captain [[Franklin D. Callender]] of the St. Louis arsenal testified on October 26, 1861: "According to the best information I have, J. Pierpont Morgan sold them to the government." He produced a bill dated August 7, 1861, headed "The United States to J. Pierpont Morgan, Dr.," for 2,500 carbines at $55,550. It was marked paid on September 10, with a receipt signed "J. PIERPONT MORGAN, By his attorney, J. T. HOWARD." A second bill in Morgan's name for $58,175 was marked "Not paid for want of funds."[^4] The committee valued the arm at "very nearly $12 50" and found that the claim sought "some $49,000 over and above the value of the property sold."[^5] The War Department commission of [[Joseph Holt]] and [[Robert Dale Owen]] docketed the claim as Case No. 97 in Morgan's name.[^6]

The standard account of the affair is *The Hall Carbine Affair: A Study in Contemporary Folklore*, printed in 1941 in an edition of 100 copies and revised in 1948 in an edition of 750. Its preface states that, "insofar as the case against Morgan is concerned," the transaction "is legend, not history."[^7] Its own reckoning, from [[Morris Ketchum]]'s statement to the committee, is that Morgan retained $26,343.54 of the voucher's proceeds, including a $5,400 commission, and passed $28,650.96 to Ketchum on September 16, 1861.[^8] The author was [[R. Gordon Wasson]], a vice president of [[J. P. Morgan & Co.]][^9]

Wasson's 1956 mushroom expedition to Huautla de Jiménez was joined by the chemist James Moore and drew a $2,000 contribution from the [[Geschickter Fund for Medical Research]], a conduit for the CIA's [[Project MKUltra|MKULTRA]] program.[^10]
