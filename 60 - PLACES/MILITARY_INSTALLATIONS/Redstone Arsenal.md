Redstone Arsenal is a [[U.S. Army]] installation in [[Huntsville, Alabama]], in Madison County. The Army took possession of the site in July 1941 for a chemical munitions plant named [[Huntsville Arsenal]], which was built for the [[Chemical Warfare Service]]. The adjoining [[Ordnance Corps]] assembly plant, the [[Redstone Ordnance Plant]], was redesignated Redstone Arsenal on February 26, 1943.[^1][^2] After [[Wernher von Braun]] and his team arrived from [[Fort Bliss]] in 1950 the post became the Army's rocket and missile center. The [[Marshall Space Flight Center|George C. Marshall Space Flight Center]] of [[NASA]] has occupied part of the site since 1960. The post also housed the [[U.S. Army Missile Command]] (MICOM), the [[Missile Intelligence Agency]] and its successor, the [[Missile and Space Intelligence Center]] (MSIC), which has been a component of the [[Defense Intelligence Agency]] since 1992.[^3]

### Huntsville Arsenal and the Redstone Ordnance Plant, 1941 to 1949

Congress approved funds in April 1941 for a chemical manufacturing and storage facility to supplement the Chemical Warfare Service's only chemical plant at [[Edgewood Arsenal]], Maryland. The site on the southwestern edge of Huntsville was announced on July 3, 1941. The Army took possession of the first tract of 160 acres on July 28, 1941, and the first commanding officer, COL Rollo C. Ditto, arrived on August 4 and broke ground.[^1] On July 8, 1941 the War Department announced a $6 million Ordnance Corps assembly plant to operate beside the chemical arsenal.[^1]

During the Second World War the arsenal made smoke, incendiary and gel-type chemical munitions. The Army chronology credits it with more than 27 million items of chemical munitions, valued at more than $134.5 million.[^4] The ordnance plant was placed on standby on February 24, 1947, and on September 30, 1947 Huntsville Arsenal received verbal notification that it was being declared excess.[^5] In October 1948 the Chief of Ordnance designated Redstone Arsenal as the center for Ordnance rocket research and development.[^6] The arsenal was officially reactivated as the site of the Ordnance Rocket Center on June 1, 1949, and Huntsville Arsenal ceased to exist as a separate installation at 2400 hours on June 30, 1949.[^7] Under General Orders No. 19, dated April 1, 1950, the Department of the Army discontinued Huntsville Arsenal and consolidated its land and facilities with Redstone Arsenal. On July 1, 1950 the former Huntsville Arsenal became the Chemical Division of Redstone Arsenal.[^8]

### The Fort Bliss group and the missile era, 1950 to 1955

On October 28, 1949 the Secretary of the Army approved the transfer of the Ordnance Research and Development Division Sub-Office (Rocket) from Fort Bliss, Texas, to Redstone Arsenal. Among those transferred were Wernher von Braun and his team of German scientists and technicians, who the Army chronology says had come to the United States under Operation Paperclip during 1945 and 1946.[^7][^9] The Army chronology says the arrival of the Fort Bliss group began on April 15, 1950, that a staff of Army officers and about 120 German scientists arrived from Fort Bliss in April 1950, and that with the arrival the post officially entered the missile era.[^8] The sub-office was redesignated the Ordnance Guided Missile Center on the same date. Some members of the team were housed in apartments in the northeast section of the arsenal.[^8] The Encyclopedia of Alabama describes the transfer as coming from the White Sands Proving Grounds in New Mexico and dates the [[Operation Paperclip]] move to 1950.[^10] NASA's Marshall Space Flight Center history office states that near the end of 1945 more than 100 Germans who had agreed to come under Project Paperclip arrived at Fort Bliss, and a NASA biography of von Braun says he and an initial group of about 125 were installed at Fort Bliss before the move to Alabama.[^11]

The team's first missile work was the [[Redstone missile|Redstone]] missile. On January 16, 1953 the Guided Missiles Development Branch was redesignated the Guided Missile Development Division, with von Braun as its chief. The first Redstone research and development missile was flight tested on August 20, 1953.[^12]

### The Army Ballistic Missile Agency, Jupiter and Explorer 1, 1956 to 1960

The [[Army Ballistic Missile Agency]] (ABMA) was established at Redstone Arsenal on February 1, 1956 as a Class II activity under the Chief of Ordnance, with the military mission of fielding the Army's first intermediate range ballistic missile.[^13] On February 9, 1956 the 283d Ordnance Detachment (Technical Intelligence) was assigned to Redstone Arsenal and attached to the Ordnance Missile Laboratory for operational control.[^14] On October 19, 1957 the Secretary of Defense directed the ABMA commander to prepare for [[Jupiter]] initial operational capability production. On November 22, 1957 the Office of the Secretary of Defense instructed the Army to launch two Jupiter C satellites carrying the cosmic radiation package prepared by [[James Van Allen]].[^15]

On January 31, 1958 a Jupiter C missile (RS-29) placed [[Explorer 1]] into orbit, launched from Cape Canaveral, Florida, at 10:48 p.m. Eastern Standard Time according to NASA.[^15][^16] The Army chronology notes that the Navy did not launch a Vanguard satellite until March 17, 1958.[^15] Another Jupiter C launch placed Explorer III in orbit on March 26, 1958, and Explorer IV followed on July 26, 1958.[^15] President [[Dwight D. Eisenhower|Eisenhower]] approved the transfer of the Army agency's scientists and engineers to the newly created NASA on October 21, 1958.[^13] On July 1, 1960 AOMC and ABMA lost all of their space missions, about 4,000 civilian employees and $100 million in buildings and equipment at Redstone and Cape Canaveral. The George C. Marshall Space Flight Center opened at Redstone Arsenal on the same date, and Eisenhower dedicated it on September 8, 1960.[^17] The encyclopedia states that by the time of the Marshall transfer von Braun and 102 other German-born scientists had become U.S. citizens.[^10]

### The Missile Command and the Missile Intelligence Agency, 1960 to 1985

An Assistant Chief of Staff for Missile Intelligence joined Army Ordnance Missile Command (AOMC) headquarters at Redstone on April 1, 1960, by the Army chronology's date; a July 1985 Missile Intelligence Agency recruiting brochure gives March 31, 1960 for the redesignation of the Technical Intelligence Division, then about 50 people.[^17][^18] The brochure traces the agency to the Army Ballistic Missile Agency of February 1956, whose commander, MG [[John B. Medaris]], established an Army Special Security Office in June 1956 and a six-person Technical Intelligence Division of engineers and intelligence analysts. It records the redesignation of the activity (about 75 people) as the Directorate of Missile Intelligence on August 1, 1962, its designation as one of six major production elements of the Defense Intelligence Agency in 1967, and its redesignation by the Army Materiel Command as the Missile Intelligence Agency, a special activity at the Missile Command, on June 17, 1970.[^18] On June 29, 1960 the Operations Division, Missile Intelligence, contracted with [[Aerojet General Corporation]] for a technical evaluation of the Soviet 150mm surface-to-surface missile known in the West as the SCUD. The study was completed on May 18, 1961.[^17] The Missile Intelligence Office revised that study in the first half of 1962 and submitted it to the Ordnance Technical Intelligence Agency and the Assistant Chief of Staff for Intelligence.[^19]

The U.S. Army Missile Command was established at Redstone on May 23, 1962 and activated on August 1, 1962, when AOMC ceased to exist.[^13] In the October 1962 Cuban missile crisis, representatives of MICOM's Missile Intelligence Agency provided the missile expertise needed to identify the type, completeness and threat of the missile bases under construction.[^20] On April 30, 1985 the Army Chief of Staff directed the [[Army Intelligence Agency]] to assume command of the Missile Intelligence Agency, which had operated as a separate activity under the MICOM commander. On August 1, 1985, by order of the Secretary of the Army, the Missile Intelligence Agency was redesignated the U.S. Army Missile and Space Intelligence Center (MSIC).[^21] A June 1993 message on a Scientific and Technical Intelligence Committee meeting lists "DIRMSIC REDSTONE ARS AL" among its addressees.[^22] A Defense Intelligence Agency briefing dated December 2025 gives the center's lineage as the Army Ballistic Missile Agency (Army research and development) in 1956, the Army Intelligence Agency in 1985 and the Defense Intelligence Agency in 1992.[^23]

On July 17, 1997 the U.S. Army Aviation and Missile Command (AMCOM) was formed on a provisional basis by merging the Aviation and Troop Command with MICOM. The Army Materiel Command made the arrangement permanent effective October 1, 1997.[^24]

### Grill Flame at Redstone, 1977 to 1980

Army notes on the [[Grill Flame]] program, undated, record that the Missile Research and Development Command at Redstone had an $80 thousand contract with the [[Stanford Research Institute]] from August 1977 to August 1978. The work was sponsored by a sub-element of that command, the Missile Intelligence Agency, described as a GDIP unit, whose interest was in psychokinesis.[^25] A June 1978 memorandum to Major [[William Stoner]] of the Army staff, referring to an Assistant Chief of Staff for Intelligence message, gives the background as the agency's contractual involvement with SRI on Soviet strategic defensive missile technologies. It states that the agency learned of the work of [[Harold Puthoff]] and [[Russell Targ]] at SRI in mid-1976, invited them to the agency for discussions in late 1976 and early 1977, and implemented an SRI task in August 1977 under contract DAAK40-77-C-0170, using research and development funds of about $60,000, whose statement of work was titled "Investigation of Unconventional Discrimination Techniques."[^26] The December 1979 report of the [[Grill Flame Scientific Evaluation Committee]] gives the sequence as an informal MICOM expression of interest in 1976 in U.S. replication of claimed Soviet experiments and a one-year $80 thousand MICOM contract of August 1977 under the same title, and says the interest was in Soviet efforts at the man-machine interface, such as radar operators who continued to guide antiaircraft missiles to the target while the images on their screens were obscured by chaff.[^27] A December 11, 1978 letter from the [[Army Materiel Systems Analysis Activity]] at [[Aberdeen Proving Ground]], transmitting Grill Flame information, was addressed to the Commander, Missile Intelligence Agency, at Redstone Arsenal.[^28]

A memorandum for the record of a November 28, 1978 meeting at Army Materiel Development and Readiness Command headquarters lists [[Randy Clinton]] of the Missile Intelligence Agency, [[John Kramar]] of AMSAA and Hugh Green of the Missile Laboratories among the attendees. The memorandum records that the [[United States Army Intelligence and Security Command|INSCOM]], AMSAA and Missile Laboratories programs each briefed their involvement, and that it was agreed no one was out of bounds and that the programs meshed well. It states that the Missile Intelligence Agency had no current contract but was interested in the psychokinesis aspects, and its author wrote that he had received the distinct impression that Charles Carter was more or less satelliting himself onto the sister organization at Redstone, the Missile Laboratories. Green briefed a Missile Laboratories proposal for a psychokinesis switching device.[^29] An undated Army briefing on the psychoenergetic effort states that the Missile Research and Development Command at Redstone was developing a pilot remote perturbation program for psychokinesis, relying on three noise sources (an electronic noise diode, a radioactive beta particle decay source and, as a control, a pseudo-random shift register) wired through an LSI-11 microprocessor. It states that the project was then under the direction of the Missile Intelligence Agency and would shift to the command's research and development laboratories, with a $118,000 contract to SRI for hardware in the first phase, an initial $90,000 for a second phase and up to $190,000 more available.[^30] On April 3, 1979 Dr. [[Walter LaBerge|LaBerge]] was briefed on the Redstone psychokinesis portion of the program and asked that this part of the work begin promptly.[^31] On May 3, 1979 Charles Carter of the Missile Intelligence Agency briefed the Grill Flame committee on efforts at Redstone Arsenal.[^32]

The protocol that followed is titled "Remote Perturbation Techniques: Project Description and Experimental Protocol" and is dated November 7, 1979. It was prepared by the USAMICOM Grill Flame Oversight Committee, with approval blocks for William C. McCorkle, director of the Systems Simulation and Development Directorate, and Richard L. Hartman, director of the Research Directorate, both of the US Army Missile Laboratory, and for Rankin A. Clinton Jr., deputy director of the US Army Missile Intelligence Agency. The principal investigator is listed as Dr. B. Z. Jenkins of the MICOM Systems Simulation and Development Directorate at Redstone Arsenal; the minutes of the DoD Grill Flame Committee meeting of November 27, 1979 list Billy Z. Jenkins, Charles Carter and Bill Johnson of the Missile Command among the attendees.[^33][^34] The Scientific Evaluation Committee's schedule lists a visit to the Missile Research and Development Command at Redstone Arsenal on July 26, 1979, with Jenkins and Clinton as contacts.[^27] The protocol describes a random binary source that participants try to bias by mental effort, and an identical experiment to be run at SRI with separately acquired equipment, as a check against manipulation and for repeatability. The MICOM experiment was to take place in Building 7770 on Redstone Arsenal. The second phase was to last six months and use no more than nine experienced remote viewers.[^33] The committee's December 1979 report includes a section of comments on the MICOM investigations, among them that the protocol offered a ready explanation of positive results at SRI and negative results at MICOM.[^27]

A paper of findings in the Grill Flame file recommended that the research efforts "to be terminated in their present forms include efforts at MICOM, AMSAA, and FTD."[^35] A Defense Intelligence Agency review prepared in February 1989 lists the Missile Intelligence Agency program with $281 thousand for 1977 to 1980. It describes two phases: a computer-based binary random number generator built in Phase I, and subject trials in Phase II.[^36] The review states that when all trials were completed the SRI investigators concluded there was an anomalous, unexplained effect on the electronic system that could not be accounted for by engineering considerations only, and gives two possible mechanisms: that the subject, without physical intervention, produced a physical change in the electronic system, or that the subject scanned the unperturbed binary sequence ahead in time and selected the time to initiate the trial. It adds that the data from these experiments were inconclusive.[^36]

An article by [[John A. Swets]] and [[Robert A. Bjork]], summarizing a National Research Council study of unconventional techniques considered by the Army, reports that little or no scientific evidence was found to support remote viewing or psychokinesis. The article also mentions an experiment underway at Redstone Arsenal to test mental practice as a component of training for soldering electronic circuits.[^37]

### Later tenants, 2004 to 2025

The [[Federal Bureau of Investigation]] Hazardous Devices School has operated at Redstone since 1971, and the FBI's executive assistant director opened its new facility on September 13, 2004; the school was run jointly by the FBI and the Army until September 2017, when the FBI accepted primary responsibility.[^38] The FBI's Terrorist Explosive Device Analytical Center (TEDAC), formally established in December 2004, was welcomed to Redstone Arsenal on February 16, 2016 at a ribbon-cutting attended by FBI Director [[James Comey]] and Senator [[Richard Shelby]], after relocating from the FBI Laboratory at Quantico, Virginia.[^39] On November 21, 2022 the FBI held a ribbon-cutting for its North Campus at Redstone, where it had completed more than 30 projects in three years; the Operations Building was to house over 1,300 personnel from a dozen FBI divisions.[^40] The Encyclopedia of Alabama reports a November 2018 announcement of plans to move more jobs to the arsenal, the clearing of 90 acres in 2024 for an academic zone, and a campus of nearly 2,000 employees.[^41]

The 2005 Defense Base Closure and Realignment Commission approved Recommendation 134, "Co-locate Missile and Space Defense Agencies," which moved [[Missile Defense Agency]] functions from leased space in Virginia to Redstone Arsenal, with an agency headquarters command center at Fort Belvoir, Virginia, and excepted the Ballistic Missile Defense System Sensors Directorate and the Command and Control Battle Management and Communications Directorate from the move to Redstone. It also relocated the headquarters component of the [[U.S. Army Space and Missile Defense Command]] from Arlington, Virginia to Redstone. The Defense Department's justification states that the Von Braun 2 building, due in fiscal year 2007, would house about 800 MDA personnel.[^42] Recommendation 148 relocated the [[Army Materiel Command]] and the Security Assistance Command from Fort Belvoir to Redstone, to collocate with the Aviation and Missile Command.[^42] A May 16, 2011 Army article gave July 17, 2011 as the target date for completing the transfer of Army Materiel Command headquarters from Fort Belvoir.[^43] The Encyclopedia of Alabama puts the BRAC moves of the two commands at about 4,600 jobs and the Missile Defense Agency transfer at more than 2,400 positions.[^41] President [[Donald Trump]] announced on September 2, 2025 that the headquarters of [[U.S. Space Command]] would move from Colorado Springs to Redstone; on April 29, 2026 the command's commander, Gen. [[Stephen Whiting]], announced operational control of its first facility on the arsenal, which houses the Joint Intelligence Support Element.[^44]

### Materiel exploitation at Redstone

The Missile and Space Intelligence Center describes its mission in a December 2025 briefing as "Provide premier technical and foundational analysis of foreign weapon systems to prevent and decisively win wars."[^23] The same briefing lists 52 buildings on Redstone Arsenal, 341,000 square feet of workspace and 305 acres under management, with the Shelby Center completed in 1999. It records about 15,000 square feet of contractor secure space supporting the center, growing to about 43,000 square feet, and a planned Material Exploitation Center of 102,000 square feet.[^23] The briefing lists "Foreign Material Exploitation" among five areas of the Contract Operations for Missile Evaluation and Testing (COMET), described as a 10-year contract with a $14 billion ceiling.[^23]

On August 25, 2026 the Defense Intelligence Agency held a ribbon-cutting for the nearly 92,000 square foot Materiel Exploitation Center. Congress allocated more than $150 million for the first phase, which includes nine high bays for the exploitation of large foreign weapons systems. MSIC chief [[Steven Rush]] said the center serves analysis of more than 700 weapons systems. The DIA deputy director said much of the work there would be too sensitive to describe.[^45]

### A 1984 observation in a private paper

In a paper dated April 24, 1984 titled "STAR WARS Now!", [[T. E. Bearden]] included a table of observations that places a "twin giant radials" system at Redstone Arsenal, Alabama, on Friday, January 6, 1984, and a figure titled "Twin giant radial pattern" for Huntsville, Alabama, of the same date. Bearden wrote that one major signature had been photographed over Huntsville by the author. The table gives the time as 0700 to 0745, with multiple observers, clear weather except for one system moving west at 20 to 30 miles per hour, and remnants of a second twin giant radial system seen in the same area from 1130 to 1145 hours. The paper's subject is the Bohm-Aharonov effect and what its author calls Soviet weaponization of scalar interferometry.[^46]
