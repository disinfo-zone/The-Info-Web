Pennsylvania is a state of the United States whose capital is [[Harrisburg, Pennsylvania|Harrisburg]] and whose largest city, [[Philadelphia]], has its own page.[^5] The state bodies include the [[Pennsylvania Crime Commission]], the state appellate courts, the [[Pennsylvania State Police]], the [[Pennsylvania Army National Guard]], the statewide investigating grand juries and the governor's office, together with the federal courts in the state and the industrial and research sites of the western and central counties, among them the [[Three Mile Island]] plant near Harrisburg, the uranium plant at [[Apollo, Pennsylvania|Apollo]] and the village of [[Kecksburg incident|Kecksburg]] in [[Westmoreland County, Pennsylvania|Westmoreland County]].

### Courts and the Crime Commission

The [[Superior Court of Pennsylvania|Superior Court]], established in 1895 to hear appeals from the Courts of Common Pleas, and the [[Commonwealth Court of Pennsylvania|Commonwealth Court]] are the two statewide intermediate appellate courts, and the [[Supreme Court of Pennsylvania]] sits above them.[^6] The Supreme Court's decisions and the Superior Court's rulings on the Philadelphia prosecutions recur in the record of the [[Garzone Funeral Home]] and the Garzone brothers.

The Pennsylvania Crime Commission was created as a fact-finding agency within the Pennsylvania Department of Justice. The Crime Commission Act was signed into law on July 31, 1968, the same day that an executive order made the Commission the state's criminal justice planning agency for federal block grants under the Omnibus Crime Control and Safe Streets Act of 1968.[^7] The Commission issued two reports on police corruption and organized crime: the March 1974 report on the Philadelphia police and the 1990 report on organized crime, titled *Organized Crime in Pennsylvania: A Decade of Change*, which opens by quoting the 1967 federal task force recommendation that states with organized crime groups create investigation commissions with independent, permanent status, adequate investigators and subpoena powers.[^8]

### Police corruption in Philadelphia, 1974

The Commission's March 1974 report found that corruption in the Philadelphia Police Department was "ongoing, widespread, systematic, and occurring at all levels of the Police Department." Corrupt practices were uncovered "in every police district," and the report identified more than 400 individual officers, by first name, last initial and badge or payroll number, as having received improper payments in cash and merchandise from gamblers, racketeers, bar owners and after-hours club operators.[^9] Its Locust Street chapter records that the owner of the [[Why Not Lounge]], Irvin Goltzer, was told by his manager, identified as Mr. LaRussa, to pay or "they will bust the joint," that payments went to the inspector of the Central Division, and that Goltzer made tape recordings of payoffs for the Commission.[^10][^11] The report's chapters also cover the After-Hours Clubs, the Road Drivers Association, the Liberty Clown Club, the Randolph Social Club and the Croatian Club.[^12] The bars, the manager [[Salvatore La Russa]] and the later history of the police inquiry are set out on the Philadelphia page.

### The Cosa Nostra families of the 1990 report

The Commission's 1990 report divides the state's [[La Cosa Nostra]] between three families: the Bruno/Scarfo family in the Philadelphia area, the [[Bufalino Crime Family]] in the northeast and the LaRocca/Genovese family in Pittsburgh and western Pennsylvania.[^13] Its summary states that the Philadelphia family, "known for its reckless use of violence and powerful corruptive influence," had been seriously weakened by a cooperative law enforcement effort "unprecedented in the history of Pennsylvania"; that its leader, [[Nicodemo Scarfo]], had been imprisoned for life; and that six members, including the underboss, had become protected government witnesses.[^14]

In the northeast the report places Russell Bufalino, "the aging boss of the Bufalino Family," in federal prison from 1981 to 1989 and states that the family "has become a second-rate operation" that would likely be merged with another family.[^14] The report traces the family's origin in Pittston through Stephen LaTorre, who came to the United States in the late 1800s and died in 1984; records that the Kefauver Committee's 1951 report focused on gambling in the Scranton area; names the family member John Parente as the dominant figure in illegal gambling in the Lehigh Valley until his death in 1978; and describes the Scranton video poker proprietor Elie "Al" Baldassari as a close friend of Russell Bufalino and records the July 1990 FBI extortion arrest of Elmo Baldassari.[^15] [[Cheri Seymour]] wrote that the family's name surfaced in FBI wiretaps of [[Music Corporation of America]] run by the Los Angeles Organized Crime Strike Force, in calls between [[Robert Booth Nichols]] and [[Eugene Giaquinto]].[^16][^17]

In the west the report states that with the death of John LaRocca in 1984 leadership passed to Michael Genovese, that Genovese's confidant Charles Porter was convicted of racketeering and sentenced to 20 years, and that from the information available the organization "remains the most powerful Cosa Nostra Family in Pennsylvania today."[^14] The summary also states that the 1980s were marked by the use of the federal Racketeer Influenced and Corrupt Organizations Act, which Pennsylvania had adopted in a version of its own.[^14] The Philadelphia family under Scarfo, the Third Circuit's account of the 1988 racketeering trial and the methamphetamine trade are set out on the Philadelphia page.

### The Garzone Funeral Home and the tissue trade

The Philadelphia cadaver case runs through the state courts. The Supreme Court of Pennsylvania recorded that the brothers Gerald and Louis Garzone, with James McCafferty, supplied at least 244 cadavers to [[Michael Mastromarino]] of [[Biomedical Tissue Services]] from early 2004 to September 2005 for over $245,000, and incinerated their records days before [[Food and Drug Administration]] investigators arrived.[^18] The Superior Court upheld Mastromarino's sentence of twenty-five to fifty-eight years in 2010; Louis Garzone pleaded guilty on September 2, 2008, the day his trial was to begin; and on April 13, 2010 the Superior Court vacated the salary portion of the costs of prosecution ordered against Gerald Garzone.[^2][^3][^19] The funeral home, the plea terms and the Brooklyn prosecution are set out on the Philadelphia page and on the Garzone Funeral Home page.

### Hoersch v. Froehlke and the National Guard

On October 11, 1974, the [[United States District Court for the Eastern District of Pennsylvania]] decided [[Hoersch v. Froehlke]], a suit by Raymond C. Hoersch against the Secretary of the Army, [[Robert F. Froehlke]]. The opinion was written by Judge [[John B. Hannum]].[^20][^21] Hannum had sat on the Superior Court of Pennsylvania by appointment before President [[Richard Nixon]] nominated him to the federal bench on March 24, 1969.[^21] The court recorded that Hoersch had enlisted in the Pennsylvania Army National Guard in 1965, that he was taken off the rolls of the Guard in July 1970 by an order to active duty as an Army reservist, and that he was absent without leave from [[Fort Dix]] from July 19, 1970. He returned to military control there on April 5, 1972, and went absent again the next day.[^20]

Hoersch argued that the Guard had failed to consider his request to wear long hair "as required for his employment as a motion picture producer." The court held that the unit commander's discretion still controlled an individual's appearance, even where a reservist showed that long hair contributed to his livelihood.[^20] The court quoted the opinion in *Harris v. Kaine*, 352 F. Supp. 769 (S.D.N.Y. 1972), that as long as the reservist "presents a neat and soldierly appearance and can perform all the duties required of him, the Army has no authority to order his hair cut, absent a showing of need."[^20] The Philadelphia filmmaker Ray Hoersch, later known as [[R.C. Horsch]], made the film [[Black Mass (1975 film)|Black Mass]], which ran in the city in 1975.[^22] A Raymond C. Hoersch, an engineer, is named as the filmmaker's father, and the plaintiff shares his name with both.

### Holmesburg Prison and the Army's tests

[[Holmesburg Prison]] in northeast Philadelphia was the site of the experiments of [[Albert Kligman]], a University of Pennsylvania dermatologist who began testing incarcerated men there in 1951 for companies and for the Army; the program ran 23 years and the city banned medical testing at Holmesburg in 1974.[^23] Allen M. Hornblum's history of the program is [[Acres of Skin]].[^24] The Department of Defense general counsel, [[Deanne C. Siemer]], told the Senate in 1977 of the Edgewood compound EA-3167: "In 1971, the Agency transferred some $37,000 to Edgewood to pursue testing of this compound, which was designated EA-3167," a project begun in 1971 and terminated in 1973.[^25] The Army's list of Edgewood drug-testing contracts printed in the 1975 Senate hearings records volunteer testing at Holmesburg.[^26]

### Three Mile Island

The Three Mile Island nuclear plant near Harrisburg suffered a core accident on March 28, 1979. On March 30 the governor, [[Richard Thornburgh]], "advised pregnant women and preschool aged children to leave the area within a 5-mile radius of TMI until further notice," according to the report of the President's Commission chaired by [[John G. Kemeny]], which reported in October 1979 under the title *The Need for Change: The Legacy of TMI*.[^27][^28] Thornburgh, born in Pittsburgh, had been [[United States Attorney]] for the Western District of Pennsylvania from 1969 to 1975 and Assistant Attorney General for the Criminal Division from 1975 to 1977 before his election as governor in 1978.[^29]

The Kemeny report records an informal agreement between Thornburgh and the White House under which the release of information was centralized beginning on the third day of the accident, with [[Harold Denton]] of the [[Nuclear Regulatory Commission]] issuing all statements from the site. It also records that on the Thursday afternoon the NRC's Charles Gallina told reporters that the danger was over for people off the island, and that Thornburgh distrusted the statement at the time.[^30] A report of the Senate Subcommittee on Nuclear Regulation, issued in June 1980, lists Thornburgh among the persons involved and describes him as responsible for determining whether an evacuation was necessary; the subcommittee described its own inquiry as one that "was kept small in size and selective in scope, to avoid duplication" of the President's Commission and the NRC's Special Inquiry Group.[^31] The same report records that in April 1979 Thornburgh asked the [[Union of Concerned Scientists]] to review the NRC staff recommendations on venting krypton from the containment building.[^32] Thornburgh served as governor from January 1979 to January 1987 and later as Attorney General of the United States from 1988 to 1991. The House Judiciary Committee's 1992 INSLAW report names him with [[Edwin Meese]] as having blocked congressional inquiries and refused an independent counsel.[^1][^33]

### Kecksburg

On December 9, 1965, an object that witnesses described as acorn-shaped came down in the woods near the village of Kecksburg, about seven miles southeast of Greensburg, at about 4:45 p.m.; the 1991 article gives 4:46 p.m.[^34][^35] Witnesses described a fireball moving at low speeds and altitudes across the southwestern Pennsylvania sky, and some said they saw a craft half buried in a gully. Others said they saw personnel from the [[United States Air Force|Air Force]], the Army and [[National Aeronautics and Space Administration|NASA]] at the site that evening, and one account describes a Volkswagen-sized object removed under a tarpaulin on a military flatbed truck, taken, some said, to [[Wright-Patterson Air Force Base]] in Dayton, Ohio.[^36] The military's public position, as the Pittsburgh Post-Gazette summarized it in 1998, was that the object was a meteorite that was never recovered.[^37]

The Tribune-Review's account of the 2003 Sci Fi Channel documentary lists the witnesses it presented. Robert Blystone described a "controlled fireball." Bill Weaver, then 19, saw a bluish light in the woods "like a welder's light." Don Sebastian of Johnstown described "probably 100 soldiers" and two "unearthly screams," and the Pittsburgh jazz musician Jerry Betters said he saw an "acorn-shaped object with hieroglyphics" hauled away on a flatbed truck. The article reports that onlookers were told a meteor had crashed and that persistent curiosity-seekers were turned away by police and military personnel, some, it says, at gunpoint.[^38] The documentary also presented John Murphy, a reporter for the Greensburg radio station WHJB who photographed the scene and interviewed witnesses that evening. The station's office manager, Mable Mazza, recalled that Murphy received several "unexpected visitors" at the station; the article states that he was later killed in a hit-and-run accident in California, that the driver was never found, and that his notebooks, tapes and photographs disappeared. His former wife, Bonnie Millslagle, was quoted as saying: "If someone did away with John, it was because he saw and knew too much."[^38] Two West Virginia University professors, J. Stephen Kite and Ray Hicks, took soil and tree core samples at the location witnesses Bill Bulebush and John Hays identified, and Hicks said the samples indicated a "traumatic event" at or near 1965.[^38]

The journalist [[David Templeton]] wrote about the case for the [[Pittsburgh Press]] in 1991. In that article [[James Oberg]], a Houston space engineer and author who had written about Soviet spacecraft, gave the Soviet Venus probe Kosmos 96 as a candidate, said that a Venus probe was designed to withstand entry into Venus's atmosphere and could have landed on Earth intact, and argued that the United States and the Soviet Union had an oral agreement to return each other's spacecraft debris, so that secrecy would have been paramount if either power kept the other's recovered debris.[^35] After examining the tracking data Oberg concluded, "Cosmos 96 is not guilty."[^35] In a follow-up printed in 1998, Oberg's account of an interview with the cosmonaut Viktor Savinykh, then rector of the Moscow State University of Geodesy and Cartography, says that Savinykh "suggested rather emphatically, and with some laughter, that whatever plunged into Kecksburg surely was some faulty American technology."[^39] The 1998 Post-Gazette piece reported that the U.S. Space Command had said Kosmos 96 crash-landed in Canada shortly after 3 a.m. on December 9, more than 12 hours before the Kecksburg event.[^40] The Kecksburg researcher [[Stan Gordon]], who had studied the case since the day it happened, told the paper: "It's been almost 33 years and we still don't have an answer."[^37]

### Kean v. NASA

In 2002 [[Larry Landsman]] of the Sci Fi Channel invited the journalist [[Leslie Kean]] to lead a public effort on Kecksburg.[^41] In October 2003 the channel's counsel told the Tribune-Review that a suit against NASA was expected within the week, with action possible against the [[Department of Defense]] and the Army.[^38] On January 31, 2003, Kean had already submitted a [[Freedom of Information Act]] request to NASA for documents relating to Kecksburg and the December 9, 1965 incident, the "Fragology Files" for 1962 to 1967, Richard M. Schulherr, Project Moon Dust and "Cosmos 96."[^42] The case was *Kean v. National Aeronautics and Space Administration*, Civil Action No. 03-2509 (EGS), in the [[United States District Court for the District of Columbia]], before Judge Emmet G. Sullivan.

The court's memorandum opinion of March 27, 2007 recites that NASA admitted its first two searches were inadequate. The first, by a FOIA specialist and a History Office archivist, found only published accounts of Cosmos 96 and produced a "no records" response dated April 11, 2003; the recommendation that the request go to the Goddard Space Flight Center was not carried out "for reasons that are not clear." NASA's associate deputy administrator granted Kean's appeal on June 18, 2003 and promised a search of all NASA centers, including the missing fragology files, which the opinion says was not acted on because of "poor tracking and the relocation of NASA's FOIA Office in August 2003." A third search was ordered on December 2, 2003. Nineteen boxes of archived records were sought from the Washington National Record Center on accession numbers Kean supplied; three were "reported as having been missing for quite some time," and when NASA's fourth search in July 2006 re-examined the other sixteen boxes, "responsive documents were found and disclosed to plaintiff." NASA withheld two emails between its FOIA officer and a NASA attorney under the deliberative process privilege, which the court upheld after in camera review. The court granted NASA's motion in part, denied it in part without prejudice on the adequacy of the searches, denied Kean's cross-motion without prejudice and directed the parties to propose search and documentation procedures by April 30, 2007.[^42]

The Reporters Committee for Freedom of the Press reported in October 2007 that NASA had agreed to search its archives again as the result of Kean's four-year-old suit, and in December 2007 that a settlement required NASA to "meticulously comb its files for documents about what many residents of Kecksburg, Pa., believe was a UFO sighting 42-yers ago."[^43] The *Pittsburgh Post-Gazette* reported on March 9, 2003 on the wish of Kecksburg residents to resolve what fell from the sky in 1965.[^44]

### Apollo and the NUMEC diversion inquiry

The [[Nuclear Materials and Equipment Corporation]] (NUMEC), a privately owned nuclear fuel plant at Apollo, about thirty miles northeast of Pittsburgh, was owned by [[Zalman Mordecai Shapiro]].[^45] A Washington Star report of August 19, 1977 stated that a CIA spokesman had told congressional investigators that a CIA probe led investigators to believe that bomb-grade nuclear material had been diverted from a U.S. facility and sent to Israel. According to three government sources familiar with the investigation, it started in Europe in the mid-1960s, when CIA operatives received information that Israel had obtained a sizable quantity of highly enriched uranium. The CIA investigation came to focus on the Apollo company, which produced highly enriched uranium submarine fuel for the U.S. Navy and had a subsidiary owned in partnership with the Israeli government.[^45] The same paper reported that the CIA's conclusion appeared to conflict with statements by officials of the Energy Research and Development Administration and the NRC that they had seen no evidence of significant diversion. The specifics had first been divulged to top NRC officials at a secret conference in early 1976, where the briefing was given by [[Carl E. Duckett|Carl Duckett]], then the CIA's third-ranking official.[^46]

The Comptroller General's report to Congress of December 18, 1978, *Nuclear Diversion in the U.S.? 13 Years of Contradiction and Confusion*, was classified until its redacted release on May 6, 2010. It records that an inspection in April 1965, the sixth consecutive inspection to find fundamental problems with NUMEC's control of material, concluded that "safeguards control of [nuclear material] at NUMEC is inadequate," and that the Department of Energy's predecessor found about 206 pounds of uranium-235 could not be accounted for, an amount it estimated could make at least four or five nuclear weapons. A November 1965 investigation found a cumulative loss since the plant's start-up in 1957 of 178 kilograms (392 pounds); 186 pounds were traced to waste and gas filters, and the remaining 206 pounds were not accounted for.[^47] NUMEC's president contended that the material was "unavoidably lost" in the processing system; the investigators concluded that the agency "could not say, unequivocally, that the material was not stolen or diverted from the facility."[^47] In June 1965 the agency had asked the FBI whether NUMEC's president needed to register under the Foreign Agents Registration Act, its concern stemming from his associations with Israeli officials and from his capacity as a sales agent for the Israeli Ministry of Defense; the FBI began that inquiry in August 1965 and reported in October 1966 that registration was not required.[^48]

The Comptroller General's report states that in February 1966 the agency asked the FBI to determine whether a theft or diversion had occurred and that its files contain a memorandum recording that the Bureau had decided not to investigate, though required to do so under the Atomic Energy Act of 1954; that the FBI "did not focus on the question of a possible nuclear diversion until May 1976 nearly 11 years later," after a request by President [[Gerald Ford]] in April 1976; and that the investigations were "uncoordinated, limited in scope and timeliness, and, in GAO's opinion, less than adequate."[^49] It states that GAO was "continually denied" access to reports and documentation by the CIA and the FBI, that it "cannot say whether or not there was a diversion of material from the NUMEC facility," and that FBI agents told it that while "circumstantial information" exists which could lead an individual to conclude that a diversion occurred, there is no substantive proof.[^50] Seymour Hersh's account of the same period, attributed here to him, describes a meeting at the Pittsburgh airport between Shapiro and the Israeli scientific attaché [[Jeruham Kafkafi]]; the FBI reported that Kafkafi flew from Washington for the meeting and returned at once, and Hersh wrote that Shapiro later told congressional investigators that he had asked for the meeting because he had not been paid for antiterrorist equipment his company had shipped to Israel.[^51]

A Washington Star report of December 31, 1977 stated that the NRC had disclosed the existence of top-secret government files on a loss of highly enriched uranium discovered in 1965 at the Apollo plant; according to the documents, the files were jointly held by the [[U.S. Department of Justice]], the CIA and the Atomic Energy Commission, and the NRC had initially been denied access to them.[^52] A Washington Post report of March 2, 1978 said a former CIA official stated that President [[Lyndon B. Johnson]] had ordered CIA director [[Richard Helms]] to keep an Israeli weapons report from Secretary of State [[Dean Rusk]] and Defense Secretary [[Robert McNamara]], and noted that a 550-page NRC document released in response to a House committee inquiry stated that the NRC's Lee V. Gossick had "testified incorrectly" when he said the agency had no evidence that a significant amount of nuclear material had been stolen from the plant.[^53] A New York Times report by [[David Burnham]] of April 29, 1979 stated that the Energy Department's Inspector General had concluded that [[Robert W. Fri]], formerly acting head of the Energy Research and Development Administration, may have made intentionally inaccurate statements to a House subcommittee about a U.S. intelligence agency's views on the diversion of special nuclear material from the NUMEC plant; the Justice Department declined to bring criminal charges, citing "insufficient evidence."[^54]

A CIA memorandum of April 25, 1978 records that the CIA had delivered documents on NUMEC to the Joint Committee on Atomic Energy in response to a request of March 25, 1976 from Senator [[Howard Baker]], which had been narrowed to the documents discussed at the committee's briefing of February 19, 1976; the memorandum states that Chairman [[John Dingell]]'s request for all documents on the subject had resulted in an additional package being compiled, that the additional documents "do not contain any significant information," and recommends offering the package to Baker's staff. The letter from Director [[George H. W. Bush]] to Baker that accompanies it reads in full: "I have been informed by my staff that copies of all documents originated or controlled by this Agency in the files identified in your letter of 25 March 1976 have been delivered to Mr. George Murphy, Jr., Executive Director, Joint Committee on Atomic Energy. I am further advised that the Federal Bureau of Investigation has not authorized us to release their documents in those files but instead expects to deal directly with Mr. Murphy on this matter. Likewise, the FBI advised that it would be inappropriate for us to transmit an inventory listing of their documents."[^55]

### The Archdiocese and the 2018 grand jury

The [[Roman Catholic Archdiocese of Philadelphia]] is the see of the [[Catholic Church]] for southeastern Pennsylvania. Pope Pius VII erected the Diocese of Philadelphia on April 8, 1808, and the diocese became an archdiocese on February 12, 1875. The western part of the state was separated as the Diocese of Pittsburgh on August 8, 1843, and the Dioceses of Scranton and Harrisburg on March 3, 1868.[^56] In February 1975 the midnight showing of the film *Black Mass* at the Arcadia Theatre on Chestnut Street was cancelled, according to the *Philadelphia Inquirer*, after reported pressure from the Archdiocese, and the film was shown instead at the Bandbox Theater in Germantown.[^57]

Report I of the Fortieth Statewide Investigating Grand Jury, released on August 14, 2018 by order of the Pennsylvania Supreme Court of July 27, 2018, examined six dioceses (Allentown, Erie, Greensburg, Harrisburg, Pittsburgh and Scranton), "every diocese in the state except Philadelphia and Altoona-Johnstown, which were the subject of previous grand juries." The jurors wrote that the six account for 54 of the state's 67 counties, that they heard dozens of witnesses and reviewed half a million pages of internal diocesan documents, and that those documents contained credible allegations against over three hundred priests and identified over one thousand child victims. The report states that almost every instance was too old to prosecute and that the grand jury issued presentments against a priest in the Greensburg diocese and a priest in the Erie diocese.[^4] It describes the diocesan records as including a "secret archive" that canon law requires and to which only the bishop holds the key, and states that agents of the Federal Bureau of Investigation's National Center for the Analysis of Violent Crime reviewed a significant portion of the evidence and identified practices that recurred across the files, summarized by the jurors as "a playbook for concealing the truth," ending with the rule "don't tell the police."[^58] The Diocese of Allentown, the report notes, was created in 1961 from part of the Archdiocese of Philadelphia, and the report refers to an earlier Philadelphia archdiocese grand jury report of 2005.[^59] On the day of release the Pennsylvania Office of Victim Advocate, the Pennsylvania Coalition Against Rape and the Archdiocese's Office for Child and Youth Protection issued a joint response that directed survivors and parishioners to counseling resources.[^60]

### Aircraft, Lakeland and the Mellon family

The Senate hearing record of [[Gary Betzner]]'s testimony describes his aircraft business in Florida and an aircraft bought in Pennsylvania. In 1983 Betzner set up a business at [[Tamiami Airport]] that modified aircraft for smugglers, and he bought a twin Comanche from [[Air America]] in Pennsylvania, operating it from Lakeland, Florida.[^61]

The [[Mellon family]] of Pittsburgh includes [[Christopher Mellon]]. In February 2003 *Roll Call* described him as a member of the Mellon family whose great-grandfather had started the Gulf Oil Company, and reported that Senator [[Jay Rockefeller]] had hired him as minority staff director of the [[Senate Select Committee on Intelligence]].[^62] On October 30, 2021 he became a research affiliate of the [[Galileo Project]]. On April 26, 2024 the project announced a grant of $575,000 from the [[Richard King Mellon Foundation]] for a third observatory station in Allegheny or Westmoreland County, Pennsylvania.[^63] An Alabama corporation, [[The Sol Foundation]], lists a director by the name "Cristopher Mellon" of Laughlintown, Pennsylvania, among its three initial directors in 2022.[^64]

### Congress and the Senate

[[Arlen Specter]] served as an assistant counsel to the [[Warren Commission]] in 1964. The commission's report lists him on the staff, and in the chapter on [[Jack Ruby]] it records "Assistant Counsel Arlen Specter" discussing by telephone with defense counsel Joe H. Tonahill the question of a polygraph examination for Ruby.[^65] Specter later represented Pennsylvania in the [[United States Senate]] from 1981 to 2011.[^66]

The Senate's resolution creating the standing Senate Select Committee on Intelligence passed in May 1976. The Rules and Administration Committee reported it after a 5 to 4 vote on an amendment offered by its chairman, [[Howard Cannon]], and the Senate's leaders, [[Mike Mansfield]] and [[Hugh Scott]] of Pennsylvania, endorsed the compromise version. The Senate agreed to S. Res. 400 on May 19, 1976, by 72 to 22.[^67]

### Delaware County and the Zizians

[[Michelle Zajko]] is a member of the [[The Zizians|Zizian]] group. Her parents, [[Richard Zajko]] and [[Rita Zajko]], were found shot to death in their home in Chester Heights, in [[Delaware County, Pennsylvania|Delaware County]], on the night of December 31, 2022. On June 24, 2026, Delaware County District Attorney [[Tanner Rouse]] charged her with her parents' killing, as the *[[The Philadelphia Inquirer|Philadelphia Inquirer]]* reported.[^68]
