Saudi Arabia was a source of funding for the [[Contras|Contra]] war that supplemented the [[Cocaine]] trafficking proceeds and [[Oliver North]]'s illegal arms sales to [[Iran]]. The Saudi government contributed millions to the Contra cause at the [[Ronald Reagan|Reagan]] administration's request, part of the parallel funding streams that kept the war alive after Congress cut off official aid.[^1]

### Third-Party Funding

The Saudi contributions to the Contra war were part of Oliver North's network of third-party funding sources that included private donors, foreign governments, and drug trafficking proceeds. When Congress passed the [[Boland Amendment]] cutting off military aid to the Contras, the administration turned to Saudi Arabia, Iran, and other sources to keep the war funded. The combination of Saudi money, Iranian arms sales proceeds, and cocaine trafficking revenue created a multi-source funding pipeline that operated outside congressional oversight.[^2]

### Saudi Nationals in American AI Companies

On October 13, 2026 the Justice Department identified [[Abdulrahman Alfozan]], 35, as a Saudi national and an engineer at [[OpenAI]] who was charged with installing a recording device in an executive conference room in San Francisco.[^3] A post on X by the [[Smashi Business]] account on September 12, 2026 described him as "currently the only Saudi employee at OpenAI."[^4] On October 13, 2026 [[Scott Stedman]] wrote on X that Alfozan is a venture partner at [[RAED Ventures]], "which has received funding from Saudi government-backed entities," and that the firm also has a Saudi royal family member, [[Khalid bin Bader Al Saud]], as a venture partner.[^5]

### Technology Competitions and Youth Programs

In 2017 a national institution called the Saudi Federation for Cybersecurity, Programming and Drones (SAFCSP) was established. Its 2018 homepage places it "under the umbrella of the Saudi Arabian Olympic Committee."[^6] The federation ran the Hajj Hackathon, which Arab News reported in August 2018 as "the largest Hajj Hackathon for cyber security, programming and drones," an entry in the Guinness Book of Records.[^7] In 2020 it ran the Homathon, an online hackathon held April 19 to 25, with a total prize pool of $85,000.[^8] Abdulrahman Alfozan's LinkedIn profile lists first place in the Homathon.[^9] In October 2022 it co-organized the Tuwaiq Drone Challenge with the German Dronemasters Academy.[^10] Tuwaiq Academy, launched in August 2019 as an alliance between the federation and Princess Nourah bint Abdulrahman University, runs its bootcamps.[^11]

The federation's chairman in April 2018 was [[Saud al-Qahtani]], identified as "Adviser at the Royal Court."[^7] Qahtani was fired as royal court adviser on October 20, 2018, in a royal order reported by Saudi state media, and the United States Treasury designated him on November 15, 2018 over the killing of [[Jamal Khashoggi]].[^12][^13]

### Twitter Espionage Prosecution

In November 2019 the United States charged two former Twitter employees, [[Ahmad Abouammo]] and [[Ali Alzabarah]], and a Saudi intermediary, [[Ahmed Almutairi]], with acting as agents of the kingdom without notice to the Attorney General, in the [[Twitter Saudi espionage case]]. The complaint alleges that Abouammo and Alzabarah obtained nonpublic account data on Twitter users critical of the Saudi government for officials of the kingdom, including an official whom the Ninth Circuit identifies as Bader Binasaker, a close associate of [[Mohammed bin Salman]] ([[Bader al-Asaker]]).[^14] Abouammo was convicted in August 2022 and sentenced to 42 months in prison; the Ninth Circuit affirmed the convictions on December 4, 2024 and vacated the sentence, and on June 26, 2025 he was resentenced to time served.[^15][^16] On June 11, 2026 the Supreme Court unanimously reversed the judgment on the venue of the falsification count, holding that the trial for that count belonged in Seattle, and remanded the case.[^17]
