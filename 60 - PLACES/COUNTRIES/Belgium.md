Belgium is a federal constitutional monarchy in northwestern [[Europe]], bordered by the [[Netherlands]], [[Germany]], [[Luxembourg]], and [[France]], with coastline on the [[North Sea]]. Brussels, its capital, is the headquarters of [[NATO]] and the primary seat of the [[European Union]]. Belgium has three official language communities (French, Dutch/Flemish, and German) and a complex federal political structure. Its political history is marked by significant institutional fragility and a series of scandals involving connections between political elites, organized crime, and law enforcement.[^1]

### Marc Dutroux and the X-Dossier

Belgium became the focus of international attention in 1996 following the discovery that [[Marc Dutroux]] had abducted, imprisoned, and murdered multiple young girls over a period of years. The case was marked by extraordinary investigative failures: Belgian gendarme [[Rene Michaux]] had searched Dutroux's house in December 1995, heard children's voices, and failed to find the dungeon where [[Julie Lejeune]] and [[Melissa Russo]] were imprisoned; both girls died of starvation shortly afterward.

The investigation generated the [[X-Dossier]], a collection of testimony from multiple witnesses who described vast elite networks of organized child sexual abuse involving aristocrats, politicians, judges, and businesspeople. Key witnesses included X1 ([[Regina Louf]], a survivor who described years of abuse organized by figures including [[Jean-Michel Nihoul]] and connected to the upper levels of Belgian society) and X2, who described events at estates and villas in the [[Knokke-Heist]] area connected to [[Count Leopold Lippens]], [[Count Maurice Auguste Lippens]], and [[Etienne Davignon]]. Named figures also included [[Paul Vanden Boeynants]], former Prime Minister of Belgium, and [[Baron Benoit de Bonvoisin]].[^2]

Belgian investigative magistrate [[Jean-Marc Connerotte]] was controversially removed from the case in 1997 after attending a fundraising dinner for the victims' families, a decision that triggered mass public protests. The removal was widely interpreted as an attempt to shield powerful figures from investigation. Dutroux was ultimately convicted in 2004 of the murders of [[An Marchal]], [[Eefje Lambrecks]], Julie Lejeune, and Melissa Russo, as well as the abduction and rape of additional victims.[^1]

The Chamber's inquiry into the Dutroux-Nihoul investigation stated in its general conclusions of February 16, 1998 that there were indications that Dutroux and Nihoul had conducted polycriminal activity, that their networks had enjoyed "at various levels an indirect protection engendered by individual or collective phenomena of the blurring of the norm or of corrupting behaviours," and that it had found no indication of protections within the police, magistracy, administration or politics capable of explaining by themselves the failures in the investigations.[^3]

### NATO and Brussels

Belgium hosts NATO headquarters in Brussels, established there in 1967 after [[Charles de Gaulle]] expelled NATO from French soil. NATO's headquarters are at Evere in the Brussels region, and the Supreme Headquarters Allied Powers Europe ([[SHAPE]]) is at Casteau, where the American commands were transferred from Rocquencourt after France left the integrated military command.[^4] The Belgian intelligence service [[SURETE DE L'ETAT]] (State Security) maintained liaison relationships with partner services, and Belgian military intelligence had connections to the [[Gladio]] stay-behind network.[^2] In 1991 a Belgian Senate inquiry into an international clandestine intelligence network examined two units, the STC/Mob branch of the Sûreté and SDRA VIII. It recorded that the Belgian secretariat of the NATO Clandestine Planning Committee was a branch of SDRA and that the secretariat was abolished on April 1, 1991, according to two witnesses.[^4]

### Gladio and the Strategy of Tension

Belgium was among the NATO member states that hosted a Gladio stay-behind network during the Cold War. The Belgian network - known as [[SDRA8]] within military intelligence - maintained weapons caches and a trained paramilitary personnel roster, as in other NATO countries. Belgian investigations following the 1990 Italian disclosure of Gladio's existence revealed that the Belgian network had possible connections to the unsolved [[Brabant massacres]] (early 1982 to November 1985; [[Daniele Ganser]] dates them 1983 to 1985), in which a gang killed 28 people in supermarket robberies whose apparent purpose exceeded any criminal rationale - suggesting, to investigators, a connection to stay-behind network operations designed to destabilize the political landscape.[^1]

The Senate inquiry reported in October 1991 that it found no indication of any link between the network and terrorist acts or serious banditry committed in Belgium over the previous ten years, and that within the limits of its information no direct link could be established between SDRA VIII or STC/Mob and the persons and organizations investigated in the 1980 and 1988 inquiries into the Brabant killings. The same report recorded that its magistrate-experts had been refused the identities of the civilian agents of the two units, which it said had prevented verifications that would probably have eliminated all doubt, and that a State Security officer, an SDRA officer, an SDRI officer who had been a court-appointed expert in several Brabant cases and a gendarme had all been in sustained contact with Major Bougerol of the gendarmerie in the same period, two of them belonging with him to a confraternity. It also recorded contacts between the military Public Information Office, the [[Front de la Jeunesse]] and other extreme-right organizations.[^5] The word Gladio was unknown in Belgium until November 1990, according to the same report.[^5]

The Chamber of Representatives opened a separate parliamentary inquiry into the Brabant killings in June 1996 and reported on October 14, 1997 (Doc. 573/8). Its first annex, an inventory of publicly formulated grievances written for the commission by the law professors [[Cyrille Fijnaut]] and [[Raf Verstraeten]], examines the leads in the investigation, among them the extreme-right Front de la Jeunesse and the [[Westland New Post]] (WNP), which [[Paul Latinus]] formed in 1981. It records that the WNP was credited by some with contacts in the army and the gendarmerie, reproduces a journalist's 1983 view that the Brabant killers were terrorists who may have belonged to the WNP, and quotes the 1995 statement of the investigator Barrez that the WNP should have been investigated more thoroughly and that it remained open whether State Security had used the group. It quotes the Senate commission's magistrate-experts as finding no element showing that SDRA VIII or STC/Mob members played a role in the crimes, while calling the continued activity of Major Bougerol after the Public Information Office was wound up a "disturbing" finding and stating that the activities of extreme-right movements "manipulated or not by foreign services," tolerated and even patronized by authorities of military intelligence, "deserve the attention of Parliament."[^6]
