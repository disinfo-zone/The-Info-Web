The North Atlantic Treaty Organization (NATO) is a military alliance founded on April 4, 1949, when twelve states signed the North Atlantic Treaty in Washington: the [[United States]], [[Canada]], the [[United Kingdom]], [[France]], [[Belgium]], the [[Netherlands]], [[Luxembourg]], [[Denmark]], [[Norway]], [[Iceland]], [[Portugal]] and [[Italy]].[^1] Article 5 of the treaty provides that "an armed attack against one or more of them in Europe or North America shall be considered an attack against them all."[^1] [[Greece]] joined in 1952 and [[West Germany]] in 1955.[^2]

The alliance's headquarters are in [[Brussels]], Belgium, at Evere. The Belgian Senate's 1991 report places the Supreme Headquarters Allied Powers Europe ([[SHAPE]]) at Casteau, and records that American commands were moved from Rocquencourt to Casteau after [[Charles de Gaulle]] withdrew France from the integrated military command.[^3] In 1967 the headquarters moved to Belgium after France withdrew from the integrated command and expelled NATO from French soil.[^3][^4]

### Cold War Role

NATO's primary Cold War function was deterring a Soviet conventional attack on Western [[Europe]] through the threat of American nuclear retaliation. The alliance maintained large conventional forces in [[Germany]], particularly the U.S. Army's VII Corps and V Corps, alongside nuclear weapons pre-positioned at European bases.[^4] The [[INF Treaty]] of 1987 between [[Ronald Reagan]] and [[Mikhail Gorbachev]], which eliminated intermediate-range nuclear missiles, was a diplomatic milestone of the period.

The integrated command required peacetime exchange of intelligence among members. The [[UKUSA Agreement]] and signals intelligence protocols, liaison between human intelligence services, and counterintelligence coordination were the main channels, with the [[Central Intelligence Agency|CIA]] as the largest partner. NATO's nuclear planning also required classified targeting and deployment data to pass to allied governments.[^4]

After the [[Cold War]] ended, NATO admitted former Warsaw Pact members over Russian objections. Article 5 was invoked for the first time after the September 11, 2001 attacks, and NATO deployed to [[Afghanistan]].[^4]

### Stay-Behind Committees: the CPC and the ACC

The stay-behind networks were run through two multinational bodies that sat around the NATO command structure. The Italian parliamentary commission's 1992 report, Doc. XXIII n. 51, traces the first, the Clandestine Planning Committee (CPC). A British member of the service sent the head of the Italian service, SIFAR, a letter on April 7, 1952 informing him that the committee had been set up at the initiative of the Supreme Allied Commander Europe. It was formally constituted in August 1951 under the SHAPE Standing Group, with representatives of the French, British and American services, and the SACEUR had asked that the services of the other NATO countries be added. Italy first attended a CPC meeting as an associate member on May 19, 1959.[^5]

The report describes the second body, the Allied Clandestine Committee (ACC), as created in 1958 on the initiative of the SACEUR and the CPC, with the United States, the United Kingdom, France, Belgium, the Netherlands and Luxembourg as participants. Italy joined on April 15, 1964, shortly after West Germany.[^6]

The Belgian Senate inquiry describes the Belgian branch of the CPC as the liaison between the Belgian services and the major NATO commands, above all SHAPE. It records that the Belgian CPC secretariat was a branch of SDRA, the military unit, and that no members of SDRA VIII or of the STC/Mob unit of the Sûreté took part in the CPC's work. The commission then asked why a body attached to SHAPE attended ACC meetings when the witnesses claimed the ACC had nothing to do with NATO's mission.[^7] Two witnesses told the commission that the Belgian CPC secretariat was abolished on April 1, 1991.[^8]

The Italian report dates the bilateral work of the Italian and American services to 1953 and 1954, when land was acquired for the Capo Marargiu base near Alghero in Sardinia and construction began with CIA financing under Colonel Santini. The name Gladio is first recorded at an October 18, 1956 meeting between SIFAR (Colonel Giulio Fettarappa Sandri and Major Mario Accasto) and the CIA (Bob Porter and John Edwards). The accord on the post-occupation Italian-American clandestine network exists in a text restated on November 26, 1956; the Italian approval of the American draft was communicated on November 29, and the accord was fixed as in force from November 28, 1956, the date of the first document in the Gladio series.[^9] The same report says the stay-behind networks, already organized in France, the Netherlands and Belgium, were later extended to Denmark and Norway and to the German and Austrian territories under Allied control.[^10] [[Daniele Ganser]]'s study lists stay-behind networks in at least fourteen European states, among them Italy, Belgium, Germany, France, Greece, Portugal, Turkey, the Netherlands, Luxembourg, Denmark, Norway and the non-member Austria.[^11]

### Italy and the 1990 Disclosure

Italian investigators linked the Italian branch, [[Operation Gladio]], to the bombings of the [[strategy of tension]]. [[Felice Casson]], the magistrate who was working on the 1972 [[Peteano bombing]], obtained documents on the network. In 1984 [[Vincenzo Vinciguerra]], an [[Ordine Nuovo]] member who had become a state's witness, testified that stay-behind networks connected to NATO had organized attacks carried out by neofascist operatives, with military intelligence providing protection. The attacks in his account include the [[Piazza Fontana bombing]].[^12]

On October 24, 1990, Prime Minister [[Giulio Andreotti]] told the Chamber of Deputies that the structure "esiste nel quadro della NATO" (exists within the framework of NATO). He described it as a network providing, in case of occupation by enemy forces, "una rete di salvaguardia, sia informativa sia di reazione" (a network of safeguard, both informational and of reaction), all within the framework of the Alliance. He also told the Chamber that he had opened the archives completely to Casson.[^13] The Italian government's figures, transmitted to the parliamentary commission, put the membership at dissolution at 622 persons, 45 of them deceased, covering 1958 to 1990: 408 members of the cells and 214 of the rapid-deployment units. Recruitment had been started for 1,915 names, two thirds of which ended negatively, and a file was kept on each. The commission recorded that 127 of the 622 were enrolled before the required information on them had been gathered, and that 24 were proposed or confirmed although they had belonged to the Fascist party, the Italian Social Republic or the Decima MAS.[^14]

A parliamentary question filed in Bonn on November 12, 1990 summarized the Italian statements. It said that Andreotti had confirmed the existence of a parallel NATO intelligence service under the name "Organisation Gladio" into the 1980s and possibly later, and that its basis was a secret addendum to the accession declarations of new NATO members. The question records that the former Greek prime minister [[Andreas Papandreou|Papandreou]] had confirmed the same basis for Greece and an activity under the codename "Haut des roten Berges." These are the questioners' statements, not findings.[^15]

### The European Parliament Resolution

On November 22, 1990 the [[European Parliament]] adopted a joint resolution on the Gladio affair, replacing five motions, published in the Official Journal C 324 of December 24, 1990 (page 201).[^16] Its recitals refer to "the existence for 40 years of a clandestine parallel intelligence and armed operations organization in several Member States of the Community," state that for over 40 years "this organization has escaped all democratic controls and has been run by the secret services of the states concerned in collaboration with NATO," and record that "in certain Member States military secret services (or uncontrolled branches thereof) were involved in serious cases of terrorism and crime as evidenced by, various judicial inquiries." The operative paragraphs condemn "the clandestine creation of manipulative and operational networks" and call for "a full investigation into the nature, structure, aims and all other aspects of these clandestine organizations," including "the possible collusion of the secret services of Member States or third countries"; protest "vigorously at the assumption by certain US military personnel at SHAPE and in NATO of the right to encourage the establishment in Europe of a clandestine intelligence and operation network"; call on the member state governments "to dismantle all clandestine military and paramilitary networks"; call on the judiciaries concerned to clarify any action taken "to destabilize the democratic structure of the Member States"; request national parliamentary committees of inquiry to draw up a complete list of such organizations; and instruct the President to forward the resolution to the Commission, the Council, the Secretary-General of NATO, the member state governments and the United States Government.[^16]

### Belgium and the SDRA8 Network

The Belgian network, [[SDRA8]] (SDRA VIII), was the subject of a parliamentary inquiry into the existence in Belgium of an international clandestine intelligence network. The report by Erdman and Hasquin is dated October 1, 1991. It examines two units, the STC/Mob branch of the [[Sûreté de l'État|Sûreté]] and the SDRA VIII unit.[^17]

The inquiry looked at the [[Brabant massacres]], the robberies and killings attributed to the Brabant gang (the Bende van Nijvel) from early 1982 to November 1985, ending with the attack on a supermarket at Aalst. Twenty-eight people died and no one has been convicted; the limitation period, due to expire in 2015, was extended by ten years.[^18] Daniele Ganser dates the massacres 1983 to 1985.[^19]

The Senate report records that the magistrates in the Brabant investigation had turned first to the structure of the military and civil intelligence services and then to the stay-behind networks, and had interviewed the acting administrator-general of the Sûreté, the chief of the military service and Colonel Bodart on the subject. It also records that the chiefs of SDRA VIII and STC/Mob and several instructors did not give the magistrates the identities of their agents, citing their undertaking not to reveal them.[^20] Ganser wrote that the senators ordered Raes (director of the Sûreté from 1977 to 1990 and chief of STC/Mob) and Lieutenant Colonel Bernard Legrand (chief of SDRA8) to supply the names, or at least the birth dates, of the stay-behind agents; that both refused although the Justice Minister and the Defense Minister had ordered them to cooperate; and that in the end the senators could not identify the men behind the Brabant terror. He quotes Legrand: "there remain very good reasons not to reveal the names of the clandestines." He wrote also that Legrand answered in Le Soir with a coded notice addressed to "Dear friends in Operation Stay Behind, section SDRA8," ending "Adolphe is looking well!"[^19]

Daniele Ganser recounted the documentary maker Allan Francovich's thesis that segments of the Belgian stay-behind may have cooperated with the far-right Westland New Post; the statement of the former gendarme Martial Lekeu that members of the gendarmerie and the army were involved in the killings and that he was told to keep quiet when he reported it in December 1983; and the account of the Westland New Post member Michel Libert that from 1982 to 1985 his group, "with cover from the Gendarmerie, with cover from Security," surveyed supermarkets on orders whose purpose he was not told.[^11] The Senate, in its own finding, wrote that on the elements available to it no direct link could be established between SDRA VIII and STC/Mob on one side and the persons and organizations investigated in 1980 and 1988 on the other, and that no known instructor or recruiter of either unit was, in any capacity, involved in the judicial investigation into the Brabant killings. The same section records that a Sûreté official, a military intelligence officer, a court-appointed expert in several Brabant cases and a gendarmerie officer all had sustained relations in the same period with Major Bougerol, that two of them belonged with him to a confraternity (the hospitaliers of Notre-Dame d'Aulne), and that a member of the B.S.R. who had attended at least one far-right meeting acted as an investigating agent in files on the Brabant killings.[^20]

### U.S. Commanders and Envoys

[[Lyman Lemnitzer]] served as Chairman of the Joint Chiefs of Staff until September 30, 1962. His move to Europe was announced in July 1962: at the news conference of July 23, 1962 [[John F. Kennedy]] answered a question on his "swift appointment" as Commander in Chief of U.S. Forces in Europe on the retirement of General Lauris Norstad, said Norstad's resignation would take effect October 1, and said Lemnitzer "could have retired in October and there would have been a vacancy as Chairmen of the Joint Chiefs of Staff."[^21] The Joint Staff's history records that Norstad announced his retirement in July 1962 and that his departure, delayed by the Cuban missile crisis, took place on January 2, 1963.[^22] He became Commander in Chief of the U.S. European Command on November 1, 1962 and Supreme Allied Commander Europe on January 1, 1963, a post he held until his retirement on July 1, 1969.[^23]

[[Alexander M. Haig, Jr.]] was announced in September 1974 as the successor to General Andrew J. Goodpaster as SACEUR, and he held the post until 1979.[^24] The Defense Planning Committee of the North Atlantic Council appointed [[Isaac C. Kidd Jr.]] Supreme Allied Commander Atlantic in March 1975, to succeed Admiral Ralph W. Cousins.[^25] [[David K. E. Bruce]] was appointed U.S. Permanent Representative on the North Atlantic Council on October 17, 1974 and left the post on February 12, 1976.[^26]

[[Vernon Walters]] wrote that after the war he served as aide to [[W. Averell Harriman]] at Marshall Plan headquarters in Paris, then helped establish NATO's Supreme Headquarters in 1951, and that during the 1950s he was aide and interpreter to Presidents [[Harry Truman]] and [[Dwight Eisenhower]] at international summits.[^27]

### Dozier and the Red Brigades

[[James L. Dozier]] was kidnapped from his apartment in Verona, Italy, on December 17, 1981 by the [[Red Brigades]]. A press clipping in the CIA reading room describes him as "the highest-ranking U.S. officer in NATO's southern Europe command."[^28] A memorandum of March 23, 1982 from the [[Grill Flame]] project manager records a debriefing of Dozier on February 9, 1982, at which he was asked to review remote-viewing drawings made during the search and to comment on places and events of his captivity.[^29] A January 1982 Army message reported visions by several private individuals in Kent, Washington, that concerned Dozier. The visions described a settlement called the "place of the shepherds" and a hallway behind metal bars, and the message records that the visions linked the settlement to Dozier's whereabouts.[^30]

### Narut and the 1975 Oslo Conference

[[Commander Narut]], a Navy clinical psychologist stationed in Naples, was reported by [[The Sunday Times|the Sunday Times]] on July 6, 1975 to have disclosed at a NATO conference in Oslo a Navy program to select and condition covert assassins. The Pentagon denied the report the same day. Narut partially withdrew his most specific claim within days, and [[Peter Watson]], the paper's science correspondent who wrote the story, did not withdraw it.[^31]

### The Assessment

Retired Army sergeant major [[Robert O. Dean]] has said in speeches and online postings that while stationed at SHAPE in the mid-1960s he read a classified document titled "The Assessment: An Evaluation of a Possible Threat to NATO Forces in Europe," which studied UFOs and the potential threat they posed to NATO.[^32] In a 1995 interview Dean said he held a "cosmic" clearance at SHAPE and found the document in the war-room vault in 1964. He described its origin as NATO detections of unidentified objects crossing from the Soviet Union over the Warsaw Pact countries and central Europe and vanishing over the Norwegian Sea, and a three-year military investigation in which aircraft sent to track "large metallic, circular objects" were outflown. He called the resulting report "mind-boggling" and said it found that "there was no military threat involved, because these dudes have enough technology that if they had wanted to take us over, they apparently had the ability to do it."[^33]

The SHAPE Historical Office has said it has been asked many times for a copy and has analyzed a photograph said to show the cover. It concluded that the cover page is a forgery and that SHAPE "prepared no report on UFOs during the 1960s." The office states that it takes no position on whether UFOs exist.[^32]

### Corporate and Political Links

In June 2026 WIRED reported on a leaked registration list for the 2026 retreat of the [[Dialog]] society, which [[Peter Thiel]] co-founded. The registration records list General Alexus Grynkewich, NATO's supreme allied commander Europe and head of U.S. European Command.[^34]

The [[Liege]] pickup point for American matériel bought from NATO stocks in Europe appears in the account of [[Ari Ben-Menashe]], who described Israeli chartered cargo flights collecting the matériel for transfer to Iran.[^35] [[Barbara Honegger]] alleged in her 1989 book on the [[October Surprise]] that a heroin-smuggling network in Italy was used to launder NATO arms stocks for diversion to Iran, with the involvement of Italian intelligence officers linked to the [[P2]] lodge.[^36]
