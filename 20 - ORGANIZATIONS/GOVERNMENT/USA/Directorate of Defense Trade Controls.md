The Directorate of Defense Trade Controls (DDTC) is the office in the [[State Department]]'s [[Bureau of Political-Military Affairs]] that administers the [[International Traffic in Arms Regulations]] (ITAR, 22 CFR Parts 120 to 130), which implement section 38 of the [[Arms Export Control Act]]. The directorate registers manufacturers and exporters of defense articles, licenses their exports, issues commodity jurisdiction determinations that decide whether an item is on the [[United States Munitions List]], and imposes civil penalties by consent agreement and statutory debarment.

### Lineage

The office descends from the [[National Munitions Control Board]], which a joint resolution of August 31, 1935 established "to carry out the provisions of" an act barring arms exports to belligerent countries. The board was chaired by the [[Secretary of State]] and included the secretaries of the [[Department of the Treasury|Treasury]], [[Department of Defense|War]], [[U.S. Navy|Navy]] and [[Department of Commerce|Commerce]]. The act vested administration in the State Department, required persons engaged in manufacturing, exporting or importing arms to register, and required the board's annual report to Congress to contain "a list of all persons required to register under the provisions of this Act, and full information concerning the licenses issued hereunder."[^1]

The licensing work was done by the Office of Munitions Control within the State Department. A public notice of March 29, 1990 announced that the Office of Munitions Control had changed its name to the Office of Defense Trade Controls, a change recorded in a September 17, 1996 ITAR amendment that also replaced the Bureau of Politico-Military Affairs with the Bureau of Political-Military Affairs.[^2] Effective January 20, 2003 the department realigned its defense trade controls. A rule published February 14, 2003 amended the ITAR to describe the new Directorate of Defense Trade Controls under a Deputy Assistant Secretary and Managing Director of Defense Trade Controls (the contact named in the rule was [[Robert W. Maggi]]), with subordinate offices for management, licensing, compliance and policy, and provided: "The position of Director of the Office of Defense Trade Controls is abolished."[^3] The Commerce Department's [[Bureau of Industry and Security]] followed in a rule effective October 12, 2004, which stated that the State Department had decided "through an internal organizational order" to change the name of the Office of Defense Trade Controls to the Directorate and replaced the office's name and "DTC" with "Directorate of Defense Trade Controls" and "DDTC" throughout the [[Export Administration Regulations]].[^4] The offices below the directorate kept the older name, as in the Office of Defense Trade Controls Compliance, which conducted the reviews behind later settlements.[^5][^6] An April 21, 2006 ITAR rule made the same change throughout the regulations, replaced references to the Center for Defense Trade with the directorate or with the Office of Defense Trade Controls Policy depending on the section, and set out the directorate's place in the bureau at 22 CFR 120.12.[^7]

The Arms Export Control Act was enacted as title II of the International Security Assistance and Arms Export Control Act of 1976 (Public Law 94-329, June 30, 1976). Its section 38 carried forward the registration and licensing requirements, and the statutory note provides that references to 22 U.S.C. 1934 are deemed references to section 38.[^8]

### Registration

Section 38 requires that every person who "engages in the business of manufacturing, exporting, or importing" designated defense articles or defense services "shall register with the United States Government agency charged with the administration of this section, and shall pay a registration fee."[^8] A 1996 amendment (Public Law 104-164, title I, section 151(a), July 21, 1996) added a second clause covering persons engaged in "brokering activities" with respect to the manufacture, export, import or transfer of foreign defense articles and services.[^8]

A rule effective January 9, 2025 revised the registration fees, setting them by tier with a multiplier of $1,100 for Tier 3 at 22 CFR 122.3(a)(3).[^9]

The company [[ELDAEON]] states its registration on its website. The footer of its home page reads "DDTC M53536" followed by a CAGE code.[^10] In a June 8, 2026 comment to the [[Federal Aviation Administration]] on a proposed rule about drone flights near fixed-site facilities, the company wrote that it "is registered with the U.S. Department of State's Directorate of Defense Trade Controls (DDTC) and is pursuing a Commodity Jurisdiction determination for our flagship system, Nemesis, under ITAR."[^11]

### Commodity Jurisdiction

Under 22 CFR 120.12, a person who submits a Commodity Jurisdiction Determination Form (DS-4076) receives a determination from the directorate on whether an article or service is covered by the U.S. Munitions List in Part 121. The regulation states that the determination "entails consultation among the Departments of State, Defense, Commerce, and other U.S. Government agencies and industry in appropriate cases," and that the three departments resolve commodity jurisdiction disputes under established procedures. The determination is made case by case "taking into account" the form and fit of the article, its function and performance capability, and the applicant's history of the product's design, development and use.[^12]

The satellite cases show how that jurisdiction moved between agencies. The House [[Cox Committee]] (the Select Committee on U.S. National Security and Military/Commercial Concerns with the People's Republic of China) recorded in its summary on Hughes that commercial satellites "had been transferred to the Commerce Department, but licensing for improvements to any part of the rocket, such as the fairing, remained with the State Department."[^13]

A directorate rule revising Category XV of the Munitions List, which covers spacecraft and related articles, took effect on November 10, 2014, with one paragraph effective June 27, 2014.[^14] A Commerce interim final rule published the same day added controls under the Export Administration Regulations for spacecraft that the President had determined "no longer warrant control" under Category XV.[^15] A December 30, 1996 Commerce rule had earlier moved encryption items from the Munitions List to the [[Commerce Control List]].[^16]

### Licensing and Brokering

The licensing record includes foreign-origin arms moved by American brokers. The [[Hartford Courant]] reported that [[E. Lawrence Barcella, Jr.]], then an assistant United States attorney in Washington, gave a legal opinion clearing the way for a private $5 million shipment of Soviet-bloc rifles, machine guns and grenade launchers to the Nicaraguan [[Contras]] in the summer of 1985. Barcella told the paper in 1992 that "somebody in the government came to me." He said that in developing the opinion he contacted the Office of Munitions Control at the State Department and the Justice Department's office of internal security. [[Joseph diGenova]], the United States attorney, said "And I never authorized any such thing."[^17] The 1996 brokering clause of section 38 postdates the shipment by eleven years.

In the prosecution of [[Ari Ben-Menashe]], [[Richard St. Francis]] and [[Joseph O'Toole]] in the [[United States District Court for the Southern District of New York]], the defendants were charged with conspiring to sell American-made C-130 cargo planes located in Israel to [[Iran]] in violation of the Arms Export Control Act. In an opinion of August 1, 1990 Judge [[Louis L. Stanton]] quoted St. Francis's memorandum as contending that he had been encouraged to think that obtaining a false end user certificate was the only "legitimate" way to complete the transaction, with the certificate "being submitted to the Israeli Government (who would know it was false) and not to the State Department (who would also know it was false)."[^18]

The Hashemi cases of the 1980s turned on the same end-user paperwork. The report of the congressional Iran-Contra committees records that in May 1984 federal criminal charges were filed against [[Cyrus Hashemi]] and his [[Hashemi brothers|brothers]] [[Reza Hashemi|Reza]] and [[Jamshid Hashemi|Jamshid]], among others, for alleged arms export control violations; that Hashemi then arranged with the [[U.S. Customs]] Service to run a [[Hashemi Sting|sting operation]]; and that it produced the April 22, 1986 indictment of 17 individuals in the Southern District of New York on charges of an illegal scheme to smuggle $2.5 billion in American-made weapons to Iran. Hashemi was to be the primary prosecution witness until his death in July 1986.[^19] The committees also recorded that the businessman [[Roy Furmark]] knew Hashemi and introduced [[Manucher Ghorbanifar]] to him and to [[Adnan Khashoggi]],[^20] and that around April 22, 1986 CIA officers learned that Ghorbanifar had allegedly funded a transaction coordinated by Hashemi that violated federal law, which was the first they knew of a Customs sting.[^20] The Second Circuit later wrote that the original indictment in the case was returned in May 1986; that its lead defendant [[Samuel Evans]] was general counsel to Khashoggi; and that the defendants were charged with conspiring to violate the Arms Export Control Act and to make false statements, under 22 U.S.C. 2778(c) and 18 U.S.C. 1001, by supplying false end user certificates to deceive the United States about the destination of arms valued at over $2 billion, which were to go to Hashemi, "a government agent pretending to be an Iranian buyer."[^21] Judge [[Leonard B. Sand]] wrote in 1987 that none of the arms deals in the fourth superseding indictment was consummated; that Hashemi was the putative Iranian buyer in each of five alleged conspiracies, including the "Dergo Establishment Arms Deal"; and that under the regulations "the written approval of the Department of State" had to be obtained before a defense article was resold, diverted or transferred to a country other than the one stated on the export license. The opinion also names Customs Agent [[Joe King|King]], who assisted a search of the defendants' luggage in Bermuda.[^22]

[[Kenneth Peoples]], a State Department licensing official who was [[Hughes Electronics Corporation|Hughes]]'s primary contact on satellite issues, had issued license number 483414 for the export of the Aussat B (later Optus B) satellite. A Hughes document on the Apstar 1 and 2 licenses stated that release of detailed design, production or manufacturing data required specific Office of Defense Trade Controls approval of a separate application.[^23]

After the failure of [[Intelsat 708]] on a [[Long March 3B]] launch in February 1996, Peoples believed there was a serious possibility the ITAR had been violated and recommended that [[Space Systems/Loral|Loral]] provide a letter to [[William J. Lowell]], Director of the State Department's Office of Defense Trade Controls. Loral delivered a box of documents to the department.[^24] On May 29, 1996 Lowell wrote to [[Eric J. Zahler]], general counsel of Loral Space and Communications, that there was reason to believe Loral may have participated in serious violations of the ITAR by providing unauthorized defense services to the People's Republic of China during the launch failure investigation, and recommended that Loral cease related activity requiring approval, make a full disclosure and enumerate all releases controlled under the regulations.[^25]

### Consent Agreements and Penalties

The directorate's public "Penalties & Oversight Agreements" page cites ITAR section 127.10, under which the Assistant Secretary for Political-Military Affairs is authorized to impose civil penalties, and describes consent agreements that may include a Special Compliance Officer (SCO), an Internal Special Compliance Officer (ISCO), a policy of denial, debarment, audits and "cradle-to-grave" export tracking. It lists 80 entries.[^26] Its 2026 entries include the [[BAE Systems]], Inc. agreement, which the page describes as including retransfer "in one case to the People's Republic of China," and an entry for [[General Electric]] Company describing unauthorized exports of technical data to the People's Republic of China, violations of the provisos of directorate authorizations, unauthorized exports of defense articles and "failure to report material changes to its ITAR registration."[^26]

On March 4, 2003 the Assistant Secretary of State for Political-Military Affairs signed a consent agreement imposing a $32 million fine on Hughes Electronics Corporation and [[Boeing Satellite Systems]], Inc., settling 123 charges under the Arms Export Control Act and the ITAR. Of the $20 million cash penalty, $12 million went to the Treasury and $8 million to the U.S. Customs Service to settle a separate claim. Another $12 million was suspended on condition that the companies apply the funds to remedial compliance measures.[^27]

A draft charging letter to Space Systems/Loral alleged 64 violations connected with the company's conduct after the failure of the Long March 3B launch carrying the Intelsat 708 spacecraft.[^28] The consent agreement of January 9, 2002 was signed for the department by Assistant Secretary [[Lincoln P. Bloomfield, Jr.]] and for the parent company by Eric J. Zahler, its president and chief operating officer, on January 8. It recites that the principal vehicle for the alleged violations was an Independent Review Committee of scientists and engineers from the United States and Europe, organized and chaired by Space Systems/Loral officers "under the aegis" of the China Aerospace Corporation. The civil penalty was $20,000,000: $14,000,000 payable ($2,200,000 within ten days of the order and $11,800,000 in seven annual installments of $1,685,714) and $6,000,000 suspended on condition that Loral and Space Systems/Loral apply that amount over seven years to remedial compliance measures, with $2,000,000 already invested credited toward it. The companies neither admitted nor denied the allegations.[^29]

On March 27, 2007 the [[U.S. Department of Justice|Justice Department]] announced that [[ITT Corporation]] would pay a $100 million penalty and plead guilty to illegally exporting secret military data overseas. The release calls ITT "the first major defense contractor convicted of a criminal violation of the Arms Export Control Act."[^30]

On May 16, 2011 the directorate reached a four-year consent agreement with BAE Systems for a civil penalty of $79 million, which the department called "the largest civil penalty in Department history." The department said it identified the violations in a review of BAE's conduct after the company's criminal conviction in March 2010 for conspiracy to violate the AECA and the ITAR. The proposed charging letter alleged an estimated 2,591 violations in connection with unauthorized brokering.[^31]

On April 30, 2013 the department announced a four-year consent agreement with [[Raytheon]] for $8 million in civil penalties and remedial expenditures, of which $4 million was suspended on condition that the funds be used for remedial compliance measures. The department's Office of Defense Trade Controls Compliance had found that hundreds of violations disclosed over many years showed "a recurring, corporate-wide weakness in maintaining effective ITAR controls."[^5]

A 2021 order against [[Honeywell International]], concerning unauthorized exports and retransfers of defense articles and technical data to various countries including a proscribed destination, set a civil penalty of $13,000,000, of which $5,000,000 was suspended on condition that the company apply that amount to remedial compliance measures under the consent agreement.[^32] A 2019 order against [[L3Harris Technologies]], concerning activities of Harris Corporation before the merger, set a civil penalty of $13,000,000; the charges were unauthorized exports of defense articles including technical data, inaccurate and incomplete Part 130 reporting, and violations of license terms.[^33]

On February 29, 2024 the State Department announced a 36-month consent agreement with [[Boeing]] for 199 violations of the Arms Export Control Act and the ITAR, including unauthorized exports and retransfers of technical data to foreign-person employees and contractors and unauthorized exports of technical data to China. The civil penalty was $51 million, of which $24 million was suspended. The department stated that all of the violations were voluntarily disclosed.[^34]

On August 30, 2024 the department announced a 36-month consent agreement with [[RTX Corporation]] (the successor name of Raytheon's parent) resolving 750 violations of the Arms Export Control Act and the ITAR, concluded under ITAR section 128.11. The violations were unauthorized exports of defense articles resulting from the failure to establish proper jurisdiction and classification, unauthorized exports including classified defense articles, hand-carry exports by employees to destinations listed in 22 CFR 126.1, and violations of the terms and provisos of directorate authorizations. The civil penalty was $200 million, of which $100 million was suspended on condition that the funds be used for remedial compliance measures, with an external special compliance officer for at least 24 months. The department stated that RTX disclosed all of the alleged violations voluntarily.[^6]

On August 13, 2026 the department announced a 36-month consent agreement with BAE Systems, Inc. resolving 104 violations, for a civil penalty of $36 million, of which $18 million was suspended. The settlement was concluded under ITAR section 128.11 and covered unauthorized exports of technical data, violations of the terms of directorate authorizations, and unauthorized exports of defense articles. The company voluntarily disclosed all but one of the alleged violations.[^35]

Suspension of licenses and statutory debarment form a second enforcement tool. On July 26, 1993 the Office of Defense Trade Controls suspended all licenses and approvals for [[Teledyne Wah Chang Albany]] after a May 26, 1993 indictment in the Southern District of Florida charging conspiracy to illegally export ordnance-grade zirconium to [[Chile]] "for use in cluster bombs and other munitions for Iraq." A second indictment returned July 13, 1994 in the District of Columbia charged the company and others with a scheme to export zirconium compacts to Greece for reexport to [[Jordan]]; the department suspended licenses for the company, Extraco Ltd., Weco Industrial Products Export GmbH, [[Edward A. Johnson]] and others effective that day. Teledyne pleaded guilty in Florida on January 26, 1995 and in Washington on January 27, 1995, and under a consent agreement the department rescinded the suspensions effective January 26 and 27, 1995, in a notice signed by Lowell on April 27, 1995.[^36] A June 12, 1996 notice debarred Teledyne Wah Chang Albany for three years after its convictions and listed [[Swissco Management Group]] and Johnson as convicted on August 7, 1995 of conspiracy to violate 22 U.S.C. 2778 under 18 U.S.C. 371.[^37] A March 24, 1997 notice by the office replaced a policy of denial for [[Fuchs Electronics]] with statutory debarment. The policy of denial had taken effect on June 8, 1994 in response to an indictment in the [[United States District Court for the Eastern District of Pennsylvania|Eastern District of Pennsylvania]]. The notice records a consent agreement between Fuchs and the department dated January 24, 1997, and a guilty plea by Fuchs on February 27, 1997.[^38]

The same page lists entries for earlier years: in 2024, Precision Castparts (technical data to foreign-person employees from Bhutan, Burundi, El Salvador, Honduras, Mexico and Peru); in 2023, Island Pyrochemical Industries (unauthorized brokering with a People's Republic of China corporation, and false statements), VTA Telecom (Vietnam) and 3D Systems; in 2022, Torrey Pines Logic and Leonid B. Volfson; in 2021, Keysight Technologies (technical data, including software, to various countries including a proscribed destination); in 2020, Airbus SE (false statements on authorization requests, failure to report political contributions, commissions or fees, record-keeping, and unauthorized re-export); in 2019, AeroVironment, L3Harris and Darling Industries; in 2018, FLIR Systems, including the retransfer of technical data and defense services to dual-national employees of Iran, Iraq, Lebanon and Cuba; and in 2017, Bright Lights USA.[^26]

In 2022 the directorate entered orders in three cases arising from work for the [[United Arab Emirates]]. Ryan Adams, Marc Baier and Daniel Gericke each "settled allegations that he violated the [ITAR] in connection with the unauthorized exports of defense services to the United Arab Emirates." Each order imposes "administrative debarment ... for a period of three years in accordance with ITAR § 127.7(a)."[^39] The proposed charging letter to Baier alleges one charge covering January 2016 to November 2019. It states that before [[DarkMatter]], "a foreign corporation registered in the UAE," Baier was employed by [[CyberPoint International]] LLC, "a U.S.-based company that provided cyber services to the UAE government pursuant to ITAR licenses or other approvals, including technical assistance agreements"; that the U.S.-person managers who joined DarkMatter "became the founding members of a Raven Team successor at DarkMatter, which was referred to as the Cyber Intelligence-Operations (CIO) group" and "operated in the same building, with the same terminals, setup, and computer infrastructure from which they operated under CyberPoint"; and that "Respondent created certain zero-click computer hacking and intelligence gathering systems that were specially designed, developed, maintained and operated by Respondent to access tens of millions of devices for the UAE government's intelligence purposes."[^40] The proposed charging letter to Gericke covers the period October 2016 to February 2017 and states that he "was a U.S. person within the meaning of § 120.15 of the ITAR until February 2017."[^41]

### Directors

William J. Lowell signed an April 27, 1995 notice as Director, Office of Defense Trade Controls, and a February 12, 1997 notice in the same capacity.[^36][^38] He is named as the office's director in the Loral failure review of 1996[^24][^25] and in a May 3, 2002 notice.[^42] The ITAR amendment effective January 20, 2003 abolished the position of Director of the Office of Defense Trade Controls and placed the directorate under a Deputy Assistant Secretary who was also Managing Director of Defense Trade Controls; the rule's contact was Robert W. Maggi.[^3]
