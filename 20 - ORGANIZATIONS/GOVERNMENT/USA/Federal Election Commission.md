The Federal Election Commission is the independent agency that administers and enforces federal campaign finance law in the [[United States]]. It was established by "section 309 of the [[Federal Election Campaign Act|Federal Election Campaign Act of 1971]], as amended (52 U.S.C. 30106)," through amendments enacted in 1974 after "reports of serious financial abuses in the 1972 presidential campaign." "The FEC opened its doors in 1975."[^1]

### Composition and voting rules

52 U.S.C. § 30106 provides: "There is established a commission to be known as the Federal Election Commission." The commission is composed "of the Secretary of the Senate and the Clerk of the House of Representatives or their designees, ex officio," who do not vote, "and 6 members appointed by the President, by and with the advice and consent of the Senate." "No more than 3 members of the Commission appointed under this paragraph may be affiliated with the same political party," and "Members of the Commission shall serve for a single term of 6 years." For enforcement actions, rulemaking, advisory opinions and the other enumerated matters, "the affirmative vote of 4 members of the Commission shall be required in order for the Commission to take any action." The commissioners appoint three statutory officers: a staff director, a general counsel and an inspector general.[^2]

On April 30, 2025, CNN reported that the departure of Commissioner [[Allen Dickerson]] at the end of his term left the commission with three of six seats filled, below the four votes required to act, and described it as "the fourth time in the FEC's history" the agency had lost its quorum and "the third loss of a quorum under a Trump presidency." Commissioner [[Sean Cooksey]] had resigned on January 20, 2025, to become counsel to Vice President [[JD Vance]]. Commissioner [[Ellen Weintraub]] stated on February 6, 2025, that President [[Donald Trump]] had sent her a letter on January 31 "purporting to remove me," and disputed its legality.[^3]

### Enforcement

On February 7, 1985, the commission sued the [[National Congressional Club]], its treasurer [[Carter Wrenn|R. E. Carter Wrenn]] and [[Jefferson Marketing|Jefferson Marketing, Inc.]] in the United States District Court for the [[Eastern District of North Carolina]]. The commission alleged that "Wrenn, [[Thomas F. Ellis|Ellis]] and other NCC officials exercised control of Jefferson Marketing," with Wrenn exercising "domination and control over the day-to-day operations of Jefferson Marketing," so that the club and the company operated as a single entity for reporting purposes. Under a consent order of May 15, 1986, the club and Wrenn were found to have violated the reporting provisions (section 434) by failing to disclose Jefferson Marketing's financial activity; the claim that the company had underpriced its services to candidates (section 441b) was not contested. The order imposed a "$10,000 civil penalty to the U.S. Treasury," required Ellis and Wrenn to resign as directors of the [[Educational Support Foundation]], Jefferson Marketing's shareholder, and required the club to amend its reports to disclose Jefferson Marketing's financial activity "from December 1978 to the present."[^4]

In June 2022 the [[Campaign Legal Center]] filed a complaint alleging that the super PAC [[Protect Ohio Values]] had illegally coordinated with the Senate campaign of JD Vance. The commission dismissed the complaint in 2024.[^5] The *[[Arizona Mirror]]* reported that the commission had raised questions about how the [[Saving Arizona PAC]], which backed [[Blake Masters]], was disclosing its spending.[^6]

### Litigation

In *[[FEC v. Ted Cruz for Senate]]*, decided May 16, 2022, "the United States Supreme Court affirmed the District Court's judgment," holding that "Section 304 of the [[Bipartisan Campaign Reform Act]]... is unconstitutional." The suit concerned [[Ted Cruz]]'s 2018 Senate campaign.[^7]

### Disclosure records

The commission maintains a public database of committee registrations and of itemized receipts and disbursements. [[Real Justice PAC]] registered on February 7, 2017, as committee C00632554, a hybrid PAC with a non-contribution account.[^8] Its 2019 year-end report records a 750,000 dollar receipt from the [[Open Philanthropy Action Fund]], the only itemized receipt from that fund in the commission's records.[^9] The super PAC [[Leading the Future]] registered on August 15, 2025, as C00916114,[^10] and the political action committee of [[Americans for Responsible Innovation]] registered on December 11, 2024, as C00893883.[^11] The commission's records show that Cruz's 2016 presidential campaign paid [[Cambridge Analytica]] more than 5.8 million dollars during that cycle.[^12]
