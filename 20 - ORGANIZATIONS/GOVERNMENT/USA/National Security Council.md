The National Security Council (NSC) is the principal forum used by the President of the United States for considering national security and foreign policy matters with senior advisors and Cabinet officials.[^1] Section 101 of the [[National Security Act of 1947]] established the Council to advise the President on the integration of domestic, foreign and military policies relating to national security.[^1] Section 102(d)(5) of the act allowed the Council to direct the [[Central Intelligence Agency]] (CIA) to perform "such other functions and duties related to intelligence affecting the national security as the National Security Council may from time to time direct."[^2] The Council used that authority to issue written directives that governed the agency's covert operations. Subcommittees of the Council, later the Special Group (5412), the 303 Committee and the 40 Committee, reviewed and approved those operations.

### Covert directives, 1947 to 1948

On December 17, 1947 the Council approved NSC 4-A, which directed the [[Director of Central Intelligence]] to "initiate and conduct, within the limit of available funds, covert psychological operations designed to counteract Soviet and Soviet-inspired activities which constitute a threat to world peace and security or are designed to discredit and defeat the United States in its endeavors to promote world peace and security."[^3] The directive charged the Director of Central Intelligence with ensuring that such operations were consistent with United States foreign policy and overt foreign information activities. The minutes of the Council's fourth meeting refer only to NSC 4. [[Harry S. Truman]] approved the companion paper, NSC 4, on December 18, 1947.[^4]

NSC 10/2, approved June 17, 1948 and dated June 18, provided that "A new Office of Special Projects shall be created within the Central Intelligence Agency to plan and conduct covert operations."[^5] [[Frank Wisner]]'s memoranda of the period carry the title Assistant Director for Policy Coordination, the title of the [[Office of Policy Coordination]] (OPC).[^6]

### Psychological operations boards

President Truman's directive of April 4, 1951 created the [[Psychological Strategy Board]] to plan and coordinate national psychological operations. The Board consisted of the Under Secretary of State, the Deputy Secretary of Defense and the Director of Central Intelligence, and reported to the Council. [[Gordon Gray]] was its first Director.[^7] NSC 10/5, approved October 23, 1951, set out the Board's functions.[^8]

Executive Order 10483, signed by [[Dwight D. Eisenhower]] on September 2, 1953, established the [[Operations Coordinating Board]] (OCB), reporting to the Council. Its members were the Under Secretary of State, who chaired it, the Deputy Secretary of Defense, the Director of the Foreign Operations Administration, the Director of Central Intelligence and a representative of the President designated by the President; the Special Assistant to the President for National Security Affairs could attend its meetings. Section 4 provided that the Psychological Strategy Board be abolished not later than sixty days after the order and its outstanding affairs wound up by the OCB.[^9][^10]

### NSC 68

The State-Defense Policy Review Group met on March 2, 10 and 16, 1950 to consider the drafts that became [[NSC-68]].[^11] The final paper is dated April 7, 1950.[^12]

### Planning Board

The NSC Planning Board prepared the papers that the Council considered. The minutes of the 225th Council meeting, November 24, 1954, list the Planning Board members present as representatives of the Department of State, the Department of Defense, the [[Foreign Operations Administration]], the [[Office of Defense Mobilization]] (ODM), the [[Joint Chiefs of Staff]], the CIA, the [[Bureau of the Budget]] and the [[Atomic Energy Commission]].[^13]

The ODM member was [[William Yandell Elliott]], a professor of government at [[Harvard University]]. The minutes of May 27, 1953 identify him as "W. Y. Elliott, ODM Planning Board Member," and the minutes of July 16, 1953 list "William Y. Elliott, Office of Defense Mobilization."[^14][^15] The Foreign Relations list of persons gives his posts as Assistant Director of the Office of Defense Mobilization, 1952 to 1953, and ODM Representative on the Planning Board of the National Security Council, 1953 to 1954.[^16] The Hoover Institution's biographical note for his papers lists him as a member of the National Security Council planning board from 1953 to 1957.[^17] The Harvard Crimson reported in July 1959, when he left for Moscow with Vice President [[Richard Nixon]] as Director of the Harvard Summer School, that since 1953 he had been a member of the National Security Council planning board and had served as assistant director of the Office of Defense Mobilization from 1951 to 1953.[^18] The Eisenhower Library's finding aid for the Council's administrative subseries lists a folder of 1949 to 1953 containing "suggestions by William Elliott re reorganization of the NSC."[^19] Kenn Thomas and Len Bracken wrote in 1998 in *Steamshovel Press* that [[Charles Marsh]] facilitated [[Marion Pettie]]'s tie to Elliott.[^20]

### NSC 5412 and the committee succession

NSC 5412, dated March 15, 1954, told the Director of Central Intelligence to consult the Operations Coordinating Board "and other departments or agencies as appropriate."[^21] The Special Group (NSC 5412) met with Secretary of Defense [[Robert McNamara]] on February 26, 1962.[^22]

NSC 5412/1 was approved on March 12, 1955.[^23] NSC 5412/2, approved December 28, 1955, superseded NSC 10/2, NSC 10/5, NSC 5412 and NSC 5412/1. It provided that designated representatives of the Secretary of State and the Secretary of Defense of the rank of Assistant Secretary or above, and a representative of the President designated for the purpose, would be advised in advance of major covert programs initiated by the CIA and would be "the normal channel for giving policy approval for such programs."[^24]

In March 1955 a Planning Coordination Group was set up within the Operations Coordinating Board. The Bureau of the Budget had proposed it as "a special grouping of OCB members chaired by Mr. Rockefeller," and [[Nelson Rockefeller]] was designated its chairman on March 10, 1955.[^25]

National Security Action Memorandum 303, signed by [[McGeorge Bundy]] on June 2, 1964 and addressed to the Secretary of State, the Secretary of Defense and the Director of Central Intelligence, stated: "Henceforth, the Special Group 5412 will be known as the 303 Committee," and that the change "in no way alters the composition, function or responsibility of the Special Group 5412 as authorized by NSC 5412/2." An undated list in the Foreign Relations volume identifies the members of the 303 Committee as Bundy, Director of Central Intelligence [[John McCone]], Acting Deputy Under Secretary of State for Political Affairs [[Llewellyn Thompson]] and Deputy Secretary of Defense [[Cyrus Vance]].[^26] The Foreign Relations annotation records that the committee considered a proposal concerning the 1964 [[Chile|Chilean]] presidential election on July 23, 1964.[^27] On October 18, 1969 Director of Central Intelligence [[Richard Helms]] sent [[Henry Kissinger]] a memorandum on the annual review of the 303 Committee's programs.[^28]

National Security Decision Memorandum 40, signed February 17, 1970, converted the 303 Committee into the [[40 Committee]], updating NSC 5412/2.[^29]

### Presidential covert-action directives

NSDD 17 of January 4, 1982, titled "National Security Decision Directive on Cuba and Central America," listed among its decisions "Support democratic forces in Nicaragua."[^30] NSDD 159 of January 18, 1985, titled "Covert Action Policy Approval and Coordination Procedures," states that "The National Security Council must coordinate all of the instruments of US national security." It made the National Security Planning Group, "as a committee of the National Security Council," the body to recommend to the President on each proposed covert action Finding, created a Planning and Coordination Group chaired by the Deputy Assistant to the President for National Security Affairs, and assigned the Senior Director for Intelligence Programs on the Council staff to act as recorder of the NSPG's minutes, to keep its records and to administer the special access security compartment protecting covert action policies.[^31]

### Psi Research

During the late 1970s, the NSC tasked remote viewers with highly sensitive intelligence problems, including the remote viewing of a new Soviet Typhoon-class submarine at [[Severodvinsk]]. The accuracy of sessions by [[Joe McMoneagle]] sparked debate within the NSC regarding the value of psychic intelligence.[^32]

Documents in the CIA's [[Stargate Project]] files refer to the Council's role in the [[Grill Flame]] program. An Army briefing text states "We were tasked by the NSC Staff in late 1979."[^33] [[Jake Stewart]] is listed as an "NSC Staff Member" at a briefing of Rose on the program.[^34] A Grill Flame schedule for August 1 lists a National Security Council stop at the Executive Office Building.[^35] A July 22, 1980 Army document (DAMI-ISH) lists the National Security Council among the officials who favor US government involvement in the field, noting that it "evidently favors" it.[^36] An information paper addressed to the Secretary of Defense states that "The NSC Staff is preparing to brief [[David Aaron]] and [[Zbigniew Brzezinski|Zbig]] on the phenomena and DoD efforts."[^37] Minutes of a Defense Department Grill Flame meeting record that the NSC regarded remote viewing work as a possible privacy act issue and that the White House legal staff was to review it.[^38]

A 1984 memorandum in the same files quotes [[Barbara Honegger]] on high-level awareness of parapsychology achievements at the [[Office of Science and Technology Policy]], the Office of Policy Development and the NSC.[^39]

### UAP records and briefings

The [[Majestic-12]] record includes the Cutler-Twining memorandum of July 14, 1954, addressed by [[Robert Cutler]], special assistant to the President, to General [[Nathan F. Twining|Twining]]. It refers to an "NSC/MJ-12 Special Studies Project" and a briefing on July 16.[^40] The National Archives wrote that its search of the National Security Council indexes was negative except for the Cutler memorandum.[^41]

A memorandum from [[H. Marshall Chadwell]] to the Director of Central Intelligence, catalogued under December 9, 1952, had attached a draft memorandum to the Council and a draft NSC directive. It stated that on August 20 the Director had directed preparation of a National Security Council directive on unidentified flying objects.[^42] The [[Robertson Panel]] met from January 14 to 17, 1953.[^43] Haines wrote that the panel recommended that the National Security Council "debunk UFO reports" and institute a policy of public education, and that following the panel's findings the Agency abandoned efforts to draft an NSC Intelligence Directive on UFOs.[^44] The panel's own report describes its proposed educational program as having the two aims of "training and 'debunking,'" and records that the panel considered the technical manpower cost of following up "every one of the thousand or more reports received through channels each year (1,900 in 1952)" unjustified.[^45]

[[Luis Elizondo]] told the House Committee on Oversight and Accountability in written testimony of November 13, 2024: "In my last position, I managed a Special Access Program on behalf of the White House and the National Security Council."[^46] The biography submitted with the testimony gives his position as Director, National Programs Special Management Staff, in the Office of the Under Secretary of Defense for Intelligence and Security.[^47] In *Imminent* he wrote that the Special Management Staff managed national-level special access programs for the NSC and that the NSC has been briefed on the [[Unidentified Anomalous Phenomena]] issue by the [[UAP Task Force]] and its successor, the [[All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office]].[^48]

[[Sean Kirkpatrick]], the first director of that office, held the post of Director, National Security Strategy, at the National Security Council, according to the Defense Department biography released on the office's establishment.[^49] Asked by [[Douglas Dean Johnson]] whether anyone in the White House, the Executive Office of the President or the NSC had pressed him to soften his opposition to the UAP Disclosure Act, Kirkpatrick replied by email on July 23, 2024: "The White House could have cared less about this issue. No one from the White House, EOP, NSC, or any other 'higher authority' called me or my boss to put pressure on me to soften my position on the UAPDA."[^50]

### Gary Sick and the October Surprise

[[Gary Sick]] was a member of the Council staff. The House Task Force report of 1993 describes him as a former NSC staff member and records that his superior was National Security Adviser Zbigniew Brzezinski.[^51] Sick published "The Election Story of the Decade" in the *New York Times* on April 15, 1991 and the book *October Surprise* the same year, alleging that the 1980 [[Ronald Reagan]] campaign negotiated with Iranian representatives over the release of the American hostages.[^51] The Task Force, chaired by [[Lee Hamilton]], gave Sick formal interviews and took his sworn deposition. Its report records his testimony that Jamshid Hashemi was his exclusive source for what was discussed at the alleged meetings in Madrid. The Task Force's joint report of January 3, 1993 stated that it "explains why either wholly insufficient or no credible evidence exists to substantiate the allegations that form the October Surprise theory."[^51]

### Central America and the Enterprise

[[Gary Webb]] wrote that under the Reagan administration the NSC became an operational intelligence agency based inside the [[White House]]. It ran [[Oliver North]]'s illegal Contra supply network, known as "The Enterprise," after Congress cut off military aid through the [[Boland Amendment]].[^52] The independent counsel's final report states that the Council's staff "at times became operational, taking on primary responsibility for the execution of the Iran and contra covert operations."[^53]

Webb wrote that CIA records showed that Hangar No. 4 at [[Ilopango Airbase]] was used by the Agency for covert Contra operations until it was turned over in 1985 to the NSC and North's network.[^54] The CIA Inspector General's report gives a different account. It states that in 1984 the CIA moved its activities from Hangar 4 to a newly built hangar, Hangar 5, and that "After CIA had moved out of Hangar 4, it was used in 1985 and 1986 by NHAO and the Private Benefactors in support of their Contra-related operations."[^55] The report says a March 18, 1986 DEA report stated that the pilot [[Carlos Amador]] had recently flown from [[Costa Rica]] to [[San Salvador]], where he had access to Hangar 4. A CIA officer who drafted one cable surmised that another entity operating from Hangar 4 "may have been associated either with Oliver North, the Private Benefactors, or the [[NHAO|Nicaraguan Humanitarian Assistance Office]] (NHAO)."[^55]

The CIA Inspector General's report, in its section on [[Moises Nunez]] (paragraphs 480 to 496), records that on March 25, 1987 the CIA questioned him about narcotics trafficking allegations and that "Nunez revealed that since 1985, he had engaged in a clandestine relationship with the National Security Council (NSC)." He "refused to elaborate on the nature of these actions but indicated it was difficult to answer questions relating to his involvement in narcotics trafficking because of the specific tasks he had performed at the direction of the NSC," and he refused to identify the NSC officials involved. A headquarters cable of April 1987 first said a decision had been made to debrief him; the next day a headquarters cable stated "Headquarters has decided against . . . debriefing Nunez," without explanation.[^56] The CIA officer Louis Dupart told the Inspector General that the Agency position was to turn the matter over to others because "it had nothing to do with the Agency, but with the National Security Council." [[Alan Fiers]] wrote to the Inspector General: "because of the NSC connection and the possibility that this could be somehow connected to the Private Benefactor program (otherwise known as the Iran Contra affair) a decision was made not to pursue this matter, but rather to turn it over to Judge Walsh."[^56] On April 10, 1987 CIA officials briefed Senators Warren Rudman and William Cohen of the Senate Select Committee on Secret Military Assistance to Iran and the Nicaraguan Opposition on Nunez's claim, and the senators asked that committee staff interview him. The report states that an interview of Nunez in San Jose in September 1987 indicated that he denied any relationship with the NSC or with anyone doing work for it.[^56]

Webb quoted the Inspector General's passage in *Dark Alliance*, naming the subject as Nunez, the Frigorificos de Puntarenas principal.[^57] Webb's text separately names a Bay of Pigs veteran, Dagoberto Nunez, as a Costa Rica based associate of North and the CIA who obtained a contract with H&M Corp. for an intelligence-gathering operation against Nicaraguan president [[Daniel Ortega]], and who with [[Felipe Vidal]] was part of the Costa Rican maritime operations.[^58] Both men are described as Cuban Bay of Pigs veterans, and both appear in [[Robert Owen]]'s memoranda to North on shrimp boats and shrimping ventures in Costa Rica. Whether Dagoberto Nunez and Moises Nunez are one person is not stated in the Inspector General's report, the Senate report or Webb's text; the Inspector General and the Senate subcommittee use only the name Moises. See [[Dagoberto Nunez]].

The Senate subcommittee report names a Moises Nunez who, with Frank Chanes, participated in Contra military assistance operations in 1984 and 1985. It says he was employed by Frigorificos de Puntarenas, which the report describes as a drug money laundering front, and by Glenn Robinette on behalf of the Second-North Enterprise, and that the former CIA Costa Rica chief of station [[Thomas Castillo]] told the Iran-Contra committees that Nunez was involved in "a very sensitive operation" for the Enterprise.[^59]

The Senate subcommittee chaired by [[John Kerry]] reported that "The NSC ordered all relevant agencies to withhold their files on [[Panama]] from the GAO," and that the subcommittee "has not been able to undertake a full analysis of how the Noriega problem was handled by the U.S. prior to his indictment."[^60] In a letter of July 13, 1988 to the General Accounting Office, [[Nicholas Rostow]], Special Assistant to the President and Legal Adviser at the Council, described the GAO's request for files on [[Manuel Noriega]] as seeking "sensitive law enforcement and intelligence files covering a substantial period of time."[^60]

### Iran-Contra staff

The Council's role became public after the October 1986 shootdown of a CIA cargo plane over [[Nicaragua]], which exposed the [[Iran-Contra Affair]]. North was indicted on sixteen felony counts.[^52] The scandal revealed that the Council had been selling arms to [[Iran]] and diverting profits to the [[Contras]] in violation of congressional prohibitions.[^52] The independent counsel's executive summary states that "both the Iran and contra operations, separately, violated United States policy and law."[^61] The congressional committees investigating the affair published their report in 1987.[^62]

Walsh wrote that the Council's staff "at times became operational" under [[Robert McFarlane]], who "did not shrink from the operational tasks that were of high personal interest to the President."[^53] The report also states that McFarlane "apparently played no role in approving or implementing the Iran/contra diversion."[^63] Walsh found that North "had working control of the Secord-Hakim covert-action Enterprise," a network of [[Richard Secord]] and [[Albert Hakim]].[^64]

President Reagan announced on December 4, 1985 the resignation of McFarlane and the appointment of Vice Admiral [[John Poindexter]] as Assistant to the President for National Security Affairs.[^65] The Tower Commission wrote that at the beginning of December 1985 McFarlane resigned and Poindexter succeeded him.[^66] The independent counsel's executive summary states that Reagan's 1985 decision "to authorize the sale of arms to Iran from [[Israel|Israeli]] stocks, despite warnings by Weinberger and Shultz that such transfers might violate the law, opened the way for Poindexter's subsequent decision to authorize the diversion." It records that Poindexter told Congress that "while he made the decision on his own and did not tell the President, he believed the President would have approved."[^61]

The [[Tower Commission]] wrote in 1987 that "it is the Assistant for National Security Affairs who seems to have had the least access to expert counsel familiar with his activities."[^67]
