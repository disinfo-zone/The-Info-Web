The Atomic Energy Commission (AEC) was established by the Atomic Energy Act of 1946, approved August 1, 1946, as a body of five members appointed by the President with the consent of the Senate, one of whom the President designated Chairman.[^1] The act defined "restricted data" as "all data concerning the manufacture or utilization of atomic weapons, the production of fissionable material, or the use of fissionable material in the production of power" (unless the Commission determined it could be published), made communication of such data with intent to injure the United States or to secure an advantage to a foreign nation punishable by death or life imprisonment on a jury's recommendation, barred any individual from access until the Federal Bureau of Investigation had made an investigation and report, and created a [[Joint Committee on Atomic Energy]] of both houses of Congress to which the Commission was to keep itself fully informed.[^1] The Energy Reorganization Act of 1974, signed October 11, 1974, provided in section 101 that "the Atomic Energy Commission is hereby abolished" and transferred its functions to a new [[Energy Research and Development Administration]] and a [[Nuclear Regulatory Commission]]; both began operations on January 19, 1975.[^2][^3]

### The Israeli Atomic Energy Commission

The name was also borne by an Israeli body. [[Seymour Hersh]] wrote in *The Samson Option* that the Israeli Atomic Energy Commission was established in 1952, chaired by the organic chemist [[Ernst David Bergmann]], and operated under the direct jurisdiction of [[Shimon Peres]] and the Ministry of Defense; Hersh describes it as publicly presented as a body for peaceful nuclear research whose primary objective was the development of [[Israel]]'s nuclear weapons program.[^4]

### Atoms for Peace and Nahal Soreq

Hersh also wrote that under the [[Dwight D. Eisenhower|Eisenhower]] administration's [[Atoms for Peace]] program the United States commission helped finance and fuel a small research reactor at Nahal Soreq in Israel, under an agreement that included inspection rights for the United States to ensure nuclear materials were not diverted to weapons research, and that [[Lewis L. Strauss]], chairman of the commission, and his successor John McCone were both briefed on the Israeli nuclear program, though their responses and actions varied.[^4] Eisenhower Library records place McCone's chairmanship from July 14, 1958 to January 20, 1961, during which, according to the finding aid to his papers, he "adopted a policy of providing intelligence briefings on Soviet nuclear capabilities" to the Joint Congressional Committee on Atomic Energy, and President [[John F. Kennedy]] named him Director of Central Intelligence in 1961.[^5][^6] James R. Schlesinger, whose Central Intelligence Agency biography lists a post as a member of the commission from 1971, became [[Director of Central Intelligence]] in February 1973.[^7]

### The first American visit to Dimona

According to the National Security Archive's collection on the first American visit to [[Dimona]], President Kennedy treated the reactor as one of his top issues after taking office on January 20, 1961. The commission selected two of its scientists, [[Ulysses Staebler]], assistant director of its Reactor Development Division, and [[Jesse Croach]], a heavy water expert employed by [[DuPont]] at the commission's [[Savannah River]] facility. They arrived in Israel on May 17, 1961 and on Saturday May 20 visited Dimona, escorted by Professor [[Ephraim Katzir]] and the Dimona director [[Manes Pratt]], who briefed them on the 1957 decision to expand the nuclear program through the Dimona project. On May 26 the State Department sent National Security Adviser [[McGeorge Bundy]] a two-page memorandum on the visit in which, in the Archive's summary, the scientists "concluded that the reactor 'is of the scope and peaceful character previously described to the United States.'" The Archive's account states that the positive report was "critical for making the meeting between President Kennedy and Prime Minister [[David Ben-Gurion|Ben Gurion]] on 30 May successful" and that "the confrontation over Dimona was delayed for another two years."[^8]

### NUMEC

[[Washington Star|The Washington Star]] reported on March 23, 1978 that the former commission, "confused and embarrassed by the worst loss of nuclear materials in its history," had decided in 1966 to conceal the problem from the public on the ground that premature disclosure "could lead to sensational and probably inaccurate press reports," according to a previously classified document released by the [[Senate Governmental Affairs Committee]]. The source of the embarrassment was the unexplained loss of 202 pounds of highly enriched uranium from a plant at Apollo, Pennsylvania, discovered in late 1965; the Star's account of the document says that [[Howard C. Brown]] told a secret briefing attended by the commissioners and top staff that because the commission's safeguards system rested on a "presumption of honesty" it "might not reveal a deliberate and systematic attempt" to divert material to a foreign government, and that the commission's officials resolved their dilemma by developing a system for estimating the loss, with the occurrence date fixed at March 25, 1966.[^9] A Star article of 1978 on a [[General Accounting Office]] report, which gives the missing quantity as 208 pounds, said the [[Central Intelligence Agency|CIA]] and the [[Federal Bureau of Investigation|FBI]] had blocked its release; that the Nuclear Materials and Equipment Corporation (NUMEC), which operated the plant, was headed by a former commission chemist, [[Zalman Mordecai Shapiro]], who had business dealings with the Israeli atomic energy establishment and insisted the uranium was lost in processing; that according to documents obtained by investigators the commission and the [[U.S. Navy|Navy]] had repeatedly reprimanded Shapiro for lax security that allowed foreign visitors, including French and Israeli scientists, too much access to a plant that housed bomb-grade uranium and plutonium and "a library of classified nuclear weapons data"; and that the commission's main investigator of NUMEC, identified as Howard Brown, said the government was never able to find sufficient evidence that Shapiro was involved in illegal activity.[^10]

The same file of CIA press clippings contains the Washington Star and Washington Post reports of March 2, 1978 that [[Carl E. Duckett]] had told Nuclear Regulatory Commission officials in a closed February 1976 briefing that the CIA considered the question of whether highly enriched uranium had been diverted from the NUMEC plant "academic" because Israel had developed its own weapons, and that Duckett said that if any scheme to let material go to Israel had been under consideration "he would have known about it and he never heard so much as a rumor"; the Post added that the NRC on the preceding Saturday had released a censored 550-page report responding to a House committee inquiry, which said that NRC Executive Director [[Lee V. Gossick]] "testified incorrectly" when he told the House Commerce Committee the NRC had no evidence a significant amount of nuclear material had been stolen from NUMEC.[^11]

### Groom Lake

In April 1955 [[Richard Bissell]] and Colonel [[Osmund Ritland]] of the [[Air Force]], with [[Lockheed]]'s [[Kelly Johnson]] and test pilot [[Tony LeVier]], spotted an airstrip at Groom Lake "near the northeast corner of the Atomic Energy Commission's Nevada Proving Ground." The CIA's official history of the U-2 program records that Bissell discovered the strip lay outside the proving ground and that the Agency asked the commission to add the Groom Lake area to its holdings; the partly legible passage refers to "AEC Chairman Adm.," and Lewis Strauss was McCone's predecessor as chairman.[^12] The strip became the site later known as [[Area 51]].

### The Los Alamos conference on green fireballs

The minutes of a conference on "aerial phenomena" held at 1300 on February 16, 1949 in room P-162 of the [[Los Alamos Scientific Laboratory]], classified Secret and later declassified on December 9, 1975, list among those present, from the [[Fourth Army]], the [[Armed Forces Special Weapons Project]], the [[University of New Mexico]] (Dr. [[Lincoln LaPaz]]), the FBI (Mr. Maxwell), the University of California ([[Norris Bradbury]], Dr. Holloway, Mr. Hoyt, Dr. Manley, [[Frederick Reines]] and [[Edward Teller]]) and "USAEC, SFOO: Mr. Morgan." Dr. LaPaz described the December 12, 1948 Starvation Peak fireball as of constant intensity and horizontal path, about two seconds in duration and of a green hue not observed in meteor falls, and said of the observed paths: "I defy you to find anywhere among meteorists, examples of conventional meteorites that move over long horizontal paths." Captain Neef of the Fourth Army stated that the Air Force's Project Sign "is now project Grudge, which includes the phenomena in New Mexico," and Teller said that from what LaPaz said, "it certainly sounds like everything else but meteors."[^13]

### Wilhelm Reich and the Argonne laboratory

The FBI's New York office recorded that in August 1949 [[Wilhelm Reich]]'s associate Washington telephoned that he had been detained for questioning at the Commission's [[Argonne National Laboratory|Argonne laboratory]] after a visit to Oak Ridge; on 24 August Reich telephoned the laboratory and was told by an officer that he could not say whether Washington was there. [[Myron Sharaf]] told the FBI's New York office on October 11, 1949 that Washington had "swindled them out of some money," that Reich feared the Russians might obtain the information, and that Mayer, security officer of the Argonne laboratory, had advised Reich to report to the FBI; Sharaf said that orgone energy "might serve as some defense to rays of radio-active material or atomic energy."[^14] A security official of the Commission in New York told the Bureau that Reich had approached the Commission in New York on several occasions and had presented his experiments to the Commission in Washington, which told him they were outside its scope; in the Bureau's words, the scientists of the Commission were of the opinion that Reich was mentally unsound in his scientific experiments.[^14] A Bureau memorandum of March 25, 1954 describes the Institute's work as discredited by the Commission and the FDA.[^15] On October 7, 1955 Assistant Director L. B. Nichols wrote that on February 23, 1954 he had told Reich's attorney Asa Richardson that the Bureau had no current interest in Reich and that he might check with the Immigration and Naturalization Service and the Atomic Energy Commission.[^16]

### Seats on the intelligence advisory bodies

The Central Intelligence Agency's history of the Directors of Central Intelligence records that the commission was represented on the Intelligence Advisory Committee chaired by the Director, alongside the FBI and the military and State Department intelligence chiefs, and on its successor the United States Intelligence Board, on which Donald R. Cotter represented the commission in 1972 when the Board met under Director [[Richard Helms]].[^17]

### The commission's grants in the psi files

The CIA's STARGATE collection includes the 1972 paper by [[Stuart J. Freedman]] and [[John F. Clauser]], "Experimental Test of Local Hidden-Variable Theories," whose acknowledgements state that the work was "supported by U.S. Atomic Energy Commission," and the 1979 [[Stanford Research Institute|SRI]] biography of [[Edwin C. May]], who joined the Grill Flame remote perturbation work, lists among his publications the 1969 "Proposal to the U.S. Atomic Energy Commission for Procurement of a Computer Implemented Nuclear Data Acquisition and Control System," [[Crocker Nuclear Laboratory]] Report UCD-112.[^18]
