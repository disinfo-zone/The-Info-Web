The United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit is the federal appellate court for the District of Columbia. It hears appeals from the [[United States District Court for the District of Columbia]] and reviews federal agency action, and Congress authorized eleven judgeships for it.[^1][^2] The court traces to a statute of February 9, 1893 that established a court of appeals for the District of Columbia.[^1]

Its rulings include the appointment of independent counsels under the Ethics in Government Act, the Iran-Contra prosecutions of [[Oliver North]] and [[John Poindexter]], the 1976 car bombing that killed [[Orlando Letelier]] in Washington, the [[INSLAW]] dispute over the PROMIS software, the Scientology appeal of 1981, the Watergate and Room 16 appeals, the wiretaps of the Nixon years, the espionage case of [[Jonathan Pollard]], the Freedom of Information Act cases in which the CIA and the National Security Agency refused to confirm or deny that they held records, and the presidential records suits brought by the [[National Security Archive]]. The court also decided the [[Association of American Physicians and Surgeons]] suit over the 1993 health care task force, the Cheney energy group litigation, and several cases from 2025 and 2026 over White House access, the [[Department of Government Efficiency]], and a tax-data leak prosecution.

### Independent counsel and the Special Division

The Ethics in Government Act, codified in 28 U.S.C. chapter 40, provides for a division of the court of three judges, one of whom must be a judge of the District of Columbia Circuit, to appoint independent counsels. The Chief Justice designates the members, and the clerk of the circuit serves as the division's clerk. A judge who participated in a function of the division involving an independent counsel may not take part in a later judicial proceeding concerning a matter that involves that counsel.[^23]

The first major use of the division concerns the Iran-Contra affair. On December 4, 1986, [[Edwin Meese III]] asked for an independent counsel, and on December 19, 1986 the division appointed [[Lawrence Walsh]].[^24] The final report that Walsh delivered carries the caption "Division No. 86-6." Volume I is dated August 4, 1993 and is addressed in a transmittal letter to the division's chief judge, David Sentelle.[^24]

The division's power came under challenge in the same period. In re Sealed Case, decided January 22, 1988 by Judges Ruth Bader Ginsburg, Silberman and Stephen F. Williams, concerned three former Justice Department officials, [[Theodore B. Olson]], [[Carol E. Dinkins]] and [[Edward C. Schmults]], who refused to comply with subpoenas from an independent counsel. The inquiry grew out of a 1982 dispute between House subcommittees and the Justice Department over Environmental Protection Agency documents. The panel held the Act unconstitutional and reversed the district court's contempt orders against the three officials.[^25] Walsh's report describes the ruling as a January 1988 panel decision that struck down the law in a case brought against Independent Counsel [[Alexia Morrison]].[^24] The Supreme Court reversed on June 29, 1988 and upheld the independent counsel provisions.[^26]

### Iran-Contra appeals

Oliver North was tried before [[Gerhard Gesell]], who sentenced him on July 5, 1989 after a conviction on three counts. A panel of Chief Judge Patricia Wald, Judge Silberman and Judge Sentelle decided his appeal in United States v. North on July 20, 1990. The per curiam opinion vacated his convictions on counts 6, 9 and 10 and remanded them for a Kastigar hearing, at which the government would have to show that its evidence came from sources independent of his immunized congressional testimony. It reversed count 9 for want of a particularized unanimity instruction. Wald dissented from several holdings, and Silberman dissented from others and concurred in one.[^27][^28]

John Poindexter was convicted on five felony counts. On November 15, 1991 the court reversed all counts in United States v. Poindexter, decided by a panel of Chief Judge Abner Mikva, Judge Douglas Ginsburg and Judge Sentelle. The opinion cites the North decision as modified, 920 F.2d 940.[^29]

The Walsh report's prosecution summaries give the same dates for these rulings.[^28]

### The Letelier murder appeals

On September 21, 1976, Orlando Letelier, the former Chilean ambassador to the United States, and [[Ronni Moffitt]], an American associate, were killed in Washington by a car bomb. The court's per curiam opinion in United States v. Novo Sampol, decided September 15, 1980, recounts the case against the defendants. [[Michael Townley]], a United States citizen who had entered the employ of the Chilean intelligence service DINA, was the principal government witness, and he had struck a plea bargain with the government in return for his testimony.[^30] The reporter caption records the argument on June 10, 1980, the decision on September 15, 1980 and the denial of rehearing on December 9, 1980.[^31]

[[Guillermo Novo]], [[Ignacio Novo]] and [[Alvin Ross]] were the defendants at the first trial.[^30] The court reversed the convictions of Guillermo Novo and Ross on the authority of [[United States v. Henry]], decided by the Supreme Court on June 16, 1980.[^32] The court reversed Ignacio Novo's conviction for failure to sever his trial from his co-defendants' trial.[^30] According to the FBI case agent's account, all three were active in the [[Cuban Nationalist Movement]], an anti-Castro organization, and Guillermo Novo and Ross had been sentenced to two consecutive life terms before the appeal. Dade County police arrested Guillermo Novo and Ross on weapons and drug charges during negotiations with Townley's lawyers, and the account says that the key to conviction lay in Townley's testimony. Ignacio Novo had been charged with one count of misprision of a felony.[^33]

The FBI case agent on the investigation, [[Carter Cornick]], wrote that the reversal in September 1980 rested on Henry. On May 30, 1981 a federal jury acquitted Guillermo Novo and Ross of all murder charges and convicted Guillermo Novo of false declarations. The two Novo brothers had also been charged with two counts each of making false declarations to a grand jury. Five months later, Ignacio Novo pleaded guilty to a charge of false declarations.[^33]

### Wiretaps and the Nixon-era national security surveillance

The court's leading en banc decision on warrantless national security wiretaps is Zweibon v. Mitchell, decided June 23, 1975. Sixteen individuals who had been members of the [[Jewish Defense League]] sued Attorney General [[John N. Mitchell]] and FBI personnel over electronic surveillance of the organization's telephones. The opinion records that Attorney General Mitchell first approved an FBI request to install wiretaps on JDL headquarters in October 1970, during the twenty-fifth session of the United Nations General Assembly, fearing "international embarrassment or Soviet retaliation against American citizens living in Moscow." He approved a second request in early January 1971 for a wiretap authorized to last 90 days. An affidavit that Mitchell later submitted stated that the surveillance was authorized by the President of the United States, acting through the Attorney General. The opinion also records that neither Mitchell nor other officials in his office reviewed the information obtained from the taps or the necessity for them, and that Mitchell was unaware that the taps continued for more than a month after criminal indictments were handed down against several individuals. The plurality opinion of Judge J. Skelly Wright, announcing the judgment, held that "a warrant must be obtained before a wiretap is installed on a domestic organization that is neither the agent of nor acting in collaboration with a foreign power."[^34]

The companion case of Halperin v. Kissinger was decided on July 12, 1979 by Chief Judge Wright, Judge Robinson and District Judge Gesell.[^35] The Halperins sued over the wiretap of the home telephone of [[Morton H. Halperin]], a National Security Council staff member. The opinion recounts that FBI officials and [[Alexander M. Haig, Jr.]], then an assistant to [[Henry Kissinger]], agreed on procedures for surveillance with "no record maintained" in the Bureau's normal files. The opinion records that [[J. Edgar Hoover]] and Kissinger conferred by telephone four times on the day the wiretap on the Halperin home telephone was in place by evening. The summaries of the logs went to Kissinger, to [[Richard Nixon]] through [[John Ehrlichman]], and to Mitchell. After May 1970 the court's account names only [[H. R. Haldeman]] as a recipient. On appeal the court affirmed on the defendants' immunity questions, reversed on the applicability of Title III of the Omnibus Crime Control and Safe Streets Act of 1968 and on the measure of damages, reversed the summary judgment for Kissinger, and remanded.[^35] The Supreme Court divided equally on the appeal and the case returned to the district court. On December 5, 1986, in Halperin v. Kissinger, 807 F.2d 180, the court addressed the twenty-one month wiretap again. The opinion, printed under the name of Scalia as circuit justice with a concurrence by Judge Mikva joined by Judge Robinson, recites that the proximate impetus for the wiretap was a New York Times article of May 9, 1969 on the bombing of Cambodia, that Kissinger dispatched the FBI to trace the source at Nixon's request, and that FBI Director Hoover named Halperin the prime suspect. It affirmed summary judgment for the defendants as to the initiation of the wiretap and remanded for the district court to fix the period, ending no later than May 1970, in which no reasonable jury could find the national security purpose pretextual.[^36]

### The Watergate and Room 16 appeals

United States v. Liddy, decided November 8, 1974 by Judge Leventhal, affirmed the conviction of [[G. Gordon Liddy]] on the six counts of the September 15, 1972 indictment arising from the burglary and wiretapping of the Democratic National Committee offices on June 17, 1972. The opinion records that five of the seven defendants pleaded guilty after the trial opened before Chief Judge [[John Sirica]] in January 1973, that Liddy and [[James McCord]] were found guilty on January 30, 1973, and that the government's evidence concerned the efforts of Liddy, [[Howard Hunt]] and McCord to obtain political intelligence from the headquarters of Democratic presidential candidates and from the committee.[^37]

On October 12, 1976 the court sitting en banc decided United States v. Haldeman in a per curiam opinion, with Judge MacKinnon dissenting in part. The indictment of March 1, 1974 had named Mitchell, Haldeman, Ehrlichman, [[Charles Colson]], [[Robert Mardian]], [[Kenneth Parkinson]] and [[Gordon Strachan]]. The court described the charges as an unprecedented scandal at the highest levels of government. The jury returned its verdict on January 1, 1975, acquitting Parkinson, convicting Mardian of conspiracy, and convicting Mitchell, Haldeman and Ehrlichman of conspiracy and obstruction of justice and of the perjury counts submitted. The three were sentenced to concurrent terms of 20 months to five years on the conspiracy and obstruction counts. The opinion calls the intelligence plan code-named Gemstone the brainchild of Liddy, general counsel of the Committee to Re-elect the President, and affirmed the convictions of the three.[^38]

Three appeals decided on May 17, 1976 by a panel of Judges Leventhal and Wilkey and District Judge Robert Merhige concerned the September 1971 entry into the Beverly Hills office of the psychiatrist of [[Daniel Ellsberg]]. The opinions describe a White House unit formed after the publication of the Pentagon Papers, called Room 16, under the general supervision of Ehrlichman and operated by [[Egil Krogh]] and [[David Young]], to which Liddy and Hunt were added. In United States v. Ehrlichman, Wilkey wrote for the court affirming the convictions of Ehrlichman for conspiracy and perjury.[^39] In United States v. Barker the court reversed the judgment against [[Bernard Barker]] and [[Eugenio Martinez]] and remanded for a new trial, with Wilkey and Merhige filing opinions and Leventhal dissenting. Wilkey's opinion records that Hunt had been Barker's immediate supervisor in the CIA's [[Bay of Pigs Invasion]] operation, and that in August 1971 Hunt asked Barker to help conduct an entry to obtain national security information on "a traitor to this country who was passing classified information to the Soviet Embassy."[^40] United States v. Liddy, decided the same day with Merhige writing, affirmed Liddy's conviction for conspiring to violate the Fourth Amendment rights of the psychiatrist; Gesell had sentenced him on July 31, 1974 to one to three years concurrent with his Watergate sentence.[^41]

On June 21, 1983 Judges Mikva, Edwards and Scalia decided Gray v. Bell, with Edwards writing. After a two-year Justice Department investigation begun in the spring of 1976 into FBI break-ins against relatives and acquaintances of suspected members of the Weatherman Underground, a grand jury indicted L. Patrick Gray III, who had served as Acting Director of the FBI, with Mark Felt and Edward Miller on a charge of conspiring to injure and oppress citizens. Prosecutors later agreed to dismissal, and Gray sued [[Griffin Bell]] and other Justice Department officials for damages. The court affirmed the dismissal of his amended complaint on grounds of official immunity and the discretionary function exception.[^42]

### The Scientology prosecutions

United States v. Heldt, decided October 2, 1981 as a per curiam opinion with a separate opinion by Judge Wald, affirmed the judgments against members of the [[Church of Scientology]] who had been convicted of conspiracy and related offenses. The appellants included [[Mary Sue Hubbard]] and [[Henning Heldt]], whom the court identifies as ranking officers of the Guardians in the United States, with offices in Hollywood, California. The appellants challenged evidence seized in searches of Scientology offices in California. The district court denied the suppression motion after a hearing, and the court of appeals affirmed the judgment.[^43] The organization was founded by [[L. Ron Hubbard]].

### INSLAW and the PROMIS appeals

INSLAW, Inc., the developer of the PROMIS case-tracking software, filed for reorganization and invoked the automatic stay of 11 U.S.C. 362(a) in its dispute with the Justice Department over the Department's continued use of the software. The bankruptcy court's ruling is reported at 83 B.R. 89 (Bankr. D.D.C. 1988). The district court's opinion of November 22, 1989, reported at 113 B.R. 802, recites that ruling and the Justice Department's argument that the bankruptcy judge should have recused himself. The opinion describes the circuit's January 11, 1988 decision not to reappoint [[George Francis Bason, Jr.]] as the factual setting for that argument.[^44]

The circuit's opinion in United States v. INSLAW, Inc., decided May 7, 1991 by Judges Buckley, Stephen Williams and Randolph, with Williams writing for the court, held that the automatic stay did not reach the Department's use of property in its possession under a claim of right at the time of the bankruptcy filing. It held that the bankruptcy court therefore lacked jurisdiction over the claims asserted under section 362(a). The court reversed and remanded with directions to vacate the orders and dismiss INSLAW's complaint against the Department.[^45] Rehearing and rehearing en banc were denied in No. 90-5052 on July 12, 1991.[^45] The opinion records that INSLAW filed a claim with the contracting officer on October 17, 1985 under the Contract Disputes Act, that the contracting officer ruled against INSLAW on February 21, 1986, and that the Department's use of the enhanced software was the subject of the stay claim.[^45] According to the House Judiciary Committee's report of September 10, 1992, INSLAW's counsel [[Leigh Ratiner]] wrote on March 14, 1986 to [[Peter Videnieks]], whom the report treats as the contracting officer, that any continued use of the enhanced PROMIS software without INSLAW's consent contravened its property rights as a debtor and violated the automatic stay. The circuit's holding was that the stay "does not reach the Department's use of property in its possession under a claim of right at the time of the bankruptcy filing, even if that use may ultimately prove to violate the bankrupt's rights."[^46][^45]

### FOIA and the Glomar responses

The Glomar response dates to Phillippi v. Central Intelligence Agency, decided November 16, 1976 by Judge Wright, with a dissent by Judge MacKinnon. The request concerned the Hughes Glomar Explorer. The press stories that prompted it said that the ship's actual owner and operator was the United States Government. The CIA answered that "in the interest of national security, involvement by the U.S. Government in the activities which are the subject matter of your request can neither be confirmed nor denied."[^47] The court of appeals reversed and remanded for a public justification of the refusal to confirm or deny the existence of records.[^47]

McGehee v. Central Intelligence Agency, decided January 4, 1983 by Judges Wright, Edwards and Bork, arose from a request by [[Fielding McGehee]] for CIA records on the [[People's Temple]]. The agency's search used a cut-off date of December 22, 1978 that it had not disclosed. The court wrote that "the cumulative weight of this evidence of bad faith is enough to vitiate the credit to which agency affidavits are ordinarily entitled." It reversed and remanded.[^48]

Electronic Privacy Information Center v. National Security Agency, decided May 11, 2012 by Judges Brown and Kavanaugh and Senior Judge Ginsburg, affirmed the NSA's Glomar response under Exemption 3 and section 6 of the National Security Agency Act. The request followed a January 2010 cyberattack on Google that targeted Gmail accounts of Chinese human rights activists. The agency answered on March 10, 2010 that it could neither confirm nor deny the existence of responsive records.[^49]

National Security Archive v. Central Intelligence Agency, decided June 7, 2024 by Judge Childs for the court, with Judge Rao and Senior Judge Ginsburg, concerned a January 1989 end-of-tour report addendum by a senior Air Force intelligence officer on an elevated Soviet alert status in 1983. The CIA produced the memorandum's cover letter and withheld the rest under Exemptions 1 and 3. The Archive argued that the memorandum had been published in the State Department's Foreign Relations of the United States in February 2021, so that the agency had waived the exemptions under the official acknowledgment and public domain doctrines. The court disagreed. It noted that the volume cited a CIA source and thanked CIA staff for arranging access to agency records, and that publication in the series requires declassification review by the originating agency. It affirmed the judgment for the agency.[^50]

### Presidential records and the National Security Archive

National Security Archive v. Department of Defense, decided July 28, 1989 by Judges Robinson, Mikva and Douglas Ginsburg, held that the Archive, which described its work as collecting and disseminating comprehensive government documentation on foreign, defense, intelligence and international economic policy, was not an educational institution but was a representative of the news media entitled to reduced fees under the Freedom of Information Act.[^51]

In Armstrong v. Bush, decided January 25, 1991 by Judges Wald, Douglas Ginsburg and Randolph, with Wald writing, [[Scott Armstrong]], the Archive and other plaintiffs sued President George H. W. Bush, the Archivist and the National Security Council to prevent the erasure of material on the NSC computer system from the last two weeks of the Reagan administration. The court reversed in part, affirmed in part and remanded.[^52] On August 13, 1993 a panel of Chief Judge Mikva and Judges Wald and Henderson decided Armstrong v. Executive Office of the President, 1 F.3d 1274, in a per curiam opinion rejecting the government's position that paper printouts satisfied the Federal Records Act, because the printouts could omit the identity of the sender or recipient and the time of receipt of the electronic record.[^53] On August 2, 1996 Judges Edwards, Ginsburg and Tatel decided the next round: Ginsburg, for the court, held that the National Security Council is not an agency subject to the Freedom of Information Act, because it does not exercise substantial independent authority, and reversed the district court; Tatel dissented.[^54]

National Security Archive v. Central Intelligence Agency, 752 F.3d 460, decided May 20, 2014 by Judges Rogers, Kavanaugh and Williams, concerned the fifth volume of the CIA staff historian Jack Pfeiffer's five-volume history of the Bay of Pigs operation, begun in 1973. The CIA had released revised drafts of the first three volumes and the draft of the fourth, and withheld the fifth under Exemption 5. Kavanaugh wrote for the court affirming on the deliberative process privilege; Rogers dissented.[^55] On May 28, 2019 Judges Tatel, Pillard and Edwards decided Citizens for Responsibility and Ethics in Washington v. Trump, in which the organization and the Archive sought mandamus against the use of White House messaging applications that delete messages once read. The court affirmed the denial of the writ because the plaintiffs had not shown a clear and indisputable right to relief.[^56]

Morley v. Central Intelligence Agency, decided December 7, 2007 by Judges Henderson, Rogers and Tatel, with Rogers writing, concerned the request of the journalist [[Jefferson Morley]] of July 4, 2003 for all records pertaining to the CIA operations officer George Efythron Joannides, also known as Howard, Mr. Howard or Walter Newby. The court reversed the grant of summary judgment to the agency and remanded so that the CIA could search its operational files, holding that its release of records under the John F. Kennedy Assassination Records Collection Act did not relieve it of its obligations under the Freedom of Information Act.[^57] On June 18, 2013 a per curiam opinion with a concurrence by Kavanaugh vacated the denial of attorney's fees and remanded; the court described Joannides as the CIA case officer in charge of a Cuban group whose officers had contact with Lee Harvey Oswald in the months before the assassination.[^58]

### Financial General and the pendent jurisdiction ruling

Financial General Bankshares, Inc. v. Metzger, decided May 18, 1982 by Chief Judge Wright, Judge MacKinnon and Judge Ginsburg, arose from a suit that [[Financial General Bankshares]] filed on February 17, 1978 against persons buying its shares after it learned that a group of Middle Eastern investors held twenty percent of its common stock. [[Eugene Metzger]], an attorney who had served as its counsel, was one of the defendants. The opinion records that the Securities and Exchange Commission filed and settled its own action on March 18, 1978 and that on April 27, 1978 the district court entered a preliminary injunction. The court of appeals held that the district court abused its discretion in exercising pendent jurisdiction. It vacated the judgment and remanded with instructions to dismiss, without reaching the merits.[^59]

### The Fairness Doctrine, Puharich and psychic claims

In Puharich v. Brenner, decided June 25, 1969 with Leventhal writing, [[Andrija Puharich]] appealed a judgment for the Commissioner of Patents in an action under 35 U.S.C. 145 for a patent on a device the opinion describes as a cage with electrical installations in the screen casing for generating currents and impulses, claimed to expand extrasensory perception. The court wrote that the trial judge had assumed for the purposes of the case that an ESP phenomenon existed and had asked whether the invention was operable, and affirmed.[^60]

The [[Committee for the Scientific Investigation of Claims of the Paranormal]] filed a complaint with the [[Federal Communications Commission]] under the Fairness Doctrine against the NBC program "Exploring the Unknown." After the commission rejected the complaint, CSICOP petitioned the court on April 27, 1979. The committee's periodical reported the appeal in its Fall 1979 issue.[^61]

In a later fairness case, [[Fairness in Media]] petitioned the court on May 18, 1987 (No. 87-1217) to review a commission ruling on [[CBS]], and the court affirmed the commission in an unpublished per curiam opinion on June 8, 1988, assuming without deciding that the group was positioned to seek review. The Supreme Court denied certiorari on October 11, 1988.[^62]

The same court had heard a broadcast dispute fifteen years earlier, in No. 18,738, in which the Citizens TV Protest Committee and [[Clarksburg Publishing Company]] appealed a March 13, 1964 FCC order. That order assigned the license of WBOY-TV in Clarksburg, West Virginia, from [[Rust Craft Broadcasting Company]] to Northern West Virginia Television Broadcasting Company, a wholly owned subsidiary of the [[Fortnightly Corporation]]. The appellants' brief states that the commission granted the assignment without an evidentiary hearing, that Fortnightly owned and operated a community antenna system in Clarksburg, and that the acquisition "would give Fortnightly, owner and publisher of the Reporter Magazine, an additional medium for public discussion of national affairs." The brief also questioned whether a hearing should have been held on a Fortnightly statement about the involvement of its organization in recent antitrust violations. The brief lists [[Max Ascoli]] as a vice president and director of Northern West Virginia Television Broadcasting Company.[^63]

The court's clearest psi ruling is Geller v. Randi, decided December 9, 1994 by Judges Wald, Sentelle and Rogers, with Sentelle writing for the court. The suit was brought by [[Uri Geller]]. CSICOP moved on January 15, 1992 for summary judgment and for Rule 11 sanctions. Geller did not respond to the motions, and the district court imposed sanctions. The court of appeals affirmed the Rule 11 sanctions.[^64]

### The 1993 health care task force

Association of American Physicians & Surgeons, Inc. v. Clinton, decided June 22, 1993 by Judges Silberman, Buckley and Williams, concerned the President's Task Force on National Health Care Reform, which [[Hillary Clinton]] chaired. The plaintiffs argued that the task force was subject to the Federal Advisory Committee Act. The task force comprised about 300 permanent federal employees and about 40 special government employees. The majority held that the task force was not an advisory group subject to the act, and it remanded for further proceedings on the working group. Its reasoning relied in part on 3 U.S.C. 105(e), which allows the President to designate a member of his family to assist him, and on whether the First Lady could be treated as a de facto officer or employee. Judge Buckley, concurring in the judgment, wrote that he did not think section 105(e) could reasonably be read to create an officer or employee, de facto or otherwise.[^65]

### Cheney and the energy group

The National Energy Policy Development Group, which [[Dick Cheney]] chaired, was sued by [[Judicial Watch]] under the Federal Advisory Committee Act. On July 8, 2003 Judges Edwards, Randolph and Tatel considered the government's petition for a writ of mandamus against a discovery order of the district court.[^66] On June 24, 2004 the Supreme Court vacated the court of appeals' judgment and remanded the case.[^67]

### Schneider and the Chile suits

The Schneider family's original complaint, filed in the district court on September 10, 2001, named Henry Kissinger, the [[United States]] and [[Richard Helms]]. The amended complaint of November 11, 2002 named Kissinger and the United States, and the district court dismissed the case on March 30, 2004. In Schneider v. Kissinger, decided June 28, 2005 by Judges Sentelle, Henderson and Rogers, the court affirmed the dismissal on political question grounds.[^68]

### The Pollard appeals

In United States v. Pollard, decided March 20, 1992 by Judges Ruth Bader Ginsburg, Silberman and Williams, with Silberman writing, the court affirmed the denial of the motion of Pollard under 28 U.S.C. 2255 to withdraw his guilty plea of June 4, 1986 to one count of conspiracy to deliver national defense information to a foreign government. The opinion describes Pollard as a Navy intelligence research specialist who removed large amounts of classified material over about eighteen months from June 1984 through November 1985. Theodore Olson argued for Pollard. His motion claimed that the government had linked the plea of [[Anne Henderson-Pollard]] to his own, had breached the plea agreement in its sentencing allocution, and had given Chief Judge Aubrey Robinson ex parte communications; the district court declined to recuse and denied access to the classified sentencing submission of [[Caspar Weinberger]]. The panel held that any error in refusing new counsel access to the Weinberger submission was harmless. Williams concurred in part and dissented in part.[^69]

On July 22, 2005 Judges Sentelle, Henderson and Rogers decided United States v. Pollard, 416 F.3d 48. Sentelle, for the court, held that Pollard's second section 2255 motion, filed September 20, 2000, was untimely and declined a certificate of appealability, and held that the federal courts lack jurisdiction to review claims for access to documents needed for a clemency petition under Article II, vacating the order on the request of his counsel for access to the classified sentencing file. Rogers concurred in part and dissented in part.[^70]

### The Esquire article and McFarlane

McFarlane v. Esquire Magazine, decided January 30, 1996 by Judges Stephen Williams, Ginsburg and Randolph, arose from the libel suit that [[Robert McFarlane]] brought against [[Esquire Magazine]] and the writer [[Craig Unger]]. The court affirmed the dismissal of the claims against Unger for lack of personal jurisdiction, and it affirmed the decision to dismiss rather than transfer the case.[^71] The opinion by Judge Williams records that the district court held that no reasonable jury could find actual malice by clear and convincing evidence, and it reproduces the disputed passage of the October 1991 article, which attributed to a sworn affidavit that [[Elliot Richardson]] submitted for INSLAW the statement of [[Ari Ben-Menashe]] that McFarlane and [[Earl Brian]] had a "special relationship" with Israeli intelligence, McFarlane having been recruited by [[Rafael Eitan]], and that "McFarlane was the famous Mr. X in the Pollard case." The court wrote that the recruitment clause "is not in the affidavit," that nothing in the affidavit supported the article's following sentences making McFarlane out to be an equivalent of Pollard, and that McFarlane did not dispute that Ben-Menashe made these assertions to Unger.[^71] The opinion also states that a bipartisan task force of the House of Representatives rejected the October Surprise claims in January 1993.[^71]

In McFarlane v. Sheridan Square Press, decided August 16, 1996 by Judges Williams, Ginsburg and Rogers, with Ginsburg writing, the court affirmed summary judgment for the publisher of Ben-Menashe's 1992 book Profits of War, on the ground that McFarlane could not show actual malice. The opinion summarizes the book's allegation of an October Surprise conspiracy involving candidate George Bush, Republicans in the 1980 campaign and Iranian officials, and Ben-Menashe's allegation that McFarlane played an important role and was an Israeli spy. The court wrote that the House task force's joint report had thoroughly discredited the October Surprise story.[^72]

### Microsoft

The court's most consequential antitrust decision in the record is United States v. Microsoft Corp., decided June 28, 2001 by the court sitting en banc in a per curiam opinion. The court affirmed in part and reversed in part the findings that Microsoft had violated sections 1 and 2 of the Sherman Act, and it addressed Microsoft's claims of judicial misconduct and appearance of partiality against the trial judge.[^73]

### Recent cases

The 2025 cases on the court's docket include In re Musk, No. 25-5072. The defendants in New Mexico v. Musk petitioned for a writ of mandamus on March 18, 2025 against an expedited discovery order that the district court had entered on March 12 in the states' suit. The circuit granted an emergency stay, ordering on March 26, 2025 that the March 12 order be stayed pending further order, and the district court stayed its own order the same day; the district court's opinion of June 10, 2025 recites both.[^74] In a separate matter, the court decided No. 25-5130 on July 14, 2025 and denied rehearing on December 18, 2025, and the government's petition for certiorari in that case, No. 25-1103, was filed on March 18, 2026.[^75]

In Associated Press v. Budowich, No. 25-5109, a panel of Judges Pillard, Katsas and Rao on June 6, 2025 granted a stay pending appeal in part, staying the district court's injunction except as applied to the East Room. Judge Rao, joined by Judge Katsas, wrote that "these restricted presidential spaces are not First Amendment fora opened for private speech and discussion" and that "[t]he White House therefore retains discretion to determine, including on the basis of viewpoint, which journalists will be admitted." Judge Pillard would have denied the stay.[^76] On July 22, 2025 the court denied the Associated Press's motion for en banc reconsideration and vacatur of the stay order.[^77]

The Commodity Futures Trading Commission appealed as No. 24-5205 after [[Jia M. Cobb]] granted summary judgment on September 12, 2024 to [[Kalshi]] on the legality of its event contracts. On May 5, 2025, after a 3 to 0 commission vote, the agency moved to dismiss its appeal voluntarily, each side bearing its own costs. [[Yaakov Roth]] argued the appeal for Kalshi in January 2025 and joined the Justice Department the following month, according to Protos.[^78]

In United States v. Littlejohn, No. 24-3019, decided July 17, 2026, a panel of Judges Rao and Walker, with Senior Judge Rogers, affirmed a five-year sentence for a former IRS consultant who leaked taxpayer data. Judge Walker wrote the opinion. The court wrote that Littlejohn "leaked what he stole to [[ProPublica]], which used data regarding at least 152 of his victims in about 50 articles," and it added that "ProPublica is sitting on the private, as-yet-unpublished data of other taxpayers stolen by Littlejohn."[^79]
