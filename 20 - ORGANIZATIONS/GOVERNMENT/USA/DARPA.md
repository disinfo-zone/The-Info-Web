The Defense Advanced Research Projects Agency (DARPA) is a research and development agency of the [[United States Department of Defense]] that funds advanced technology for military use. It was established in 1958 as the Advanced Research Projects Agency (ARPA).[^1]

### Names, leadership and headquarters

[[Barry M. Leiner]] and coauthors date the first renaming to DARPA to 1971, a return to ARPA in 1993, and a second return to DARPA in 1996.[^2] A history prepared for the agency by the [[Institute for Defense Analyses]] gives 1972 for the renaming to DARPA.[^1] A letter from the agency to [[Warren G. Magnuson]], chairman of the Committee on Commerce, Science, and Transportation, gave its headquarters as 1400 Wilson Boulevard, Arlington, Virginia.[^3]

The same Institute for Defense Analyses history lists [[Jack Ruina]] as director from 1961 to 1963, [[Robert Sproull]] from 1963 to 1965, and [[Charles Herzfeld]] from 1965 to 1967. [[Eberhardt Rechtin]] became director in 1967 after about six months in which the deputy director, [[Peter Franken]], served as acting director. [[Stephen Lukasik]] became director in 1971, and [[George H. Heilmeier]] was named by the Director of Defense Research and Engineering, [[Malcolm Currie]], with [[James Schlesinger]] as Secretary of Defense. [[Robert Fossum]] became director in 1977, [[Robert Cooper]] in 1981 and [[Robert Duncan]] in 1985, and [[Raymond Colladay]], formerly of [[NASA]], served as director for 18 months. The history describes the agency's Vietnam-era role as operating particularly through its Advanced Sensor program.[^1] [[Anthony Tether]] was director in August 2003.[^4]

### Long-horizon research and military psychology

ARPA was designed to sponsor projects whose results would not arrive for ten to fifteen years, providing a funding mechanism for long-horizon military science outside standard procurement budgets. Its [[Arlington, Virginia]] headquarters sat in the same building complex as the [[USARIBSS|Army Research Institute]] and other military psychological research organizations. In the psychological sciences its Cold War work concentrated on biofeedback research and early brain-weapon studies (including whether pilots' brain states could be linked to missile systems), and on the psychology of insurgent organizations, with [[Vietnam War|Vietnam]] as the primary focus. The most prominent ARPA-commissioned psychological study was the [[Rand Corporation]]'s Viet Cong motivation and morale study of the early 1960s, and ARPA also sponsored [[Project Agile]], a series of Asian military-science studies that included the [[Battelle Memorial Institute]] smell-weapons research of 1966. A 1964 classified memorandum from the Director of Defense Research and Engineering placed ARPA in an integrating role for behavioral and social science research across the service branches.[^5]

### Early psychic inquiries, 1972 to 1973

[[Ray Hyman]] wrote that he visited SRI on December 8, 1972, with the agency's George Lawrence.[^6] Beaumont wrote that in 1972 the agency sent a team to evaluate the claimed results obtained at [[Stanford Research Institute]] (SRI) with the spoon-bender [[Uri Geller]], headed by [[George Lawrence]], a psychologist open-minded on parapsychology, and including Hyman, an accomplished magician, and [[Robert Van de Castle]], a University of Virginia sleep and dream expert and a past president of the [[Parapsychological Association|American Parapsychological Association]]. Beaumont described the team's report as totally negative.[^7] A CIA Office of Research and Development memorandum of January 24, 1973 refers to "Drs. Austin Kibler and George Lawrence" of ARPA as having been to SRI to observe Geller, [[Hal Puthoff]] and [[Russell Targ]].[^8] [[Jim Schnabel]] wrote that the agency sent a three-man team to look at Geller at SRI in early 1973, one member of which was a University of Virginia psychologist While the agency's corporate stance was not to pursue Geller further, Schnabel wrote, Van de Castle was impressed by Geller's abilities.[^9]

[[Time]] reported in 1974 that Hyman, accompanied by Lawrence, described as a DoD projects manager for the Advanced Research Projects Agency, caught Geller in some outright deceptions. Time quoted Hyman as calling the SRI tests conducted with what he termed "incredible sloppiness."[^10]

[[Michael Zarbo]], an Army captain writing a master's thesis at the [[Defense Intelligence Agency]] college in 1992, citing [[Ronald McRae]]'s [[Mind Wars]], wrote that Lawrence "was ordered by his superiors to find something to satisfy Congressional demands to match purported Soviet efforts in the field."[^11]

Beaumont wrote that the Army awarded a contract of $145,000 to examine Kirlian photography in 1976 and that the researchers found the "aura" to be an interaction of body moisture and the electric field.[^7] [[David Suter]], in an undated [[Washington Post]] article, attributed a $145,000 contract to the Defense Department's Advanced Research Projects Agency, placing it "last year."[^12]

Suter reported that DARPA spokesperson Jane Smith denied that the agency had any interest in psychic research or in Hal Puthoff and Russell Targ. He reported that Director George H. Heilmeier had stated that the agency had not received, and was not considering, a formal or informal proposal on psychic research. According to a paid consultant who briefed the agency, SRI had proposed a $3.5 million program on remote viewing. Suter also reported that Colonel Austin Kibler, director of DARPA's human resources division in 1973, confided to a consulting psychologist that "it could be that we are pretending to support this [psychic] work to throw off the Russians."[^12] Skeptics at the agency, Suter wrote, flew in an outside team of debunkers to brief engineers considering SRI's psychic work.[^12]

[[Kenneth A. Kress]], writing in the [[Central Intelligence Agency]] journal [[Studies in Intelligence]] in the winter of 1977, recalled that DARPA reported "not only a showing of interest but a hostile response." He described a briefing at which "the SRI contractors and I" faced "an assemblage of hostile DARPA people who had been convened especially to debunk our results," after which contacts with DARPA stopped for several years and the Navy funded a replication.[^13]

### The DARPA computer network and remote viewing experiments, 1975 to 1979

A 1977 SRI report by Puthoff, Targ and [[Edwin May]], titled "Advanced Threat Technique Assessment," has a section on long-distance remote viewing that lists "Use of the DARPA teleconferencing computer network to provide date and time records of experiments in progress." The experiments ran between New York and California, New York and Dayton, and New Orleans and California.[^14] A 1977 proceedings volume of the [[Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers]] Systems, Man and Cybernetics Society describes the TALK mode of the DARPA net being used for post-experiment feedback between a subject at SRI and an experimenter on the East Coast.[^15] Zarbo describes a 1976 test, reported in a [[New York Times]] article that he dates to 1983, in which Targ acted as a conduit in New York City while a viewer at SRI typed impressions on a terminal linked to the DARPA network, the target being a fountain in Washington Square Park. The thesis footnote for the article lists William J. Broad, "Pentagon is said to Focus on ESP for Wartime Use," January 10, 1984, section C.[^11]

In 1979 [[Harry Snyder]] and Arlene Snyder of [[Systemetrics]], Inc., issued a critical evaluation of remote viewing research under contract 77F104420. The report states that the DARPA computer teleconferencing network coordinated experimental timing and provided time and date stamped records of all communications.[^16]

### Funding routed through DARPA, 1983

Army and Defense Intelligence Agency records released through the CIA reading room describe DARPA as the source of money for remote viewing work at SRI in 1983. A February 21, 1983 information paper of the [[United States Army Intelligence and Security Command]] (INSCOM), signed by [[Robert Jachim]] as project manager, states that a memorandum of January 19, 1983 from the Under Secretary of Defense for Research and Engineering lifted a prohibition on the use of P6 funds for the [[Grill Flame]] project. The paper reports that Dr. [[Jack Vorona]] of the Defense Intelligence Agency "has either obtained or received commitment for $200K from DARPA" to keep SRI intact for fiscal 1983.[^17] The same paper states that the Director of the GDIP Program, Mr. Hurwitz, wrote the memorandum for the Under Secretary, and lists among his objectives for Grill Flame: "Keep the SRI team intact - Use the Dr DeLaurer Memo to get funds to SRI."[^17]

A February 28, 1983 INSCOM information paper repeats the $200,000 figure and describes it as money DIA had determined was needed to keep DIA intact for fiscal 1983.[^18] An undated Army memorandum (DAMI-ISH), concerning a funding initiative for the INSCOM unit called [[Center Lane]], states that "Contact with Defense Advanced Research Projects Agency (DARPA), the actual source of the funds, resulted in a decision to move the money covertly through DCSRDA, if approved." It refers to the end of fiscal 1983 and records that the Congressional prohibition against use of [[National Foreign Intelligence Program]] (NFIP) or NFIP-derived funds "has been discussed with DARPA, but will be reiterated."[^19]

A Defense Intelligence Agency project report dated October 19, 1983 totals the three-year Grill Flame project at $1,475.5K for fiscal years 1981 to 1983. It divides that sum into $962K for DIA, $313.5K for the Army, and $200K for DARPA. Its largest task line is remote viewing enhancement training at $575K.[^20] A later DIA special access program file on [[Sun Streak]] describes the R/D link through [[SRI International]] as "accomplished via HQ SGRD funding and a DARPA MIPR."[^21]

### Congressional funding through DARPA, 1986 program

Edwin May and Wanda Luke wrote in a research plan dated May 10, 1991 that beginning in fiscal year 1986 the [[United States Army Medical Research and Development Command]] began a five-year investigation of anomalous mental phenomena at SRI International. They wrote that the Command did not fund the fourth and fifth years, and that the House Appropriations Committee funded the fourth year, at the level of the third, through DARPA to the Command's contract.[^22]

### Project Pandora and the Moscow Signal

A memorandum dated September 27, 1967 reports on Project BIZARRE and its relation to Project PANDORA. It is signed by [[Richard S. Cesaro]] as acting director of Advanced Sensors at ARPA. It states that "The central nervous system of primates has been penetrated directly or indirectly by non-thermal modulated microwave radiation," and that the frequency and modulation used "simulated a portion of the 'Moscow Signal.'" The memorandum adds: "The potential of exerting a degree of control on human behavior by low level selectively modulated microwave radiation should be investigated for potential weapon applications."[^23]

In the September 15, 1977 letter to Senator Magnuson, the agency stated that Pandora was classified at the direction of the Department of State and the United States Intelligence Board, that its total cost was $4,615,000, and that DARPA terminated the project in March 1970 and transferred its assets to the [[Walter Reed Army Institute of Research]].[^3] The letter states that "classification of Project Pandora was a foreign relation rather than a national security matter," and that DARPA did not foresee the development of microwave weapons directed at altering nervous system function or behavior.[^3]

[[Annie Jacobsen]] reports that in 1969 the program was expanded to include human subjects, under the code names Big Boy and Project Bizarre, with microwave beams directed at sailors at the [[Philadelphia Naval Yard]].[^24]

### Information Awareness Office and Total Information Awareness, 2002 to 2003

The conference report on the fiscal 2004 defense appropriations act, House Report 108-283, was taken up by the House on September 24, 2003. Section 8131 provides that none of the funds may be obligated "for the Terrorism Information Awareness Program," with a proviso excepting "processing, analysis, and collaboration tools for counterterrorism foreign intelligence" funded in the National Foreign Intelligence Program, and defines that program as "the program known either as Terrorism Information Awareness or Total Information Awareness, or any successor program, funded by the Defense Advanced Research Projects Agency, or any other Department or element of the Federal Government."[^25] The joint explanatory statement records that the conferees "agree with the Senate position which eliminates funding for the Terrorism Information Awareness (TIA) program within the Defense Advanced Research Projects Agency (DARPA)," and "direct that the Office be terminated immediately," meaning the [[Information Awareness Office]].[^25]

The statement names four projects previously under the office that may continue under DARPA: Bio-Event Advanced Leading Indicator Recognition Technology, Rapid Analytic Wargaming, Wargaming the Asymmetric Environment, and Automated Speech and Text Exploitation in Multiple Languages (including Babylon and Symphony).[^25] The conference agreement states that it "does not restrict the National Foreign Intelligence Program from using processing, analysis and collaboration tools for counterterrorism foreign intelligence purposes."[^25] DARPA created the Information Awareness Office in mid-January 2002, and [[John Poindexter]] directed it. On August 12, 2003 he submitted a five-page letter of resignation to Tether, effective August 29.[^26]

### ARPANET

Leiner and coauthors state that [[Lawrence G. Roberts]] went to DARPA in late 1966 to develop the computer network concept and published a plan for the ARPANET in 1967. [[Bolt, Beranek and Newman]] developed the switches, called IMPs, under Frank Heart's leadership, with [[Robert Kahn]] responsible for overall system design. The first switch was installed at UCLA in September 1969. The authors credit Kahn with introducing open-architecture networking in late 1972, shortly after he arrived at DARPA.[^2]

### Neural interface programs

In January 2016 DARPA announced the Neural Engineering System Design (NESD) program, managed by [[Phillip Alvelda]]. The release sets a size goal of about one cubic centimeter for an implantable device that would link neural activity with digital systems, and gives a ceiling of $65 million over four years.[^27] The release lists devices that could compensate for deficits in sight or hearing by feeding digital auditory or visual information to the brain.[^27]

### Contractors

Federal award records list 113 DARPA prime contract awards to SRI International with action dates from October 1, 2007 to September 30, 2026, whose Award Amount fields total $462,329,429.73 and whose transactions inside that date range total $423,420,369. The largest record is contract HR001106C0023, with action dates from November 2005 to March 2011 and an award amount of $41,671,826.77.[^28] Other records describe supply chain hardware security, machine translation under the BOLT program, semantic forensics, and molecular discovery.[^28]
