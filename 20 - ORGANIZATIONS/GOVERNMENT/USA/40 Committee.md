The 40 Committee was the subcommittee of the [[National Security Council]] that reviewed and approved covert operations of the [[Central Intelligence Agency|CIA]] during the administration of [[Richard Nixon]]. It succeeded the 5412 Panel of the 1950s, the [[Special Group]], and the [[303 Committee]], and was chaired by the president's national security adviser, [[Henry A. Kissinger|Henry Kissinger]].[^1]

### Chile

The 303 Committee approved the campaign against [[Salvador Allende]] in the Chilean election of 1964 on April 2 of that year. National Security Decision Memorandum 40, signed February 17, 1970, converted the 303 Committee into the 40 Committee, updating NSC 5412/2. The Foreign Relations annotation on the memorandum reads "thus updating NSC 5412/2."[^2] On October 18, 1969 Director of Central Intelligence [[Richard Helms]] sent Kissinger a memorandum on the annual review of 303 Committee programs, which the Council's covert-action directive had put under committee review.[^3] In 1970 the 40 Committee approved 125,000 dollars for a "spoiling operation" against Allende's coalition and then 300,000 dollars more for propaganda. After Allende's plurality on September 4 it met on September 8, where Kissinger asked for a "cold blooded" assessment of the prospects of a military coup, and approved 250,000 dollars for Ambassador [[Edward Korry]] to use on the Chilean congress. It also approved 60,000 dollars for Korry to buy a radio station, which was never spent.[^1][^4]

On September 15 Nixon ordered the agency to organize a coup. The Senate's report states: "the 40 Committee was never informed of this direct CIA role." The operation, [[Project FUBELT]], reported to Kissinger or his deputy, and the agency's instruction of September 16 was that it never be discussed at a committee meeting. Korry, who told the Senate "I even attended a 40 Committee meeting," was not told either.[^5]

The committee authorized 700,000 dollars for the newspaper [[El Mercurio]] on September 9, 1971, and 965,000 dollars more on April 11, 1972. In September 1972 it gave 24,000 dollars to a "powerful businessmen's organization" and declined to fund others "because of their possible involvement in anti-government strikes." That October it approved 100,000 dollars for three private groups within a 1.5 million dollar program for the opposition. On the truckers' strikes the Senate staff wrote: "The 40 Committee did not approve any such support. However, the U.S. passed money to private sector groups which supported the strikers. And in at least one case, a small amount of CIA money was passed to the strikers by a private sector organization, contrary to CIA ground rules." On August 20, 1973, it approved a further million dollars. None of it had reached the private groups when the armed forces took power on September 11.[^1]
