The Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) is the [[United States]] agency that regulates civil aviation, operates the national airspace system and keeps the public record of U.S.-registered aircraft.[^1] It was created as the Federal Aviation Agency in 1958 and took its present name on April 1, 1967, when it became one of the modal administrations of the [[Department of Transportation]].[^2][^3]

### Creation and leadership
The Federal Aviation Act of 1958 established the agency in its first section: "There is hereby established the Federal Aviation Agency."[^1] The act was signed on August 23, 1958. [[Elwood R. Quesada]], a retired [[U.S. Air Force]] general, became the first administrator on November 1, 1958, and the agency began operations on December 31 of that year.[^3] The Department of Transportation Act of October 15, 1966 created the department and placed a Federal Aviation Administration within it.[^2] The department began full operations on April 1, 1967, and the agency took the new name.[^3] The FAA's biography series lists eighteen administrators, from Quesada through [[Stephen M. Dickson]], who took office on August 12, 2019.[^4]

### Aircraft registry
The N-Number Inquiry on the FAA's registry site reports, for a registration mark, whether the mark is assigned, deregistered or reserved, the registered owner name and mailing address, the certificate issue date, the make, model and serial number, and the Mode S code. The page states that its data is "Updated Each Federal Working Day At Midnight."[^5] Reserved marks carry a reservation date and renewal and purge dates. The mark N3FD, which appears in the [[Epstein Files]] and on the [[St. Thomas Jet Center]] page, was listed on October 14, 2026 as reserved, with a reservation date of July 21, 2022, a renewal date of May 25, 2026 and a purge date of August 21, 2027.[^6]

Two marks connected to [[Barry Seal]] and his [[Southern Air Transport]] operations, N4410F and N5575A, are listed as deregistered.[^7] The registry also lists N1437J, a Cessna 210A that the record associates with the family of [[R.C. Horsch]], as deregistered.[^8]

Active marks appear in the same inquiry. N114BD is assigned, with [[Black Diamond Aviation Group LLC]] as registered owner.[^9] N615BD is registered to [[Stephen Deckoff]] as an individual.[^10] N908JE, the mark of the Boeing 727 known as the [[Lolita Express]], is assigned to [[Jet Assets Inc.]][^11]

Section 803 of the FAA Reauthorization Act of 2024 added 49 U.S.C. 44114, which requires the FAA, on request of a private aircraft owner, to withhold the registration number and personally identifiable information from broad public display, with exceptions for data furnished to government agencies, including for traffic management.[^12] The FAA opened the request process on March 28, 2025 through its Civil Aviation Registry Electronic Services system, and said it was evaluating whether to withhold the personally identifiable information of private owners from the public registry by default.[^13] A request for comment followed in the [[Federal Register]] on April 3, 2025.[^14] The N615BD record displayed the registered owner's name in the public inquiry on October 14, 2026.[^10]

### Japan Air Lines flight 1628
On November 17, 1986 a [[Japan Air Lines]] Boeing 747 cargo flight, flight 1628, flew from Reykjavik to [[Anchorage]] under Captain Kenju Terauchi. FAA special agents interviewed the crew at Anchorage that evening. The crew described lights in two sets, yellow, amber and green and none red, first ahead of the aircraft and then on its left; an object on the aircraft's weather radar at about seven to eight nautical miles; a 360 degree turn near [[Fairbanks]] during which the lights stayed with the aircraft; and loss of the lights about 40 miles north of Talkeetna. A record by the FAA Alaskan Region Flight Standards manager states that the duty officer of the [[North American Aerospace Defense Command]] told him that military radar also showed two targets, one of them the JAL aircraft.[^19]

The FAA's Alaskan Region recorded the sequence of its public response: a correspondent of Kyodo News visited on December 24, 1986 and the Alaska bureau chief of United Press International on December 29; the agency re-interviewed the crew on January 4, 1987; and on January 5 the regional director approved a plan to withhold further release until all material had been collected and to release it at a press conference on March 5, 1987 at 10:00 a.m.[^20] United Press International reported on January 1, 1987 that Terauchi said the largest object was "two times bigger than an aircraft carrier" and that the sighting appeared on FAA and Air Force radar and on the cockpit radar.[^21] At the March 5 press conference the Alaskan Region public affairs officer, [[Paul Steucke]], handed out a statement that read in part: "We have not tried to determine what the crew of Japan Airlines flight 1628 saw based on scientific analysis of the stars, planets, magnetic fields, angle of view, etc." and "The FAA has completed its investigation of JAL flight 1628, and does not intend to pursue it any further."[^22] In a letter of April 1, 1987 to [[Philip J. Klass]] he wrote that the FAA was "not concerned with UFO's per se" and that its primary concern was the safety of the airspace.[^22] Steucke said the radar blip on FAA screens had been analyzed as a split image of the JAL aircraft; Sam Rich, an FAA controller on duty, said in a news report that none of the controllers thought so at the time.[^20] In a letter of May 14, 1987 the FAA's Alaska Region told a correspondent that the report, titled "JAL Flight 1628 Unidentified Traffic Sighting, 11/17/86," was available from the [[National Technical Information Service]] as accession PB87-184206 at $30.95 for a paper copy.[^23]

[[John Callahan]] was an FAA official in Washington, D.C. (office symbol AAT-63). In memoranda of March 10 and 11, 1987 Steucke recorded that Bruce Maccabee of the Naval Surface Weapons Center told him on February 20 that he and several other people had been invited by Callahan, in late January or early February, to see the Alaskan material, which Maccabee described as including the transcript, color photographs of the radar screen, the crew interview and controller statements, and that Callahan told him about a C-130 and Alaska Airlines sighting of January 29, 1987. Steucke attributed to Callahan the release of material that had reached Klass, and wrote that Callahan "probably" received it from one of two FAA employees to whom the Alaskan Region Air Traffic Division had sent it.[^24] Callahan later described the Washington briefing as including personnel of the [[Central Intelligence Agency]] and science advisers of the Reagan administration, and said that at its end a CIA representative told the attendees that the event never happened and that they were sworn to secrecy. Skeptoid reported that Ron Pandolfi and Maccabee, both of whom attended, said that no one was sworn to secrecy.[^25] Callahan is credited in the acknowledgments of the 2007 [[National Aviation Reporting Center on Anomalous Phenomena]] (NARCAP) report on the 2006 O'Hare sighting as "John Callahan, FAA, ret."[^26]

### The 2006 O'Hare sighting
On November 7, 2006 witnesses at [[Chicago O'Hare International Airport]] reported an object hovering above Gate C17. The [[Chicago Tribune]] reported on January 1, 2007 that the FAA had at first told the newspaper it had no information, and reversed after the Tribune filed a Freedom of Information Act request; an internal FAA review of air traffic communications tapes found a call from a United Airlines supervisor to an FAA manager in the tower.[^27] The FAA spokeswoman [[Elizabeth Isham Cory]] was quoted as saying that none of the tower controllers saw the object, that "a preliminary check of radar found nothing out of the ordinary," and: "Our theory on this is that it was a weather phenomenon. That night was a perfect atmospheric condition in terms of low (cloud) ceiling and a lot of airport lights. When the lights shine up into the clouds, sometimes you can see funny things." She said the FAA was not investigating.[^26][^27] NARCAP, which examined the case as its Case 18, reported that its examination of the primary radar data supplied by the FAA found that nothing was detected by radar at that location and time.[^26]

### UAP reporting rules
The FAA's controller order, JO 7110.65, tells controllers to "Inform the operations supervisor/CIC of any reported or observed unidentified anomalous phenomena (UAP)/unexplained phenomena activity."[^28] The companion order, JO 7210.3, states that pilot reports and air traffic observations of UAP "must be reported to the National Tactical Security Operations (NTSO) Air Traffic Security Coordinator (ATSC) team on the Domestic Events Network (DEN)." It refers other people who want to report UAP to the website of the [[All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office]].[^29]

The Aeronautical Information Manual's section 7-6-4 on UFO reports, in the web edition containing changes through August 27, 2009, told the public to contact "an UFO/Unexplained Phenomena Reporting Data Collection Center, such as the [[National Institute for Discovery Science|National Institute for Discovery Sciences (NIDS)]], the National UFO Reporting Center, etc." and, if concern was expressed that life or property might be endangered, to report the activity to the local law enforcement department.[^30] Section 7-6 of the current manual on the FAA site does not carry that paragraph.[^31]

Section 1673 of the National Defense Authorization Act for Fiscal Year 2023 directed the Secretary of Defense to establish a "secure mechanism for authorized reporting" of events relating to unidentified anomalous phenomena.[^32] The 2025 workshop paper of AARO states that "the FAA continues to collect pilot reports."[^33] A 2024 workshop on UAP data organized with [[Associated Universities, Inc.]] proposed designing its reporting schema so that "reports can be linked to FAA/NASA Aviation Safety Reporting System (ASRS) data," along with ADS-B flight tracks.[^34]

Luis Elizondo wrote that [[U.S. Navy]] intelligence leadership tasked [[Jay Stratton]] with building a whole-of-government task force with members from the FBI, the [[National Reconnaissance Office]], [[NASA]] and the FAA, and that Stratton became the first director of the [[UAP Task Force]].[^35]

### Drone rules and the fixed-site restriction process
The FAA's small unmanned aircraft rules, 14 CFR part 107, were adopted in a final rule published June 28, 2016 at 81 FR 42064.[^36] Remote identification of unmanned aircraft, 14 CFR part 89, was adopted in a rule published January 15, 2021 at 86 FR 4390.[^37]

Section 2209 of the FAA Extension, Safety, and Security Act of 2016 (Public Law 114-190, enacted July 15, 2016) directed the Secretary of Transportation to establish a process for petitioning the FAA Administrator to restrict unmanned aircraft operations in close proximity to a fixed site facility.[^38] Executive Order 14305, "Restoring American Airspace Sovereignty," dated June 6, 2025, was published at 90 FR 24719 on June 11, 2025.[^39]

On May 6, 2026 the FAA published a notice of proposed rulemaking at 91 FR 24650 under docket FAA-2026-4558. It proposes a new part 74 under which operators or proprietors of fixed site facilities could request an unmanned aircraft flight restriction. Eligible facilities are limited to those that meet the critical infrastructure definition in 42 U.S.C. 5195c(e), a limit the notice describes as consistent with Executive Order 14305.[^40] The comment period, originally set to close on July 6, 2026, was extended to August 5, 2026 by a notice published June 30, 2026.[^41]

On June 8, 2026 [[ELDAEON]] filed comment FAA-2026-4558-0285 in the docket, asking the FAA to recognize passive radar as an approved method of meeting the protective security measures requirement of proposed 14 CFR 74.54.[^42]

### New Jersey and the December 2024 statement
On December 17, 2024 the [[Department of Homeland Security]], the FBI, the FAA and the [[Department of Defense]] issued a joint statement on reported drone sightings in New Jersey and the northeast. The statement said the FBI "has received tips of more than 5,000 reported drone sightings in the last few weeks with approximately 100 leads generated." It attributed the sightings to a combination of lawful commercial, hobbyist and law enforcement drones, as well as "manned fixed-wing aircraft, helicopters, and stars mistakenly reported as drones," and said: "We have not identified anything anomalous and do not assess the activity to date to present a national security or public safety risk over the civilian airspace in New Jersey or other states in the northeast."[^43] The statement also referred to "a limited number of visual sightings of drones over military facilities in New Jersey."[^43]

### The September 11 attacks
The 9/11 Commission Report describes the FAA's Boston Center as the center that had tracked the first two hijackings. At 8:28 a.m. on September 11, 2001, Boston Center called the FAA's Command Center in [[Herndon, Virginia|Herndon]] to advise that it believed [[American Airlines]] Flight 11 had been hijacked. At 8:32 the Command Center passed word of a possible hijacking to the Operations Center at FAA headquarters. The report states that "FAA headquarters began to follow the hijack protocol but did not contact the NMCC to request a fighter escort."[^51] The report places the [[Northeast Air Defense Sector]] of the North American Aerospace Defense Command in [[Rome, New York]].[^51]
