The Assassination Records Review Board was an independent agency created by the President [[John F. Kennedy]] Assassination Records Collection Act of 1992, Public Law 102-526, to re-examine for release the assassination records that federal agencies still held too sensitive to open. Representative Louis Stokes and Senator David Boren introduced the legislation on March 26, 1992; President [[George H.W. Bush]] signed it on October 26, 1992, and left the appointments to [[Bill Clinton]], whose five nominees were sworn in on April 11, 1994: John R. Tunheim, chairman, Henry F. Graff, Kermit L. Hall, William L. Joyce and Anna K. Nelson. The act provided that "No assassination record shall be destroyed, altered, or mutilated in any way." Extended by Congress in 1994 and 1997, the board finished on September 30, 1998, leaving "more than four million pages of records" in the National Archives.[^1]

### Releases

Among the records released through the board were the [[Joint Chiefs of Staff]] proposals of 1962 known as [[Operation NORTHWOODS|Operation Northwoods]], made public in 1997.[^2] The files of [[Edward Wegmann]], lawyer for [[Clay Shaw]], given to the board by his daughter in 1995, showed "that the Shaw defense team had retained [[Southern Research Company]] as of mid-1967," the Shreveport firm whose [[New Orleans]] office had investigated [[David Ferrie]], and recorded [[Wackenhut Corporation|Wackenhut]] men reporting to the defense.[^3] On January 18, 1996, the board's lawyer Philip Golrick telephoned the former [[Guy Banister]] partner [[Joseph Oster]] in search of Banister's files, the Ferrie investigation and the Shaw defense.[^4] The board classed four [[Federal Bureau of Investigation|FBI]] headquarters records on the family of [[H.L. Hunt]] as assassination records.[^5]

### Records Destroyed

The board's final report recorded what it could not recover:

- The [[Central Intelligence Agency|CIA]] "destroyed most of its formal [[HTLINGUAL]] records," the files of its 1952-1973 mail-opening program, "in 1990 at the direction of CIA's Office of General Counsel."[^1]
- The CIA "destroyed tape[s] containing Oswald's voice and other related calls as a matter of routine procedure," although its [[Mexico City]] station had transcribed the calls and reported them to headquarters in an October 8, 1963, cable.[^1]
- Among the papers of Director [[John McCone]], a record of a dinner of May 19, 1964, with [[John J. McCloy]] "Re: Oswald" is annotated "Destroyed 1-28-72"; a record of a meeting with [[McGeorge Bundy]] on November 26, 1963, about the assassination is missing.[^1]
- [[Richard Helms]] directed that the notes and source material for the 1967 Inspector General's report on the plots against [[Fidel Castro]] be destroyed; no "operational diaries" of [[William Harvey]] were found.[^1]
- After [[J. Edgar Hoover]]'s death his secretary, Helen Gandy, destroyed many of his "Personal and Confidential" files.[^1]
- The Army destroyed its intelligence dossier on [[Lee Harvey Oswald]] in 1973 "as part of a program to purge domestic surveillance files."[^1]
- In 1974 the Secretary of the Joint Chiefs of Staff ordered the destruction of the minutes and transcripts of the chiefs' meetings from 1947 to 1978.[^1]
- In January 1995, with the act in force, the [[United States Secret Service|Secret Service]] destroyed protection survey reports for some of Kennedy's trips in the fall of 1963; the board learned of it about a week later.[^1]
