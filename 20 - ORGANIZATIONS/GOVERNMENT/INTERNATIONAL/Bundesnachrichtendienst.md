The Bundesnachrichtendienst (BND; Federal Intelligence Service) is the foreign intelligence service of the [[Germany|Federal Republic of Germany]]. It collects foreign intelligence, conducts signals intelligence (SIGINT) and maintains liaison with the [[Central Intelligence Agency|CIA]] and the [[NSA]]. The service descends from the [[Gehlen Organization]], the network that [[Reinhard Gehlen]] ran under [[United States Army|U.S. Army]] and then CIA sponsorship from 1946 until West German sovereignty was restored, and it was formally established on April 1, 1956.[^1]

### Origins under the Army and the CIA

The Gehlen group was first run under an American arrangement codenamed [[Operation RUSTY]]. Gehlen had headed the Wehrmacht's [[Fremde Heere Ost]] (Foreign Armies East) directorate, which covered the Soviet Union and the Eastern Front. His memoir describes microfilming the [[Wehrmacht]]'s files on the Soviet Union, burying them in the mountains and surrendering to American forces with the microfilm.[^2] A statement by his deputy [[Gerhard Wessel]] recorded in the CIA's history places the decision in spring 1944: Gehlen resolved then to salvage the directorate's files and records "for the West," and after [[Adolf Hitler]] relieved him on April 9, 1945, he moved the files, already photostated and microfilmed, by army truck to three sites in southern Germany, near the Wilder Kaiser, Marquartstein and Schliersee. Wessel succeeded him as chief of the directorate.[^3]

Between 1945 and 1949 the U.S. Army handled the organization and funded its intelligence collection, and the Army wanted the CIA and its predecessors to take over responsibility for it.[^4] Lieutenant General [[Lucius D. Clay]], the U.S. military governor, opposed CIA involvement in RUSTY, and his retirement in the spring of 1949 cleared the way for the Agency to assume control in July of that year. [[James H. Critchfield]]'s December 1948 report for Director of Central Intelligence [[Roscoe Hillenkoetter]] was decisive in persuading CIA officials to take responsibility for the Organization.[^5]

The Army's [[Counter Intelligence Corps]] arrested several Germans in 1946 who said they worked for American intelligence. The [[Strategic Services Unit]] representative who investigated the arrests reported that "some of the agents employed were [[SS]] personnel with known Nazi records and, in most cases, undesirable people."[^6] In August 1948 the acting chief of the CIA's [[Karlsruhe]] Operations Base wrote about RUSTY's recruiting methods "with great disgust." A former Wehrmacht officer named Baun had built his own network inside RUSTY, and the Americans and Gehlen removed him during 1947. In late 1947 RUSTY moved from [[Oberursel]] to its own compound at [[Pullach]], a village near [[Munich]], and Col. Willard K. Liebel replaced Col. Deane as operations officer.[^6]

The CIA assumed responsibility for the Organization from the Army in 1949 and held it in what the CIA called "trusteeship" until 1956. Critchfield was the Agency's representative at Pullach from June 1949 until 1956.[^7] The CIA's 1945 to 1949 history describes the group as a "double edged sword" that "boosted the [[Warsaw Pact]]'s propaganda efforts" and that "suffered devastating penetrations by the [[KGB]]," as shown by the [[Heinz Felfe]] and [[Hans Clemens]] cases.[^8] The [[National Security Archive]] posted the history in February 2005 in a briefing book that notes Gehlen employed numerous former Nazis and known war criminals.[^9]

The CIA acknowledged an intelligence relationship with Gehlen for the first time in an October 2000 court filing in the Freedom of Information Act case Oglesby v. Army. The [[Nazi War Crimes Disclosure Act]] processing that followed released the Army's 2,100-page Gehlen file and nearly 2,100 further pages from CIA files, together with files on many of Gehlen's personnel and agents.[^10]

### Founding in 1956 and the succession to Gehlen

Under the 1955 [[Paris Agreements]], West Germany regained sovereignty and the Organization passed to the new federal government. On April 1, 1956 it became the Bundesnachrichtendienst, with Gehlen as its first president. He held the post until 1968.[^7] The Pullach compound, the personnel roster and the operational assets went with it, and the CIA-BND liaison replaced the CIA-Organization relationship.[^1]

A CIA field report distributed on December 20, 1967 relayed an account from a "qualified American observer" of a conversation with a West German official named [[Horst Ehmke|Ehmke]]. According to the account, the [[Social Democratic Party of Germany|Social Democratic Party]] (SPD) had decided to back Lieutenant General Gerhard Wessel as Gehlen's successor and wanted the vice presidency in return. The Christian Democratic Union and Christian Social Union had proposed Ambassador Swidbert Schnippenkoetter for the presidency. The SPD candidates for vice president were [[Guenther Nollau]], vice president of the [[Bundesamt für Verfassungsschutz|BfV]], and Guenther Redding, president of the Hamburg office for the protection of the constitution.[^11] Wessel was the BND president by December 1969, when the service's telex on the security review of [[Günter Guillaume]] was sent.[^12]

The BND's Cold War work was shaped by Germany's position as the front line between NATO and the Warsaw Pact. The service ran agent networks inside [[German Democratic Republic|East Germany]], monitored [[Stasi]] and KGB activities, and served as the CIA's primary liaison for operations against the Soviet Bloc. [[Berlin]], divided between Western and Soviet occupation zones, was the central arena of these operations.[^13] After the uprisings in East Germany in the summer of 1953 the Soviets and East Germans moved against the Organization: in late November 1953 the Communists announced the roll-up of West German agent networks in the East, three cases led to the exposure of hundreds of other assets, and by the end of that year more than 200 Organization agents had fled to West Germany to avoid arrest.[^7]

### Former SS and SD personnel

The Organization's counterintelligence base at Karlsruhe, designated GV"L" in the CIA's study of the Felfe case, was heavily staffed by former [[Sicherheitsdienst|SD]] and SS personnel who had to conceal their backgrounds to keep their jobs. The base developed an informal association of former SS and SD men for mutual protection and career advancement, and that group was susceptible to blackmail and to appeals based on revenge and vindication.[^14]

The Soviets exploited the Organization's employment of former SS officers, and after the Felfe and Clemens scandals "it is believed that over 200 BND officers resigned or were dismissed in the 1960s because they had concealed their wartime pasts."[^7] The Pullach operations base played no role in vetting the Organization's staff, because Gehlen refused to give the true names of his staff and agents.[^7] The KGB exploited the wartime records of potential recruits to identify those who could be approached.[^13]

The historians of the U.S. Interagency Working Group on the Nazi War Crimes Disclosure Act described nine of fourteen second-tier CIA name files as involving individuals who had contact with the Gehlen Organization. Their case summaries record the following:[^15]

- [[Emil Augsburg]], an SD major and department director at the Wannsee Institute who had been wanted by Poland for war crimes, was used by the CIC in 1947 and 1948 and then taken on by the Organization. By 1953 the Organization viewed him as a "shining star" of counterespionage. Gehlen insisted in 1954 that he work outside Pullach because of his RSHA past, and he was dismissed in 1966 after the BND concluded in late 1965 that he could be a double agent.
- [[Franz Goering]], an aide to Walter Schellenberg in the RSHA foreign intelligence branch, was a BND official in 1959 when a former colleague who was a Soviet agent took intelligence records from his home in Hamburg and left a note inviting him to defect.
- [[Wilhelm Harster]], an SS lieutenant general and Security Police commander in the Netherlands and Italy, whose file notes implication in the murder of 104,000 Jews, could not join the BND as a security risk but presented himself to others as a BND agent and recommended former SS contacts to the service. He had been Heinz Felfe's superior, and the file indicates he used Felfe to reach the BND.
- [[Wilhelm Krichbaum]], former head of the Wehrmacht Secret Field Police and a district chief in the Organization, did not tell the Organization or the CIA of his contacts with two Viennese brothers-in-law who ran a literary agency in the Soviet sector of Vienna that the file describes as an espionage front. A 1963 document apparently from the BND concluded he had worked for the Soviets since 1950.
- [[Franz Six]], an Einsatzkommando officer sentenced at Nuremberg, was reported to have joined the Organization in the mid-1950s. In 1963 the East German media charged that Gehlen had recently transferred a number of former SS and SD officials, among them Six, Augsburg and Goering, to camouflaged positions to reduce criticism that he was using war criminals.
- [[Friedrich Panzinger]], a former Gestapo colonel, was released from Soviet prison in 1956 with a secret mission to penetrate the BND, reported the mission to the West German authorities and was used by them as a double agent. He killed himself in a cell in 1959 before a possible Bavarian war-crimes arrest, and West German intelligence reviews afterwards could not determine whether he had ever been loyal to the West.
- [[Hans Sommer]], a Gestapo official in Norway and France, was recruited for counterespionage in early 1950 and dropped in 1953. [[Wilfried Krallert]] was picked up in 1952. There are indications that [[Horst Kopkow]] surfaced as an Organization official. [[Guido Zimmer]] contacted Gehlen in December 1948.

The same historians concluded that in settling on men such as Augsburg the CIC, the CIA and the Gehlen Organization made the West more vulnerable to Soviet espionage.[^15]

The BND's own Independent Historians' Commission (Unabhängige Historikerkommission, UHK) was established by contract in February 2011. A parliamentary question of May 2022 describes its research focus as the BND's handling of the Nazi past, both its own personnel "Altlasten" and the early Federal Republic's handling of the subject, and states that fourteen studies had been published by then.[^16] The commission's first volume, by Christoph Rass, drew on a random sample of 3,650 life histories from the personnel files of the just under 12,000 staff who worked for the BND at Pullach until 1968 or for the Organization until 1956, and counted formal "markers" of National Socialist ties (NSDAP and other organizations, and service in the Reich's police, Waffen-SS and RSHA).[^17] The historian [[Dominik Rigoll]] summarized the first four volumes in 2016: Nowack's study of security reviews in the 1960s recorded the removal of 68 persons, who were treated as security risks because they could be blackmailed, and Sälter's volume described the Organization's revival of the Nazi image of the "Red Orchestra" resistance as a fifth column.[^17] In October 2022 a report attributed to Sälter's volume on Nazi continuities stated that up to 20 percent of the personnel in the Organization's early years "demonstrably had blood on their hands," that more than 30 of them had taken part in mass shootings with Einsatzgruppen, and that the recruitment of accomplices in the Holocaust and in war crimes had been a system until the 1960s.[^18]

The most recent study cited in the 2022 parliamentary question is [[Klaus-Dietmar Henke]], "Geheime Dienste. Die politische Inlandsspionage des BND in der Ära Adenauer," which the question describes as covering BND surveillance of the SPD carried out on the instruction of [[Konrad Adenauer]].[^16] A volume by Agilolf Keßelring on the Organization and the defense of West Germany from 1949 to 1953 documents, in a reviewer's summary, a secret emergency plan to gather veterans of the old Wehrmacht elite divisions, and of the Waffen-SS, behind the Rhine in case of a Soviet attack. The Wehrmacht colonel Albert Schnez had begun preparing such an army cadre in 1949 without the knowledge of the federal government; connections between the Schnez organization and Gehlen existed from the summer of 1951, and Gehlen evidently made the project a matter for his personal attention. About 3,000 men were recorded on index cards and lists, and the plan provided for a force of 40,000 in three divisions.[^19]

### The BND and Alois Brunner

Members of [[Die Linke]] asked in May 2022 whether the redactions in the Henke volume concealed BND knowledge of the whereabouts of [[Alois Brunner]], whom the question described as a 1938 staff member of the Vienna office for Jewish emigration and deputy to [[Adolf Eichmann]]. The same question asked whether the BND had a "nachrichtendienstliche Verbindung" to the Eichmann trial in Jerusalem, citing [[Willi Winkler]], who wrote in the [[Süddeutsche Zeitung]] on April 20, 2022 that a lawyer may have been that link and that a journalist may have been an informant placed near the trial.[^16] In June 2022 the government answered the Brunner question with "Ja, dies kann ausgeschlossen werden" ("Yes, this can be ruled out") and referred back to earlier answers on the trial.[^20]

CIA files released under the Disclosure Act and described by the historians Richard Breitman and Norman Goda record several BND contacts around Brunner's circle in the Middle East. In 1959 a high BND official codenamed Winterstein conceded to the CIA that the BND had a loose relationship with [[Wilhelm Beisner]], a former SD officer who was in Cairo trading arms, and most of the CIA's information on his Egyptian activities originated with the Gehlen Organization. Mail to Beisner's Munich apartment was intercepted by an intelligence agency and copied to the CIA; the BND said it was a French operation and the CIA thought the BND itself might have done it. From a handwritten reply in that mail the CIA concluded, by comparing handwriting, that a correspondent in Damascus signing as Rischer was Brunner, then an adviser to Syrian intelligence. In March 1961 a BND official codenamed Glueckrath told a CIA officer in Munich that Brunner had attended meetings of an "Egyptian SS group" in late 1960 and January 1961 with [[Fritz Katzmann]] and the Nazi propagandist [[Johannes von Leers]], where Brunner claimed to hold a list of Jews who had collaborated, to be used for blackmail. The former German Foreign Office deportation specialist [[Franz Rademacher]], who had fled to Syria, was put on the payroll of the West German secret service in 1961 or early 1962; the CIA knew of his BND status and had no direct contact with him. Brunner survived an assassination attempt and, in the historians' account, remained in Syria and apparently died there in 1992.[^21]

### Klaus Barbie, Merex and the arms trade

In 1966 the BND recruited [[Klaus Barbie]], the former Gestapo chief of Lyon then living in La Paz as Klaus Altmann, as a source codenamed Adler.[^22] The historian [[Peter Hammerschmidt]] wrote in 2012 that the Amt used Barbie between May and December 1966, shielded him from prosecution and paid him 5,300 marks in total; he reported on left-wing forces in Bolivia, and the BND ended the connection after he declined to travel to Germany for training.[^23][^24] A few weeks after the recruitment Barbie became the Bolivian representative of the arms firm Merex.[^25]

Merex was run by [[Gerhard Mertins]], a former SS man who in Hammerschmidt's account had worked for the BND under the codename Uranus since 1956. The firm sold surplus [[Bundeswehr]] weapons and equipment abroad on the BND's behalf, above all in the Middle East. Hammerschmidt reported in June 2011, from files of German and American intelligence services, that a Merex representative acting as a double agent of the BND and CIA toured Latin America in July 1966, and that during the tour the former Luftwaffe pilot [[Hans-Ulrich Rudel]] introduced him to Friedrich Schwend, a former RSHA counterfeiter of British banknotes, and to the former SS officer Willem Sassen, who became a representative in Argentina two days after the first contact with the Merex representative; that Schwend, Barbie and Rudel had founded a firm named La Estrella in the early 1960s that employed former SS officers; that [[Otto Skorzeny]] was drawn into the Merex business; and that Merex and Barbie handled the delivery of four Transall C-160 aircraft from the West German defense ministry to Bolivia and Peru in March 1968. The CIA files, in Hammerschmidt's description, record that the BND was "very interested" in the representative's contacts with Rudel and other Germans in Latin America, which he called a clear sign that the Barbie recruitment was "only the tip of the iceberg" of BND collaboration with Nazi criminals.[^25] Marti wrote in 2025 that Barbie, as Merex's Bolivian representative from 1966, also arranged the delivery of Austrian tanks used in the 1980 coup, and that the Germans had made no effort to inquire about his identity.[^24]

The Federal Chancellery gave an official account of the BND's arms-trade contacts in February 1979 in a reply to the Higher Regional Court of Cologne in a civil suit involving [[Karl Carstens]]. The Chancellery stated that in the 1960s the BND maintained connections to arms trading firms, among them Merex AG and its dependent companies Deutsche Merex GmbH and Wehrexport GmbH, and a firm dependent on Dobbertin & Co. The purpose was stated as reconnaissance of the international arms trade. The BND went beyond observation: to move the firms to provide information it advised them on initiating and conducting deals, named them to the defense ministry as buyers for surplus Bundeswehr material and occasionally acted as intermediary by passing sales requests from the ministry or from foreign interested parties to the firms. From 1965 to September 1969 the Merex companies exported arms to various states on a larger scale, in numerous cases having obtained the required export approvals by naming incorrect recipient countries or, in some cases, by use of forged end-user certificates. A criminal case against Mertins and others ended in acquittal on November 26, 1975. The Chancellery stated that the BND had evidently known of the transactions in all essential phases, and that a senior BND official was released from service for five years from May 1, 1969 to steer the reconnaissance activity and took the post of deputy chairman of the management at the Dobbertin company. The same file records that Wessel told a dpa bureau chief on December 15, 1978 that the BND had never itself traded in arms and that its task was to find out where weapons were flowing.[^26]

The CIA's records also show the BND's relations with the Ukrainian nationalist leader [[Stepan Bandera]]. After a brief sponsorship by Italian military intelligence in early 1956, Bandera offered the BND his lines into Ukraine in March 1956 in return for money and weapons. The CIA warned the West Germans against any operational relationship, saying all alleged Bandera assets in Czechoslovakia, Poland and Ukraine were non-existent or ineffective. In April 1959 Gehlen's adviser Heinz Danko Herre, Bandera's contact in the BND, supported a mission, and a team trained and funded by the BND crossed from Czechoslovakia in late July.[^27]

### Heinz Felfe

Heinz Felfe was born in [[Dresden]] in 1918. According to the CIA study he was inducted into an SS reserve unit in 1938, and from then on his schooling, legal training and police career were guided by the SS; the historian Goda gives 1936 for his entry into the SS and 1943 for his commission.[^14][^28] In 1943 he joined the foreign intelligence section of the [[Reich Main Security Office]] (RSHA), serving first in its Swiss section and then in the Netherlands. He finished the war as an Obersturmführer in the [[Waffen-SS]] and was taken prisoner by the British.[^14] The British held him at an interrogation center near Utrecht that specialized in former German intelligence personnel. After his release he worked from 1947 for a British military intelligence unit in Bonn, assigned to report on communist student groups, and joined the [[Communist Party of Germany|Communist Party]] (KPD).[^14]

The British decided in April 1950 to drop Felfe, after he had tried to sell information to several agencies and two news services and had revealed himself as a British agent to his own contacts. Their files, received by the BND in 1961 and by the CIA in 1962, recorded that they told him his contacts with former SS personnel, named as [[Helmut Proebsting]] and Hans Clemens, could no longer be tolerated.[^14] Clemens was a former colleague from the RSHA foreign intelligence arm. The KGB recruited Clemens first, in Dresden in February 1950, and then, through Clemens, Felfe and a third Dresdener, [[Erwin Tiebel]].[^14] Goda dates Felfe's full status as a Soviet agent, codenamed Paul, to September 1951.[^28] After leaving the British Felfe worked for the North Rhine-Westphalia state security office and then as a refugee interrogator at the Ministry for All-German Affairs, and he was recruited into the Gehlen Organization in 1951.[^14] The CIA study records that British files showed he had told his case officer in early 1950 that he intended to offer Clemens to the North Rhine-Westphalia state security office.[^14]

Felfe was a BND officer for ten years, six of them as deputy chief of the section responsible for countering Soviet espionage, and the CIA study describes the post as in practice the real chief of the section. He was a dedicated Soviet agent throughout.[^14] Goda states that he took charge of counterintelligence against the Soviets in 1955, and Hechelhammer, the BND's chief historian, describes him as having risen to head of counterintelligence against the Soviet Union.[^28][^29] The Army's Counter Intelligence Corps had noted in 1953 his role as leader of an "SD clique" in the Organization, and in 1955 that suspicion of him and the clique was growing; the CIA suspected him by 1957, while he had Gehlen's confidence and personally briefed him on Soviet matters.[^28] The CIA's record shows that the first official briefings on the agency's Karlshorst penetration program were given to him in May 1958, February 1959 and July 1959, and that he became the BND headquarters supervisor of the joint Karlshorst program.[^14] Felfe was detected through a lead from a CIA-run penetration of the Polish intelligence service (UB), identified in the study as the defector Michal Goleniewski, and he was arrested on November 6, 1961, with Clemens and Tiebel.[^14][^30] Felfe is credited with the compromise of approximately one hundred Western agents operating in the Soviet Bloc, many of whom were arrested or killed.[^13] The CIA's 1963 damage assessment, as summarized by Goda, estimated that about 15,000 individual items were blown or compromised.[^28]

Felfe was tried in July 1963 and sentenced to fourteen years. Clemens received nine years and Tiebel two years at hard labor.[^14] From prison Felfe kept up contact with the KGB, and the Soviets tried to obtain his release in exchange for prisoners held in the East.[^14] In 1969 he was released to East Germany in exchange for three West German spies, and a BND archive photograph shows the exchange at the Herleshausen border crossing.[^28][^29] Hechelhammer states that he later worked for the Stasi as a full-time informal collaborator.[^29] He published a memoir, *Im Dienst des Gegners* (1986).[^31]

### The Günter Guillaume affair

The BND's most politically significant Cold War case involved not its own personnel but a Stasi agent operating inside the West German government. Günter Guillaume was an East German citizen who had emigrated to [[West Germany]] in 1956, established himself as a Social Democratic Party (SPD) activist and risen to become a personal aide to Chancellor [[Willy Brandt]]. Guillaume was an officer of the [[Hauptverwaltung Aufklärung|HVA]], the Stasi's foreign intelligence directorate headed by [[Markus Wolf]].[^32]

The BND had a card entry dating from April 1954 that recorded that Guillaume was reported to have travelled the Federal Republic for the East Berlin publisher [[Volk und Wissen]], to make contacts with publishers, printers and persons and then infiltrate them from the east. The Bundestag's Second Inquiry Committee reproduced a BND telex received on December 17, 1969 that reported the entry and noted that its truth could no longer be checked.[^33] The BND also held information that Volk und Wissen served as a legal residency for [[Ministry for State Security]] members.[^33]

BND suspicions about Guillaume developed from the late 1960s, but the sensitivity of disclosing that a spy had reached the Chancellor's office complicated the investigation. Guillaume was arrested on April 24, 1974, and confessed within days. Brandt resigned as Chancellor on May 7, 1974, partly because of the security failure. The affair was among the most damaging East German intelligence operations of the Cold War.[^32]

Markus Wolf wrote that the HVA penetrated the BND, the Federal Chancellery, the Foreign Ministry and both major West German parties, and that the full scope became clear only after reunification.[^34]

### Reunification and the Stasi files

The backup copy of the microfilmed index cards and statistical sheets of the Stasi's foreign intelligence directorate (Hauptverwaltung A) carries the name Rosenholz, assigned by the Federal Office for the Protection of the Constitution. The Stasi Records Agency received the data sets concerning German nationals from the CIA by March 2003; data on foreigners were not handed over. The 280,000 person records cover the whole period of the directorate's activity to January 1988, with 54 percent concerning citizens of the Federal Republic, 38 percent citizens of the GDR and 8 percent unassigned. The copies were handed to the German authorities between 1999 and 2003 on 381 CDs.[^35] The files took years to analyze and generated prosecutions, diplomatic incidents and revelations about the extent of Eastern Bloc penetration of Western institutions.

### Curveball and the Iraq assessments

The CIA's own memorandum on its contact with the BND's source codenamed [[Curveball]] states that the BND provided the vast majority of Curveball's reporting to the United States government, and that U.S. intelligence agencies had no direct access to him until mid-March 2004, when the BND allowed CIA officers to debrief him. The memorandum judges him unreliable because he fabricated his claimed access to a mobile biological weapons production project.[^36]

The [[Robb-Silberman Commission]] described Curveball as an Iraqi chemical engineer who came to the Intelligence Community's attention through a "foreign liaison service." The commission wrote that the service debriefed him and shared the results with the United States but would not give American officers direct access. Between January 2000 and September 2001 the [[Defense Intelligence Agency]]'s Defense HUMINT Service disseminated almost 100 of his reports on mobile biological weapons facilities. The commission wrote that "virtually all" of the Intelligence Community's information on those facilities came from a source "who was a fabricator."[^37] The CIA memorandum names the BND as the service.[^36]

### Crypto AG and Operation Rubicon

The Swiss parliamentary Control Delegation (Geschäftsprüfungsdelegation, GPDel) reviewed an American history of the operation, titled MINERVA, which describes how American intelligence services used the Swiss manufacturer [[Crypto AG]] for their own purposes in agreement with its Swedish owner. According to the GPDel's summary of that history, in 1970 the company passed into joint ownership of American services and the German intelligence service. German participation ended in late 1993, and the account closes in 1995. The GPDel also found that the Swiss [[Strategic Intelligence Service]] had reliable information from autumn 1993 that American and German services owned the firm, and that the Swiss service set out to break the encryption of the firm's weaker export devices.[^38]

The Washington Post reported in February 2020 that the company had been owned and run by the CIA in partnership with the BND for most of its history. In the Post's audio report the reporter [[Greg Miller]] said "By 1970, the CIA and the BND own this company," after buying it from its founder.[^39] The Post's coverage of the operation names Thesaurus and Rubicon.[^39]

### Bad Aibling and the NSA

The BND's [[Bad Aibling Station]] was built on a U.S. military site. According to the Bundestag's NSA inquiry, the American Army operated the station from 1952 to 2004, collecting satellite traffic from crisis regions. Former BND president [[August Hanning]] testified that the station was not run by the NSA and described it as a relic of the occupation period.[^40] Closure planned for 2002 was postponed after the attacks of September 11, 2001, and the NSA collected satellite traffic from the station until 2004. In April 2004 the U.S. Army gave up the station, and the [[Joint SIGINT Activity]] took over its essential technical facilities, including the antenna field. Hanning testified that the arrangement placed Bad Aibling under German sovereignty, with American access to its results provided through the BND.[^40]

### Operation Eikonal

Operation [[Eikonal]] was a BND collection point on a fibre-optic cable near Frankfurt am Main, run with the NSA in the Joint SIGINT Activity from 2004 to 2008. Its internal codename was GRANAT. Witnesses said it rested on a Memorandum of Agreement of 2002. The former BND department head [[Reinhardt Breitfelder]] testified that cooperation at Bad Aibling had been extended from satellite collection to a cable project for foreign-to-foreign traffic, with the results to be shared.[^41] A witness also said that cable collection in Frankfurt was discussed at a BND leadership conference in March 2002, and that Breitfelder sketched the plan in a paper.[^41]

The Süddeutsche Zeitung reported in June 2014 that the BND had collected data at a Frankfurt data node for years and passed raw data to the NSA, that the arrangement was halted in 2007 over its political sensitivity, and that the NSA had protested. The committee's report cites that reporting.[^41]

### NSA selectors

In late 2005 or early 2006 BND staff at Bad Aibling found NSA selectors (telephone numbers and other communication identifiers) targeting the European companies [[EADS]] and [[Eurocopter]], with numbers located outside Germany. On January 25, 2006 the station confirmed that the selectors were no longer used for collection, and the BND stopped using 74 numbers, 52 for EADS and 22 for Eurocopter.[^42] Former BND president [[Gerhard Schindler]] described the finding as the start of a third filter stage, for selectors that violated "deutsche Interessen."[^42]

According to the committee's report, in August 2013 a special review of the NSA selector stock found a large number of unlawful selectors concerning European government bodies. The Bad Aibling staff member who ran the search deleted about 12,000 features on the instruction of the station head. The report lists at least nine persons in the BND who knew of the review and its findings before March 2015, and states that the president and the Chancellery were reportedly informed only in March 2015.[^43] The report's section on the period is headed as the concealment of the NSA selector findings in the 2013 election year. Schindler was the BND president at the time of the 2013 review.[^43]

### Surveillance law and reform

The Bundestag's NSA inquiry followed the 2013 disclosures of [[Edward Snowden]], which the report describes as the source of public knowledge of the BND's role in mass surveillance.[^43] The 2016 reform of the BND Act, passed by the grand coalition, put strategic foreign-to-foreign surveillance into statute. The [[Federal Constitutional Court]] ruled on May 19, 2020 (1 BvR 2835/17) that this surveillance was unconstitutional as then regulated, that Article 10 of the [[Basic Law]] protects foreigners abroad, and that an independent oversight body was required. The legislature had until the end of 2021 to rewrite the rules.[^44] The federal cabinet approved a new bill on December 16, 2020.[^44]

### Later espionage cases and Berlin headquarters

In 2014 a BND employee, Markus R., was exposed as a source for the CIA; between 2008 and 2014 he passed on more than 200 documents, received at least 80,000 euros and was sentenced in March 2016 to eight years. In the case of the BND employee Carsten L. the Federal Prosecutor charged him with obtaining nine internal documents in September and October 2022 for the businessman Arthur E., who allegedly passed information to the Russian service FSB; both are charged with especially serious treason in a trial that began in December 2023.[^30] The BND was headquartered at Pullach until its new complex in Berlin was opened. Chancellor [[Angela Merkel]] opened the new headquarters on February 8, 2019, and called the move a milestone in the development of the German foreign intelligence service.[^45]

### Former president arrested in 2026

On October 6, 2026 the Federal Prosecutor (Bundesanwaltschaft) had August Hanning and his former office head arrested by officers of the [[Bundeskriminalamt]] in the district of Bad Tölz-Wolfratshausen in Bavaria and in the district of Steinfurt in North Rhine-Westphalia, on warrants issued by the investigating judge of the [[Federal Court of Justice]]. Searches of the suspects' premises began there and in Berlin the same day. The release states a strong suspicion against Hanning of unlawful espionage (landesverräterische Ausspähung, section 96 paragraph 1 of the Criminal Code), gathering state secrets (section 96 paragraph 2), attempted treason (section 94 paragraph 1 with section 22) and intelligence agent activity (section 99 paragraph 1 number 1). The office head is charged with aiding attempted treason and unlawful espionage (sections 94, 96 and 27).[^46]

The release gives the following facts as the warrants' allegation. Hanning was BND president from December 1998 to the end of November 2005, and the office head served as his office head, followed him as a seconded assistant when he was state secretary in the Federal Ministry of the Interior from December 2005 to November 2009, and from March 2012 to March 2026 served as office head for several later BND presidents. Probably in spring 2010 the two agreed that the office head would continuously obtain valuable BND findings for Hanning for payment, which Hanning could exploit commercially in his private consulting work. Until June 2022, by the state of the investigation, the office head supplied nearly 2,000 documents, many classified, intended only for internal BND use for briefing and preparing the agency's leadership, in particular for the intelligence situation meeting held at the Federal Chancellery. In one case Hanning used classified findings so supplied to prepare an analysis for an employee of a foreign intelligence security apparatus; whether the product reached the addressee could not be established. Hanning was also in intensive exchange with a member of another state intelligence service, whom he supplied over several years with political information, in part based on material from the office head.[^46]

The proceeding rests on finds from a search of Hanning's premises that the Hamburg public prosecutor carried out in September 2025 in an investigation concerning [[Christina Block]] and others; the matter was submitted to the Bundesanwaltschaft in early May 2026 for takeover. The investigation is conducted with the BND and the Bundeskriminalamt.[^46] A second release of October 7, 2026 states that both men were presented to the investigating judge on October 6 and 7 and that the arrest warrants were put into effect.[^47] Both men are presumed innocent. beck-aktuell reported the same charges and allegations on the day of the arrests, and ZDF reported that Hanning denies involvement in the Block case and that, as of the arrest day, nothing pointed to a political motive; ZDF described Hanning as well connected and as having run several security firms. The current BND president [[Martin Jäger]] stated that the BND had opened extensive internal investigations early, which hardened the suspicion.[^48][^49] In November 2005, while he was BND president, Hanning apologized to a journalist after BND staff had surveilled journalists to find leaks, a matter later examined by parliament.[^50]
