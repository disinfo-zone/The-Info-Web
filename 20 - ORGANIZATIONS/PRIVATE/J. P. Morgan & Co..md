J. P. Morgan & Co. was the New York banking partnership of the [[J. P. Morgan]] family, which underwrote, lent to and advised corporations and governments until it merged into [[Chase Manhattan Corporation]] on December 31, 2000. The name Drexel, Morgan & Co. appears in an 1886 Supreme Court caption for the New York house.[^1] In 1912 Mr. Scudder testified to the House subcommittee that the New York firm was "identical with Drexel & Co., of Philadelphia, in firm members."[^2] [[Antony C. Sutton]]'s 1983 chart of old-line and new-wealth families places [[Henry P. Davison]] among the new-wealth families beside "J. P. Morgan & Co."[^3]

### Partners and the partnership

The senior member of the firm was J. P. Morgan. In December 1912 the [[Pujo Committee|Pujo subcommittee]] asked him whether he was the senior member of the partnership, and he answered "I am, sir."[^4] The partners he named included [[Charles Steele]], who confirmed his membership before the subcommittee, and [[Thomas W. Lamont]], whom Morgan identified as a partner.[^5][^6] Davison testified on January 23, 1913 that he had been a member of the firm "Since the 1st of January, 1909."[^7] [[Walter E. Frew]], a director of [[Bankers Trust Company]] from about 1905, was asked in December 1912 whether Davison was a member of J. P. Morgan & Co. Frew answered "Of the First National Bank at that time," and then said that Davison "is now" a member of the firm.[^8]

The membership was read into the record again in 1933. Before the [[Senate Banking and Currency Committee]], [[J. P. Morgan Jr.]] gave the date on which the firm had been constituted as it then stood as March 31, 1916, and read a list of the partners of J. P. Morgan & Co. at the end of 1931. It opened "Myself, E. T. Stotesbury, Charles Steele, Thomas W. Lamont, Horatio G. Lloyd," and then named Dwight W. Morrow ("no, he retired") and, after [[Thomas Cochran]], Julius S. Morgan and [[George Whitney]], Thomas S. Gates ("no, he has retired"). The list went on with [[Russell C. Leffingwell]], Francis D. Bartow, [[Arthur M. Anderson]], [[William Ewing]], [[Harold Stanley]], H. S. Morgan, Thomas S. Lamont, H. P. Davison, Thomas Newhall, Edward Hopkinson Jr., [[S. Parker Gilbert]] and, from January 2, 1932, [[Charles D. Dickey]].[^9] The Thomas S. Gates of that list is [[Thomas S. Gates Sr.]], listed as president of the [[University of Pennsylvania]] in the 1930 to 1931 Who's Who, whose entry gives "partner Drexel & Co. since 1918, J. P. Morgan & Co. since 1921."[^10]

Keyes listed separate accounting periods marked by the deaths of partners: [[Arthur E. Newbold]] in 1920, Davison in 1922, Bacon in 1924, [[Edward R. Stettinius Sr.]] in 1925 and Porter in 1926. He also said "We had a change in 1927 by the retirement of the late Mr. Dwight W. Morrow, which occurred in September 1927."[^11]

### Directorships and the Pujo subcommittee

The Pujo subcommittee tabulated the directorships of a group of individuals in Exhibit 134-C of December 18, 1912. Its explanation of the table treated 180 individuals who were firm members or directors of eighteen financial institutions, and stated that "In the aggregate they hold 385 directorships in 41 banks and trust companies" and, "in all, 746 directorships in 134 corporations having total resources or capitalization of $25,325,000,000."[^12][^13]

The exhibit then gave the figures for J. P. Morgan & Co. separately: "J. P. Morgan & Co. of New York (identical with Drexel & Co. of Philadelphia) have 23 directorships in 13 banks and trust companies." Three of those were at the [[Guaranty Trust Company of New York]], the bank whose 1959 merger with the firm produced [[Morgan Guaranty Trust]]; the list also shows three each at the Astor Trust, Bankers Trust and the [[First National Bank of New York]], and one each at the Fourth Street National Bank, the Franklin National Bank, the [[Girard Trust Company]] and the [[Philadelphia National Bank]]. The same section lists 4 directorships in 4 insurance companies and "a controlling stock interest in another, the Equitable Life Assurance Society of New York"; 20 directorships in 12 transportation systems; 12 in 7 producing and trading corporations, among them 4 in the [[United States Steel Corporation]] and 2 each in the General Electric Company and the International Harvester Company; and 4 in 3 public utility corporations. It closes the Morgan summary: "In all, 63 directorships in 39 corporations having total resources or capitalization of $10,036,000,000."[^12]

The exhibit also records that J. P. Morgan & Co. held "2 of the 3 voting trustees of the Guaranty Trust Co. of New York and 1 of the 3 voting trustees of the Bankers Trust Co. of New York."[^12] A supplemental table in the same exhibit combined the affiliations of J. P. Morgan & Co., the First National Bank, the National City Bank, the Guaranty Trust Company and the Bankers Trust Company. It states that the five together had "118 directorships in 34 banks and trust companies," 30 in 10 insurance companies, 105 in 32 transportation systems, 63 in 24 producing and trading corporations and 25 in 12 public utility corporations: "In all, 341 directorships in 112 corporations having aggregate resources or capitalization of $22,245,000,000."[^14]

Asked about the control of credit, J. P. Morgan told the subcommittee, "I want to control nothing," and "I do not want any control."[^15]

### Early transactions, 1861 to 1907

In 1861 the House committee on government contracts reported, of the sale of Hall carbines to the government, that the arms "were placed under the control of J. Pierpont Morgan to secure him the payment of $20,000 he had advanced to Stevens."[^16]

In February 1895 the Treasury's gold contract was taken by a syndicate that the firm managed with [[August Belmont]]. The contract stipulated that the syndicate would "exert all financial influence and will make all legitimate efforts to protect the Treasury of the United States against the withdrawals of gold."[^17]

The firm was a syndicate manager for the 1901 offering that organized the United States Steel Corporation. The prospectus dated March 2, 1901, issued from the firm's office, described a $200,000,000 syndicate with "Syndicate Managers" named in the text.[^18] In the House inquiry into the steel corporation, [[Andrew Carnegie]] was asked why Morgan had "send word to me that he would like to buy me out."[^19] Treasury Secretary [[George B. Cortelyou]] testified that a conference was held at the [[Hotel Manhattan]] on October 22, 1907, and that members of the firm attended it.[^20]

### The purchasing agency, 1914 to 1917

On August 10, 1914 Secretary of State [[William Jennings Bryan]] wrote to President [[Woodrow Wilson]]: "Morgan Company of New York have asked whether there would be any objection to their making a loan to the French Government and also the Rothschilds." Bryan wrote that he had suggested the Department take the position that it would not approve of any loan to a belligerent nation, and that "Money is the worst of all contrabands because it commands everything else."[^21] On August 21, 1914 J. P. Morgan wrote to Wilson on British objections to the purchase of German ships, quoting cablegrams from "my London firm, which is at the moment in very close touch with the British Government."[^22]

On January 23, 1915 Davison returned from London and told the press that the firm had been appointed the sole commercial agent for Great Britain in the United States, so that "all purchases in this country for Great Britain would be made through J. P. Morgan & Co."[^23] By March 25, 1915 the firm wrote to the Secretary of State, jointly with the [[National City Bank]] and the First National Bank of New York, that it was arranging to place for the French Government "some $50,000,000. One-Year Treasury Notes, the proceeds of which are to be expended in this country."[^24]

On September 6, 1915 Secretary of State [[Robert Lansing]] wrote to Wilson on the need for loans to the belligerents. He gave the American export surplus as nearly a billion dollars from December 1, 1914 to June 30, 1915, wrote that if the European countries could not find means to pay "they will have to stop buying and our present export trade will shrink proportionately," and concluded that "there is only one means of avoiding this situation": "the flotation of large bond issues by the belligerent governments."[^25] On September 28, 1915 the Anglo-French financial commission announced a $500,000,000 credit at the [[Hotel Biltmore]], with Davison reading a statement for the firm.[^26]

On July 3, 1915 Frank Holt, a former instructor in German at Cornell University, shot J. P. Morgan Jr. at his home at Glen Cove, New York. The New York Times reported on July 4 that Holt had confessed to setting the bomb that wrecked the Senate reception room at the Capitol on the night of July 2, and that he described to the police how he had practised for months the making of a bomb; the paper's subheading called the explosion "a protest against shipments of war munitions."[^27]

After the war began in Europe, J. P. Morgan & Co. was the principal fiscal and purchasing agent in the United States for the British and French governments. The [[Special Committee Investigating the Munitions Industry]] (the [[Nye Committee]]) examined the arrangement in 1934 and 1935. [[Pierre S. du Pont]] testified that "a great many of the contracts were made through Morgan & Co." and that these were "for the British and French Governments." He said the firm had negotiated with the du Pont company "as agents for the British Government."[^28] The du Pont company's letter of December 18, 1934 put the total sales value of its contracts with the two governments at $490,573,806, of which "J. P. Morgan & Co. acted as agent, under sales contracts aggregating $351,259,813.28."[^28]

Senator Clark asked whether money was owing to the du Pont company "from the allied and associated nations or their fiscal and purchasing agents in this country, such as Morgan."[^29] [[Col. Hawkins|Colonel Hawkins]] testified that the British purchasing commission had worked "in connection with Mr. Stettinius," and that the commission had been organized by J. P. Morgan & Co., "who were the fiscal agents for the British in this country."[^30] Hawkins also said that [[Lt. Col. Black]], who had been connected with that commission before American entry into the war, came to Washington in January 1918 and had since returned to his engineering firm.[^30]

The Senate report on the government manufacture of munitions (1936) recorded the contracts of the [[United States Steel Products Company]]. It gave the British contracts "through J. P. Morgan & Co., of $62,514,546 from 1915 to 1917" and the French contracts as "$52,781,486."[^31]

In January 1936 the committee questioned J. P. Morgan Jr., Thomas W. Lamont and George Whitney. The United States News reported that Morgan produced a copy of his 1915 speech to the bond houses that were to sell the Anglo-French loan, in which he said: "this is not a pro-Ally affair or an anti-German affair, or anything else; it is a pro-American affair, and that only." The same report quoted Morgan before the committee: "Increased exports helped this country and I don't think they lead us into war."[^32]

### Foreign loans, the Consortium and the State Department, 1924 to 1927

The firm was the American banker for the French, Italian and Japanese governments in the 1920s, and its partners were in regular contact with the State Department on each issue. On November 11, 1924 Secretary of State [[Charles Evans Hughes]] telegraphed the Ambassador in France that the Department had "just been consulted by J. P. Morgan and Company on the proposed French $100,000,000 loan which the firm is considering bringing out shortly if the Department sees no objection to it," and that the bankers' negotiations "have been awaiting consummation of the German loan." Hughes wrote that he "should certainly oppose flotation of a French loan in the United States were there any doubt of France's support" for the American claim to participate in the Dawes Plan annuities, and authorized the Ambassador to seek French assurances, "for the bankers will await the Department's answer."[^33] The same volume identifies Lamont in an editorial note as "member of the banking firm of J. P. Morgan & Co., New York City."[^34]

The February 1924 Japanese loan was a Morgan syndicate issue. The advertisement in the Commercial and Financial Chronicle of February 16, 1924 described a $150,000,000 Imperial Japanese Government External Loan of 1924, thirty-year sinking fund 6 1/2 per cent gold bonds due February 1, 1954. It stated that the proceeds were to retire the outstanding 4 1/2 per cent sterling loans and to purchase materials for reconstruction after "the earthquake and fire of September, 1923," and that subscription books would open "at the office of J. P. Morgan & Co." on February 15, 1924. The managers' notice listed J. P. Morgan & Co., Kuhn, Loeb & Co., the Guaranty Company of New York, Harris, Forbes & Co. and Lee, Higginson & Co. among others; a second notice carried the names of the National City Company and the First National Bank. In London the issue of sterling bonds was made by a group that included Morgan Grenfell & Co.[^35]

The American Group of the China Consortium also operated from the firm. On June 12, 1924 J. P. Morgan & Co. wrote to the Secretary of State "For the American Group," enclosing material for the Consortium Council meeting in London on July 14, which Lamont, "Chairman of the American Group," was to attend, and on July 29 forwarded the minutes of that meeting.[^36]

The Italian loan of 1925 followed the same pattern. On May 26, 1925 Lamont wrote to the Secretary of State that the [[Bank of Italy]], together with the [[Bank of Naples]] and the [[Bank of Sicily]], was arranging a $50,000,000 banking credit with American correspondents, which the Italian government would guarantee. Lamont wrote that "as explained to you, Mr. Mellon and Mr. Hoover, we regard this as a banking matter and so very different from any foreign government loan issued in this market." [[Andrew W. Mellon]] was then Secretary of the Treasury and [[Herbert Hoover]] was Secretary of Commerce. The Department replied on May 29 that, while it expressed no opinion on the transaction, it could not withhold objection to a public flotation of Italian bonds unless Italy had taken steps toward settling its debt to the United States.[^37]

The $100,000,000 issue followed. On November 18, 1925 the firm wrote that it had been "carrying on discussions with Count Volpi, the Italian Minister of Finance," on a loan "to be used for stabilization purposes," and that the contract had been executed that day. The firm noted that the Italian debt settlement had been reached at Washington on November 14.[^38] The Department answered on November 23 that it offered "no objection to the flotation of this issue in the American market," and that the Department "does not pass upon the merits of foreign loans as business propositions."[^39]

Lamont's 1927 trip to Japan was the subject of State Department telegrams. On November 19, 1927 Secretary of State [[Frank B. Kellogg]] telegraphed the Ambassador in Japan that the Department had received "an informal inquiry from Mr. Thomas W. Lamont as to whether any objection would be made to loan by American bankers of $30,000,000 to the South Manchuria Railway, the loan to be guaranteed by the Japanese Government," and that to pass such a loan "would apparently involve reversing previous decisions." The Department thought the Chinese "would very likely conclude" that the American government "has lined up behind Japan in her penetration of South Manchuria." The Ambassador, [[Charles MacVeagh]], answered on November 21 that Lamont's conversations had been "almost all with men who represent important financial interests," that near the end of the visit Lamont and Jeremiah Smith "gave a clear-cut and sweeping criticism of Japanese industrial and financial methods," and that if J. P. Morgan & Company was willing to make the advance and was deterred solely by the Department's attitude, the Japanese would ascribe it to "distrust and suspicion of Japan."[^40]

On November 19, 1927 the American representative at Peking reported that General Dzau of [[Zhang Zuolin]]'s headquarters had called to learn "whether I knew the object of the recent trip to Japan by Thomas W. Lamont." The representative also relayed a report from the commander of the [[Asiatic Fleet]] that Kuomintang leaders expected such loans to strengthen Japan's position in Manchuria.[^41] On December 9, 1927 the Tokyo mission reported that "J. P. Morgan & Company has refused to make loan to South Manchuria Railway," and that Dr. [[Takuma Dan]], president of the Mitsui company, attributed the refusal to the Department's unwillingness to pass the loan in the face of Chinese protests.[^42]

The Reparation Commission, jointly with the German government, named [[Owen D. Young]] and J. P. Morgan as the American members of its 1929 committee of experts, each with the right to name an alternate, and [[Thomas Nelson Perkins]] was named as an alternate.[^43] The report of June 7, 1929 carries the signatures of Young, J. P. Morgan, Thomas N. Perkins and T. W. Lamont.[^44] On April 28, 1930 [[Louis T. McFadden]], chairman of the House Committee on Banking and Currency, told the House that the State Department's statement of May 16, 1929 had turned America's participation in the [[Bank for International Settlements]] over to a group of bankers headed and dominated by J. P. Morgan & Co.[^45]

### The Wall Street explosion of 1920

On September 16, 1920 a bomb exploded at noon at Broad and Wall Streets, beside the firm's offices at [[23 Wall Street]]. The [[New York Times]] reported the next day under the headline "Wall Street Explosion: Morgan Office Hit" that the explosion had killed thirty persons and injured probably 300 others.[^46] J. P. Morgan was in England at the time. The paper reported that he had received private reports that were reassuring, and its London dispatch named [[C. F. Whigham]], a director of the affiliated London house of [[Morgan, Grenfell & Co.]].[^46]

### The Pecora examination, 1933

The Senate Banking and Currency Committee, with [[Ferdinand Pecora]] as counsel, examined the firm's partners and the allotments of its securities in 1933. J. P. Morgan Jr. opened his testimony with a statement on private bankers. He said that a private banker, unlike an incorporated bank, "has none of these privileges" of a charter and so "has a somewhat greater freedom of action," that the private banker was one for whom "none of the aids provided by the Government for incorporated banks, such as the Federal Reserve System or the Reconstruction Finance Corporation, are at his disposal," and that in his experience of over forty years "I cannot remember any partner of the house taking a directorship except at the earnest request of the board of directors of the company in question."[^47]

Pecora later put to him that, when he read the list of corporations on whose boards the partners sat, "I tallied the number and reached the total of 167 directorships; 167 directorships held by members of your firm." Morgan answered, "If that corresponds with the list that is right. I do not know the number."[^48] The partners' directorships read into the record included Lamont's at the United States Steel Corporation and the Northern Pacific Railway, and Whitney's at General Motors, the Kennecott Copper Corporation and the Consolidated Gas Company.[^49]

Pecora then asked whether the firm had paid income tax. Morgan answered for himself: "My 'No' means that I did not pay any income tax," for 1931 and 1932. Asked whether the aggregate paid by the members of J. P. Morgan & Co. and Drexel & Co. for 1931 "was zero," Morgan answered, "I believe it to be true, but I do not know. I don't see their income-tax returns."[^50]

An exhibit marked "Committee Exhibit No. 10" listed the distribution of 1,250,000 shares of Alleghany Corporation common stock issued February 15, 1929 at $20 a share, "at a price equivalent to our cost," in the words of George Whitney. The list includes J. P. Morgan (40,000 shares), Lamont (18,000), Leffingwell (13,500), [[Charles E. Mitchell]] (10,000), [[John J. Raskob]] (2,000), [[William Gibbs McAdoo]] (500), [[John J. Pershing]] (500), [[Frank L. Polk]] (800), Owen D. Young, [[Newton D. Baker]], [[John W. Davis]] (400), [[Charles A. Lindbergh]] (500), Harold Stanley, [[Henry S. Morgan]] (2,500), C. F. Whigham and [[William H. Woodin]], and shows 50,000 shares to Drexel & Co. and 175,000 shares to "J. P. Morgan & Co. stock A/C." Whitney testified that the firm had arranged with the Guaranty Company of New York to underwrite the sale of 500,000 of the shares.[^51]

Pecora asked Whitney whether those named were "persons who also could be characterized as among the close friends of J. P. Morgan & Co." Whitney answered: "I should assume that the great majority; yes." He said of Woodin, identified in the testimony as "Secretary of the Treasury," that "I have already testified that for many years Mr. Woodin has been a personal friend of many of the partners," and said of the $20 price that "we did not desire to make any profit out of the sale."[^52] On the following day, resuming, Whitney was asked whether there was "a standing list or record in the office of J. P. Morgan & Co., or in the possession of any of its partners, which gives or shows the names of the close friends of J. P. Morgan & Co. who were invited from time to time to subscribe." He answered "No, sir."[^53]

### The Banking Act of 1933 and Morgan Stanley

The [[Banking Act of 1933]], approved June 16, 1933 as Public No. 66 of the 73d Congress, bears the short title "Banking Act of 1933" and was enacted as an act "to provide for the safer and more effective use of the assets of banks, to regulate interbank control, to prevent the undue diversion of funds into speculative operations."[^54] In the 1953 antitrust opinion of Judge Harold R. Medina, the Act is described as requiring commercial banks and their security affiliates to go out of the investment banking business if the banks wished to continue taking deposits, with a deadline of June 16, 1934.[^55]

According to that opinion, J. P. Morgan & Co. "elected to cease engaging in the investment banking business, and chose to remain a bank of deposit," and discontinued its investment banking activities on or about June 16, 1933. The opinion finds that the first discussions within the firm about a new investment banking organization took place in June and July 1935, that the final decision to organize [[Morgan Stanley]] & Co. Incorporated was made around the middle or the 20th of August 1935, and that "There was some thought of using the Drexel name, but difficulty arose with the heirs of the Drexel Estate." The corporation was organized on September 6, 1935 under New York law and commenced business on September 16, 1935. Of the then seventeen partners of J. P. Morgan & Co., Harold Stanley, Henry S. Morgan and William Ewing resigned to form it, together with John M. Young, head of the firm's bond department, Allen Northey Jones, head of its statistical department, and Perry E. Hall and Edward H. York Jr., who held the authority of partners in Drexel & Co. Stanley was president, Ewing executive vice president and Henry S. Morgan treasurer and secretary. On September 18, 1935 the corporation issued $7,000,000 of non-voting preferred stock, of which $6,600,000 "was purchased by nine partners of J. P. Morgan & Co." The opinion states that Morgan Stanley "set up an entirely new office at No. 2 Wall Street."[^56]

### Partners in government

[[Dwight W. Morrow]] was a lawyer with Simpson, Thacher & Bartlett until 1914 and a member of J. P. Morgan & Co. from 1914 to September 30, 1927, when he resigned to become Ambassador to Mexico. He was awarded the Distinguished Service Medal in 1919 by General Pershing for services in connection with military shipping and the Military Board of Allied Supply, and was adviser to the Allied Maritime Transport Council in 1918.[^57] The Office of the Historian records his appointment as Ambassador to Mexico on September 21, 1927, his presentation of credentials on October 29, 1927 and his departure on September 17, 1930.[^58] The Biographical Directory of the American Congress lists him among the Senators of New Jersey, with the entry for his successor reading "Appointed to fill vacancy caused by death of Dwight W. Morrow" and giving his death as October 5, 1931.[^59]

Russell C. Leffingwell was a special assistant to the Secretary of the Treasury in the flotation of the Liberty Loan in May 1917 and Assistant Secretary of the Treasury from October 30, 1917 until 1920; he was a member of Cravath, Henderson, Leffingwell & de Gersdorff from October 1920 to July 1923 and a member of J. P. Morgan & Co. from July 1923.[^60] Seymour Parker Gilbert, known as S. Parker Gilbert, was Assistant Secretary of the Treasury in charge of fiscal affairs from June 1920 and Under Secretary from July 1, 1921 to November 17, 1923, and Agent General for Reparation Payments from 1924. Pecora put to Keyes that January 2, 1931 "was advisedly fixed as the date of the admission of Mr. Gilbert into the firm," and Keyes answered "Yes, sir"; the 1931 list of partners names him.[^61]

George Whitney, who gave the firm's testimony on the Alleghany allotments, was a partner of J. P. Morgan & Co. from 1920 after beginning with Kidder, Peabody & Co. in Boston in 1907, and a director of the Guaranty Trust Company, General Motors and Kennecott Copper among other companies.[^62] Lamont had been a member of the firm since 1911 and a director of the Guaranty Trust Company and United States Steel, and was chairman of the American group of the International Consortium for Assistance of China and chairman of the International Committee of Bankers for the adjustment of the Mexican foreign debt.[^63]

Edward R. Stettinius Sr. is the partner whose death in 1925 Keyes recorded, and Colonel Hawkins's remark about the purchasing commission names "Mr. Stettinius."[^64] His son, [[Edward R. Stettinius Jr.]], was Director of Lend-Lease and Special Assistant to the President from 1941, Under Secretary of State from October 4, 1943 and Secretary of State from December 1, 1944 to June 27, 1945, according to the Office of the Historian, and the first American Ambassador to the United Nations.[^65] The Historical Dictionary of the New Deal describes Stettinius Sr. as "a business partner of J. P. Morgan."[^66]

His son, [[Thomas S. Gates Jr.]], born in 1906, began in investment banking with Drexel & Co., where he became a full partner in 1940. He served as Under Secretary of the Navy from 1953, Secretary of the Navy from 1957, Deputy Secretary of Defense and from 1959 Secretary of Defense until January 1961, and then returned to investment banking at the Morgan Guaranty Trust Company; President [[Gerald Ford]] nominated him in 1976 as Chief of the United States Liaison Office in Beijing.[^67]

[[F. Trubee Davison]], the son of Henry P. Davison, became a partner in 1929 and a vice president and director of the firm in 1940.[^68]

[[R. Gordon Wasson]], who wrote a study of the 1861 Hall carbine transaction in which J. P. Morgan lent [[Simon Stevens]] $20,000 on the arms, is described by [[Annie Jacobsen]] as a vice president of the firm.[^69][^70]

### Morgan Guaranty and the 2000 merger

The 1959 merger of J. P. Morgan & Co. Incorporated and Guaranty Trust Company of New York formed Morgan Guaranty Trust Company of New York, which the firm held entirely. The Commercial and Financial Chronicle of April 30, 1959 announced that the bank "began operation April 27, 1959 as the banking institution formed by the merger of J. P. Morgan & Co. Incorporated and Guaranty Trust Company of New York," with [[Henry C. Alexander]] as chairman of the board, Thomas S. Lamont as vice chairman and Dale E. Sharp as president.[^71] The International Year Book listed the bank as the result of a merger on 24 April 1959 in its 1974, 1975 and 1983 editions.[^72]

A Senate staff study of interlocking directorates (January 1978) recorded that J. P. Morgan held 100 percent control of the bank and described it as the parent of the nation's largest institutional investor.[^73]

On September 13, 2000 Chase and J. P. Morgan announced that they had entered into an Agreement and Plan of Merger dated September 12, 2000, under which "each outstanding share of common stock of J.P. Morgan" would be "converted into 3.7 shares of common stock of Chase," with reciprocal stock options for 19.9 percent of each company.[^74] On December 31, 2000 the firm "merged with and into The Chase Manhattan Corporation," which was renamed J.P. Morgan Chase & Co. A merger charge of $1.25 billion was recorded that day, and the bank was succeeded by [[JPMorgan Chase]] Bank.[^75]
