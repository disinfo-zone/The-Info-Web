The [[Music Corporation of America|MCA]] was a talent agency founded in [[Chicago]] in 1924 that grew into a major film and television producer. It was sold to the Japanese electronics maker [[Matsushita Electric Industrial]] in 1990 and, in 1995, 80 percent of it was sold to [[Seagram Company|Seagram]]. It was the defendant in a 1946 antitrust jury trial in [[Los Angeles]] (*Finley v. Music Corporation of America*), in a 1962 civil antitrust action by the [[Department of Justice|Justice Department]] that ended in a consent judgment, and in a 1987 to 1989 [[Federal Bureau of Investigation|FBI]] wiretap inquiry into alleged organized crime penetration. Its ties were part of the broader network that [[Danny Casolaro]] termed "[[The Octopus]]."[^1]

### Founding in Chicago

[[Jules Stein]], a Chicago ophthalmologist who worked as an assistant to the eye surgeon [[Harry Gradle]], started the Music Corporation of America in 1924 with a college friend, [[William R. Goodheart]]. Moldea wrote that Stein gave up his position with Gradle to found the agency, which worked from a tiny two-room office in downtown Chicago and booked dance bands into nightclubs, ballrooms and hotels for a ten percent commission, drawing its principal business from the nightclubs and speakeasies of the city's South Side.[^2] MCA signed [[Guy Lombardo]] and his orchestra in 1928, and by the mid-1930s represented more than half of the major bands in the country.[^2]

Moldea wrote that [[James Petrillo]], president of the Chicago local and later national president of the [[American Federation of Musicians]], kept other agencies from obtaining booking licenses, that the union's executive board sided with MCA in every dispute between a band and the agency, and that the union gave MCA a "blanket waiver" allowing it to act as booking agent and radio production company at once. Moldea cited Justice Department documents as alleging that union officials, club owners and bandleaders who cooperated with Stein received cash, cars and sometimes MCA stock options, and that Petrillo was never indicted.[^3]

Moldea wrote that accounts of Stein's dealings with the Chicago underworld conflict. In one version, recounted from Justice Department documents, Stein's bootlegging sales for the nightclubs he booked crossed into the territory of the bootlegger Roger Touhy, an enemy of [[Al Capone]], and Touhy threatened Stein or his wife. Stein said later that he stood up to Touhy and took out a $75,000 kidnapping insurance policy.[^4]

Moldea wrote that [[Lew Wasserman]] was groomed as Stein's heir and was named president of the company in 1946, when Stein became chairman of the board. Moldea dated Stein's step back to April 26, 1946, Stein's fiftieth birthday, and quoted Stein as saying of his thirty-three-year-old protege, "Lew was the student who surpassed the teacher."[^5]

### Lew Wasserman

Lew Wasserman was the Chairman of MCA Corporation. He was a powerful figure in Hollywood and a long-time political supporter of [[Ronald Reagan]]. Cheri Seymour wrote that Wasserman represented Reagan during the actor's early days in Hollywood, and that Wasserman and Stein negotiated the sale of Reagan's [[California]] properties at inflated prices, which helped make Reagan a millionaire.[^1]

Wasserman was a major contributor to and sat on the board of the Reagan presidential library, along with [[Edwin Meese]] and others. He was involved in raising $80 million to build it.[^1]

Wasserman was on the MCA board of directors during the FBI investigation into alleged mob ties to MCA. [[Edward Sciandra]] reportedly wanted to go to Wasserman and [[Sidney Sheinberg]] (President of MCA) and tell them who they were up against.[^1]

Reagan and [[Nancy Reagan]] were known to celebrate New Year's Eve with Wasserman and his wife, Edie. [[Howard Baker]] returned to MCA's board of directors in March 1989, just in time to help Wasserman celebrate his seventy-sixth birthday.[^1]

Wasserman's employment agreement with MCA Inc. is dated December 6, 1988, and was amended on November 26, 1990.[^6]

### Finley v. Music Corporation of America, 1946

[[Larry Finley]], who had leased the [[Mission Beach]] amusement park and dance hall from the city of [[San Diego]] in November 1944, sued MCA, Stein, [[Larry Barnett]] and the MCA agent [[Harold Eames Bishop]] on March 20, 1945. Moldea wrote that Finley claimed MCA had charged him far more for bands than it charged other ballrooms and had conspired to freeze him out of San Diego, and that he sued for triple damages under the Sherman Act in a $3.1 million suit.[^7] The case was docketed as Civil No. 4328 in the U.S. District Court for the Southern District of California, and the trial before Judge [[Paul J. McCormick]] began in Los Angeles on January 29, 1946.[^8][^9]

After about ten days of trial the jury returned a general verdict for the plaintiffs, with treble damages of $55,500. Moldea wrote that the jury deliberated for only a few hours, that McCormick had earlier dismissed the charges against Stein, and that in his final statement to the court McCormick called MCA "the Octopus ... with tentacles reaching out to all phases and grasping everything in show business." Moldea wrote that the image became MCA's nickname in the Hollywood trade and the press.[^8] A four-part series by [[David G. Wittels]] titled "Star-Spangled Octopus" ran in the *[[Saturday Evening Post]]* beginning August 10, 1946.[^10]

In the memorandum reported at 66 F. Supp. 569, dated June 24, 1946, McCormick held that the evidence did not fix the damages with the certainty the Supreme Court's decisions required, found the jury's estimate of $18,500 in actual damages legally unsupported, and assessed costs and attorney's fees of $9,092.85 against the defendants.[^9] Moldea wrote that the court's ruling on damages quickly put a damper on the Justice Department's MCA investigation in Washington.[^11]

### The 1952 blanket waiver under Reagan's SAG presidency

Moldea wrote that Reagan, president of the [[Screen Actors Guild]] for five consecutive one-year terms, engineered a "blanket waiver" in 1952 exempting MCA from Guild rules that barred a talent agency from also engaging in film production. Reagan's second wife, actress Nancy Davis, sat on the SAG board at the time. MCA was the only such firm granted that status, which gave it the ground floor in television production and placed it in a position to offer jobs to the actors it represented. Other talent agencies complained that the arrangement gave MCA an unfair advantage.[^12]

Moldea wrote that in July 1952 there was a series of meetings between certain members of the SAG board and MCA, including Wasserman, [[Taft Schreiber]], the MCA attorney [[Laurence Beilenson]] and another MCA representative, and that no record of the meetings was kept. A Justice Department document quoted by Moldea stated that "Wasserman and Schreiber could sell SAG anything" because of their relationship with Reagan. The deal allowed MCA to work in television production through its talent agency, MCA Artists, and a new production company, [[Revue Productions]], headed by Schreiber.[^13] The Antitrust Division later reported, in Moldea's quotation, that "The MCA-SAG arrangements, in effect, have placed MCA in a highly favorable competitive position over other talent agencies and television producers," and Moldea wrote that the Justice Department received information that "The central fact of MCA's whole rise to power was undoubtedly the blanket waiver."[^14]

Moldea wrote that, according to Justice Department documents, several government sources believed the favorable treatment Reagan later received from MCA, after his film career declined, was a payoff for services rendered while he led the Guild.[^12] Moldea wrote that MCA arranged for Reagan to appear as master of ceremonies at the Last Frontier hotel in [[Las Vegas]] from February 14 to 28, 1954, where [[Johnny Roselli]] was on the payroll as a "public relations consultant."[^15] In a June 7, 1961 interview recorded by the Antitrust Division attorney [[Leonard Posner]], an industry source called Reagan "a complete slave of MCA who would do their bidding on anything."[^16]

Moldea wrote that in 1959 the SAG membership re-elected Reagan president for a sixth term to lead a strike against the studios, that MCA and a few smaller studios made an early separate peace with the Guild, and that, according to the president of the International Alliance of Theatrical Stage Employees, the 1960 settlement with the major studios came with the help of [[Sidney Korshak]].[^12]

### Grand jury and Reagan's testimony

Moldea wrote that after the election of [[John F. Kennedy]] and the appointment of [[Robert F. Kennedy]] as attorney general, the government began a concentrated probe of MCA's business affairs and empaneled a federal grand jury to hear charges including restraint of trade, conspiracy with the Guild to monopolize talent and film program production, extortion, discrimination, blacklisting and predatory business practices.[^12] The [[Federal Communications Commission]] subpoenaed Schreiber on March 8, 1961, and he declined to testify except in secret session. On August 25, 1961 the attorney general authorized a grand jury in the Southern District of New York; the Antitrust Division then moved it to Los Angeles, and by mid-November 1961 it was calling witnesses, among them [[Cary Grant]] and [[Danny Kaye]].[^17]

On February 5, 1962, Reagan testified before the federal grand jury in Los Angeles, questioned by the Antitrust Division attorney [[John Fricano]]. The transcript, taken down by the shorthand reporter Lucille Girlow, was first published in full in Moldea's book. Asked his profession, Reagan answered, "Actor, I think." He testified that MCA had represented him since about 1939 or 1940, when it bought the William Meiklejohn agency and took six of its clients, among them [[Jane Wyman]] and [[Bill Demarest]], as its first motion picture clients; that his first MCA agent was Wasserman and then [[Art Park]]; that he had been president of the Guild about five and a half years from 1946 or 1947 and again for a final year; and that his wife Nancy Davis, whom he married in March 1952, had sat on the Guild board in 1952.[^18]

Fricano asked Reagan why the Guild had granted MCA a blanket or unlimited waiver. Reagan answered that "I personally never saw any particular harm in it," that the Guild believed it could "pull the rug out from under them" if harm followed, and that he did not recall participating in the July 1952 negotiations or the June 1954 extension. Fricano showed him Grand Jury Exhibit 41, a letter of June 7, 1954 from the Guild's counsel Beilenson to Wasserman stating that the July 23, 1952 letter agreement "was executed under a specific set of circumstances where Revue was willing to sign a contract giving the guild members reuse fees when no one else was willing to do so." Reagan answered, "I don't recall it, no." He said he had told the Guild's executive secretary that he felt self-conscious about his long association with MCA and "sometimes I kind of ran for cover and was very happy to duck a committee duty in these matters." Of the many meetings in his career he said, "my memory is like a kaleidoscope of meetings."[^18]

Reagan also testified about *[[General Electric Theater]]*: that Schreiber of Revue approached him, that he was employed by the advertising agency [[BBD&O]] rather than by Revue at a salary he put at $120,000 a year, and that in the spring of 1959 Wasserman, with Schreiber present, set terms giving him a 25 percent ownership interest in the films. He said Wasserman ruled in his favor in a dispute between Schreiber and his agent Park over foreign motion picture rights.[^18]

Moldea wrote that Reagan "displayed a remarkable loss of memory while on the witness stand."[^12] In *Where's the Rest of Me?* (1965), written with [[Richard Hubler]], Reagan described the day as "a long, unhappy afternoon being interrogated by a federal lawyer who'd seen too many Perry Masons."[^19] On February 13, 1962 the Antitrust Division subpoenaed the [[Internal Revenue Service]] for the income tax returns of Ronald and Nancy Reagan for 1952 through 1955, and for the returns of the Guild's executive secretary [[John Dales]], his assistant [[Chet Migden]] and four members of the 1952 board. Moldea wrote that no charges were filed against anyone, and that on February 15 Posner learned from a source that MCA "has a means of obtaining information from the grand jury itself," a leak he could not trace.[^19]

A *[[Washington Post]]* article of March 15, 1987 reprinted by Moldea states that the grand jury never charged Reagan with any crime, and Moldea states that Los Angeles journalist [[David Robb]] first uncovered and published portions of the transcript.[^20]

Moldea wrote that Reagan later acknowledged that the government's action against MCA affected his political beliefs, inclining him toward a more conservative stance, and that MCA's Jules Stein and Taft Schreiber were among the guiding forces in shaping that philosophy.[^12]

### The 1962 Justice Department action

The Antitrust Division filed a civil complaint against MCA Inc. on July 13, 1962 in the U.S. District Court for the Southern District of California, Central Division, docketed as Civil No. 62-942-WM, under Sections 1 and 2 of the Sherman Act and Section 7 of the Clayton Act. The government was represented by Leonard R. Posner and [[Malcolm D. MacArthur]] and MCA by [[Allen E. Susman]] and [[Albert C. Bickford]] of the firm of Beilenson, Meyer, Rosenfeld and Susman.[^21] Moldea wrote that the complaint was approved by Robert Kennedy, named the Guild and the [[Writers Guild of America West|Writers Guild of America, West]] as co-conspirators for their waivers to MCA, and was filed with a request for a ten-day temporary restraining order against MCA's planned sale of its talent agency to its own employees.[^22]

MCA had acquired 80 percent of the stock of [[Decca Records]], the parent of [[Universal Pictures]], on June 18, 1962, a purchase that included Universal's library of 250 post-1948 feature films and about 2,500 hours of filmed television programs.[^23] Moldea wrote that on July 15 Wasserman filed an affidavit stating "I deny that MCA has been guilty of any violations of law or any other alleged wrongful acts charged," that Judge [[William C. Mathes]] granted the restraining order on July 16, and that Posner told the court the government wanted "to dissolve this mammoth corporation and turn these actors free to whomever they want."[^24]

On July 23, 1962 the parties filed a stipulation and order under which MCA dissolved its talent agency without selling it, surrendering its guild and union franchises. The stipulation stated that nothing in it was an admission of any violation of law. Posner said the action "destroys the power base MCA Inc. used for leverage for its filmed television production," and *[[Daily Variety]]* headlined its report "MCA DISSOLVES ENTIRE AGENCY."[^25]

The Final Judgment was entered October 18, 1962. It enjoined MCA from engaging in the talent agency business; barred it for seven years from acquiring or merging with any major television, motion picture or phonograph record production or distribution company without the Justice Department's written consent or a court finding, naming five television, six motion picture and six record companies as the "major" companies; barred tying the license of television programs, feature films or phonograph records to one another; and set a procedure, running to October 1, 1967, under which Universal could license 215 of 229 listed feature films for television to distributors other than MCA. The defendant denied any violation, and the judgment was entered on consent before any testimony was taken.[^21] Moldea wrote that the settlement took the case off the public record and spared Stein, Wasserman, Schreiber, Reagan and others from answering questions in open court, and that the Guild franchise for MCA expired at 12:01 a.m. on July 19, leaving 1,400 clients free.[^26]

The Antitrust Division's public listing of legacy judgments includes *U.S. v. MCA, Inc.*, case open date July 13, 1962, final judgment October 18, 1962, with a public comment period on its possible termination that closed April 19, 2019.[^27] A 1952 consent judgment against Decca Records, *United States v. Decca Records, Inc.* (S.D.N.Y., Civil No. 46-779), was the subject of a Justice Department motion to terminate in December 2020 stating that Decca Records, Inc. merged into MCA Inc. in 1966 and that MCA Inc. dissolved in 2015.[^28]

### Universal and Decca

Moldea wrote that after MCA bought Universal Pictures it made plans to produce motion pictures as well as television programs, that in the midst of the grand jury's investigation MCA purchased Universal and its parent company Decca Records, that the government went to court to block the takeover, and that the consent decree forced MCA to choose between being a talent agency and a production company. He wrote that production yielded nearly ten times the money of the agency and that MCA dissolved its talent agency.[^12] Moldea also wrote that Decca Records bought Universal Pictures in 1952, the same year as the MCA-SAG arrangement.[^29]

Moldea wrote that on September 19, 1962, the day after Attorney General Kennedy announced the proposed consent decree, workers began razing the Revue-Universal studio for the $110 million Universal City, which included a fifteen-story black-glass administration building.[^29]

### Reagan's Malibu Canyon land

Moldea wrote that Reagan bought 290 acres in Malibu Canyon in 1951 for $85,000, and that on December 13, 1966, a little over a month after his election as governor of California, he sold 236 of those acres to [[Twentieth Century-Fox]] for $1,930,000, or $8,178 an acre, in an arrangement handled by Stein and Schreiber with legal work by Reagan's attorney [[William French Smith]]. Moldea wrote that Fox's own appraisers had put the land at about $4,000 an acre.[^30]

### Sidney Korshak

Moldea wrote that federal investigators described Sidney Korshak, a [[Beverly Hills]] attorney, as the principal link between the legitimate business world and organized crime.[^31] He wrote that after [[Willie Bioff]], a front man for the Chicago Mafia in Hollywood, testified, Chicago's new liaison in the motion picture industry became Korshak, who had represented Bioff. Moldea quoted [[Charles Gioe]], a Chicago Mafia figure, as telling Bioff that Korshak was "our man."[^31] Moldea also wrote that Korshak became a close friend of Stein and Wasserman.[^31]

[[Seymour Hersh]], with [[Jeff Gerth]], published a four-part series on Korshak in *[[The New York Times]]* from June 27 to 30, 1976. Moldea quoted the Times as stating that "Mr. Korshak is the most important link between organized crime and legitimate business," and quoted Wasserman in that series as calling Korshak a "very good personal friend" and saying of the underworld allegations, "I don't believe them." Moldea wrote that three days after the series Korshak was among about three hundred guests at the Wassermans' fortieth wedding anniversary party.[^32]

The Bureau's file on Korshak, file 92-5053, is held on archive.org in three parts.[^33] A Bureau memorandum in that file dated July 2, 1976 quotes the Hersh and Gerth article, headlined "Korshak Again the Target of a Federal Investigation," as reporting that officials said the FBI "was accumulating evidence on Korshak's alleged involvement in a labor racketeering scheme in southern California." The memorandum states that the two reporters had interviewed over 300 people, including members of the Los Angeles and San Francisco Justice Department strike forces, while making inquiries into labor problems in the Long Beach area.[^33]

### Organized crime allegations

MCA was the subject of an FBI wiretap investigation which, according to [[Cheri Seymour]], was supervised by the federal prosecutor [[Marvin Rudnick]] and later by [[Richard Stavin]] of the Justice Department's Los Angeles Organized Crime Strike Force. These investigations, which also involved FBI agent [[Thomas Gates]], led them to believe that MCA had been penetrated by organized crime. Specifically, the wiretaps recorded communications between [[Robert Booth Nichols]] and [[Eugene Giaquinto]], then president of MCA home entertainment division. Giaquinto was also a board member of Nichols' holding company, [[Meridian International Logistics|MIL]], which was under FBI investigation as a source of funding for narcotics and the allocation of drug trafficking proceeds.[^1]

John Connolly reported in *[[Spy (magazine)|Spy]]* in January 1993 that Giaquinto left MCA and its board when reports of the investigation surfaced.[^34]

The intercepted conversations reportedly revealed connections to [[La Cosa Nostra]], including members of the [[Gambino Crime Family]] and [[Bufalino Crime Family]]. The wiretap application itself stated that the purpose of the interceptions was to determine the "source, type and quantity of narcotics/controlled substances, methods and means of delivery, and the source of funding for purchasing of narcotics/controlled substances." It was also apparent from the intercepted conversations that Eugene Giaquinto had a special relationship with [[John Gotti]].[^1]

The continuation affidavit of Gates, sworn in 1987, names Giaquinto and [[Joseph Garofalo]] as persons committing offenses "involving the sale and possession of controlled substances," and lists among the matters the intercepts were to establish "The source of funding for the purchasing of narcotics/controlled substances and the allocation and use of the proceeds from its trafficking."[^35] It records a July 20, 1987 call in which Giaquinto told Nichols that MCA stock had fallen four points, and a July 23 call in which Giaquinto told Nichols that [[ZZZZ Best]] was a money-laundering operation for heroin money and asked what to do if Nichols's name came up.[^35] An excerpt of the affidavit reproduced in Meridian's Ninth Circuit brief describes agents investigating whether others used extortionate practices "to ensure that Eugene Giaquinto retains his position as the president of Home Entertainment for MCA/Universal, Inc."[^36]

Seymour reproduced reports she attributed to John Cohen, an investigator for the [[House Committee on the Judiciary|House Judiciary Committee]], in which Gates is recorded as saying that the wiretaps showed a close working relationship between the MCA board and high-level Justice Department officials.[^1]

### Pisello and the strike force

[[Sal Pisello|Salvatore Pisello]] was convicted in federal district court on two counts of tax evasion for 1984 and 1985, acquitted on a third count for 1983, and sentenced to four years' imprisonment on one count and three years' probation on another; the Ninth Circuit affirmed on June 8, 1989. The court recited that in June 1984 [[MCA Records]] gave Pisello $30,000 to investigate a "Latin record label," that in January 1985 it paid him another $50,000, and that in 1984 [[Sugar Hill Records]] paid him about $156,109 in connection with his efforts to have MCA distribute its records and purchase its catalog of master recordings.[^37]

Moldea reported in June 1988 that Pisello attended the March 1984 convention of the [[National Association of Recording Merchandisers]] at the [[Diplomat Hotel]] in [[Hollywood, Florida]], as a representative of MCA Records. He wrote that in December 1987 the strike force's chief, [[David Margolis]], and his deputy, [[Michael DeFeo]], told Rudnick that he could pursue a tax case against Pisello, who had earned about $600,000 from his dealings with MCA, but could not call several MCA executives as witnesses.[^38] Moldea reported that on October 2, 1987 MCA filed a formal complaint against Rudnick with the Justice Department, retained [[William Hundley]] to present its case in Washington, and received a letter in mid-October from [[John Newcomer]], the head of the Los Angeles strike force, stating that "Neither MCA nor any of its executives or employees are targets of that case and its attendant investigation."[^38]

### Leveraged buyout and Matsushita

At the time of the wiretap investigations, MCA was being offered in a leveraged buyout to Japanese interests. An FBI investigation could have jeopardized this sale. In Seymour's account the wiretaps were then sealed by the Justice Department, and no case against MCA or its executives followed. The Los Angeles Organized Crime Strike Force was disbanded, Richard Stavin resigned, and Thomas Gates closed the FBI investigation. The Japanese ultimately purchased MCA in what was reported as the largest corporate sale in U.S. history at the time.[^1]

Marvin Rudnick, who lost his job for refusing to drop the investigation, later had part of his story told in the 1993 book *Stiffed: A True Story of MCA, The Music Business, and the Mafia* by [[William Knoedelseder]].[^39]

*[[Broadcasting (magazine)|Broadcasting]]* reported on October 1, 1990 that MCA had issued a statement on September 25 that it was "in discussions with a major international company regarding a possible negotiated acquisition of MCA," that Matsushita acknowledged it was talking with MCA, that MCA's stock had risen more than $25, or 66 percent, in two days, and that [[Michael Ovitz]] of [[Creative Artists Agency]] was helping represent Matsushita. The same article stated that "MCA continues to be subject to allegations of ties with organized crime," and that Wasserman, then 77, was assumed by many to be the sole arbiter of a sale.[^40] The October 22 issue reported that, with MCA-Matsushita and MGM-Pathe, four of the eight major Hollywood studios would be foreign-owned.[^41]

Matsushita closed its purchase on December 29, 1990, acquiring about 77.7 million MCA shares at $66 each for about 97 percent of the company, in a transaction reported at $6.6 billion. A federal judge in Los Angeles, [[Manuel Real]], had declined the day before to block the sale in a shareholder suit contending that Wasserman was to receive a new preferred Matsushita stock that would save him $100 million in capital gains and estate taxes.[^42]

On June 5, 1995 Seagram completed the purchase of an 80 percent interest in MCA Holding I Corp., the indirect parent of MCA Inc., from Matsushita for $5.7 billion, with Matsushita retaining 20 percent. Seagram's September 1996 annual report lists [[Frank Biondi]], president and chief executive of [[Viacom]] until January 1996, as chairman and chief executive officer of MCA Inc.[^43]

### Yosemite concession

Seymour wrote that the park concessioner [[Curry Company]] was owned by MCA. A park ranger, [[Paul Berkowitz]], testified before the House Interior Subcommittee on National Parks and Recreation about drug distribution by Curry Company officials.[^1] Moldea wrote that at the end of 1972 MCA added the Yosemite Park and Curry Company, which held the food, lodging and transportation concessions at [[Yosemite National Park]], to its Recreation Services Division. The National Park Service's finding aid for the park's concession records lists the changes in control of the company as Shasta Natural Resources in 1970, MCA in 1973 and Matsushita in 1991.[^44][^45]

On January 8, 1991 the [[National Park Foundation]] and MCA entered an agreement in principle under which the Foundation, as a pass-through, would buy 100 percent of the stock of the Yosemite Park and Curry Company from MCA. The House Subcommittee on National Parks and Public Lands held an oversight hearing on the transaction on January 9, 1991 and the Senate Subcommittee on Public Lands, National Parks and Forests held one on February 21, 1991; a final purchase agreement was approved on September 20, 1991, and the [[General Accounting Office]] reported on the sale on September 10, 1992. The National Park Service planned a sale on September 29, 1993 to the concessioner it selected, at an effective price of $61.5 million, which the new concessioner could finance through MCA at 8.5 percent interest over fifteen years.[^46] At a joint House oversight hearing on March 24, 1993, an Interior Department attorney, [[Lars Hanslin]], testified that the National Park Service recommended [[Delaware North]] for a fifteen-year contract and that the price of $49.5 million plus interest, a total of $61.5 million, had been negotiated by the Interior Department with Matsushita and the present owner. The committee chairman, [[George Miller]], said that MCA at one point had put the figure at $100 million, and Hanslin answered, "I think that is true." A National Park Service witness described a note held by MCA under which MCA reserved the right to select a successor concessioner if the contract was terminated.[^47]

### Connection to "The Octopus"

Danny Casolaro's investigation into "The Octopus" included MCA, and he was reportedly told by Robert Booth Nichols about the leveraged buyout.[^1] The nickname predates his investigation. Moldea wrote that it originated in McCormick's 1946 statement in the Finley case, and his bibliography lists a 1961 article by Nat Hentoff, "The Octopus of Show Business," in *The Reporter* and a February 1962 article, "MCA: The Octopus Devours the World," in *Show*. [[Dan Moldea]] applied it to the company in his 1986 book *Dark Victory: Ronald Reagan, MCA, and the Mob*.[^8][^48]
