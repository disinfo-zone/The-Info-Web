Banca Nazionale del Lavoro (BNL) is a bank headquartered in [[Rome]] whose majority shareholder was the [[Italy|Italian]] treasury. A cable from the [[United States]] embassy in Rome, quoted on the House floor in 1992 by [[Henry B. Gonzalez]], described it as "an upstart bank by Italian standards, dating only to 1913 and owing its growth to its role as the key bank for the government in the 1920s and 30s."[^1] Its branch in [[Atlanta]] was searched by agents of the [[Federal Bureau of Investigation|FBI]] on August 4, 1989, and the records they seized showed the loans that became the [[BNL Scandal|BNL affair]].[^2]

### The Atlanta Branch

The Atlanta branch was managed by [[Christopher Drogoul]]. A [[Central Intelligence Agency|CIA]] memorandum of November 6, 1989 recorded that the branch had issued 2,500 unauthorized letters of credit for [[Iraq]] totaling $3.2 billion between February 1988 and July 1989, and that about $1.85 billion of that amount had been disbursed, including at least $800 million in letters of credit guaranteed by the [[Commodity Credit Corporation]] of the [[United States Department of Agriculture|Department of Agriculture]].[^3] The memorandum recorded that the branch had exceeded its allowable debt of $500,000 per customer, that it charged Baghdad an average commission of 0.2 percent where the usual rate for a poor credit risk was 15 percent, and that it funded the credits by borrowing from other banks for 90 to 180 days while allowing Iraq up to five years to repay.[^3] The same memorandum stated that BNL's North American headquarters in New York and the bank's directors in Rome publicly denied knowing about the letters of credit, "although a BNL official in Chicago claims he notified New York and Rome several times about the unusual activity in Atlanta, according to press reports," and that Rome "appears satisfied to date with the cooperation of the US investigating agencies and appreciates the low-key manner in which Washington has reacted."[^3] In the [[Los Angeles Times]] of November 13, 1992, Douglas Frantz described the investigation as concerning "$5 billion in secret loans to Iraq by the Atlanta branch."[^4]

Gonzalez put the total at $4 billion and said that $2.2 billion of the loans had gone to the Iraqi military technology procurement network.[^5] The [[Italian Senate]] commission of inquiry, in a preliminary report approved on December 21, 1991, put the exposures at about 4,000 billion lire, roughly a third of the bank's net worth, and recorded that facilities outside the official books, found after August 4, 1989, totaled $2,867.6 million against $991 million in credits registered at July 31, 1989.[^6] Two BNL-Atlanta employees went to federal authorities in July 1989 and reported the unauthorized loans. FBI agents raided the branch on August 4, 1989 and seized its records.[^7]

### What Rome Knew

[[Luigi Sardelli]] was appointed executive vice president and managing director of BNL's North American operations in July 1987. Gonzalez reported that Sardelli audited the bank's American operations and found major problems in Los Angeles, Chicago and Atlanta. In August 1988 he found a poorly collateralized loan to [[Entrade International]], which Gonzalez described as one of Iraq's key procurement agents, and reported it to Rome. Two months later his superior praised the Atlanta manager, Drogoul, despite Sardelli's criticisms. In October 1988, according to Gonzalez, [[Giacomo Pedde]], the director general in Rome, told Sardelli that it was premature to chastise the Atlanta manager. A critical audit report that Sardelli sent to Rome in late December 1988 was in English and was not translated into Italian until July 10, 1989, less than a month before the raid.[^8]

At about the time that report was sent, the management of the [[Udine]] branch received a request to finance a steel plant in Iraq for the Italian steelmaker [[Danieli]]. On January 26, according to Gonzalez, Pedde telephoned the head of his financial division and told him that the Danieli deal should get personal attention. Two days later Danieli signed a contract for 140 million German marks, about $70 million, to supply a rolling mill to the [[Nassr State Enterprise]] in Iraq. The loan was originated at Udine but handled through Atlanta.[^8] "Why not [[New York City|New York]]," Gonzalez asked, "especially since Sardelli had already reported problems in Atlanta to his superiors in Rome?"[^8] Gonzalez reported that the Italian Senate believed the deal was routed through Atlanta to keep other Italian companies with claims against Iraq from freezing the account.[^8] The Senate commission's own report recorded that the offices of the central direction in Rome worked to have the DM 140 million operation carried out through the Atlanta agency, and that the Danieli operation had been moved from Udine to Atlanta "anche per dirette pressioni esercitate dalla Direzione centrale" (also through direct pressure exercised by the central direction).[^6]

The commission also recorded the history of the audit report prepared by the bank's auditor Messere after the inspection that Sardelli ordered in September 1988: the report was filed away by the competent office of the inspectorate in Rome instead of being forwarded, and when Sardelli later sent all the area's inspection reports to Rome, the Messere report, written in English, was translated only after August 4, 1989.[^6] No inspection of the Atlanta branch had been ordered directly by the central inspectorate in Rome before the raid.[^6] Sardelli wrote that when he directed a Rome chief inspector then working in New York to audit the Atlanta agency, the inspector answered that Pedde, then managing director, had instructed him to audit Sardelli's expense account instead.[^9]

The [[Senate Select Committee on Intelligence]] staff recorded that CIA analysts concluded in January 1990 that "managers at BNL headquarters in Rome were involved in the scandal," and that in November 1991 CIA reports "confirmed press allegations that more senior BNL officials in Rome had been witting of BNL-Atlanta's activities."[^10] The staff wrote that "the evidence does not show that this failure was a deliberate attempt to withhold information from the Department of Justice."[^11] The staff also reported that it "could find no legal support" for the CIA guidance that a report not formally disseminated outside the agency would not be subject to discovery in a criminal case.[^12]

Gonzalez stated that the scandal forced the chairman, [[Nerio Nesi]], and Pedde, whom he there called vice chairman, to resign soon after the disclosure.[^13] Pedde was indicted in Italy by 1992 over transactions with [[Iran]].[^8] The Italian Senate commission recorded that no official of BNL in New York or Rome was among the defendants in the Atlanta prosecution, and that the thesis of a fraud by disloyal employees had proved "inconsistente" in light of the evidence the commission had gathered, which included confessions, testimony and documents.[^6] Of the thirteen Italian nationals whose depositions the government sought in Rome in 1993, six declared that they would testify in the United States and seven that they would not. The Eleventh Circuit's opinion lists the seven as Angelo Florio, "Nero Nesi" (the bank's chairman in 1989 was Nerio Nesi), Giacomo Pedde, Giovanni D'Ercole, Marco Lunazzi, Francesco Bignardi and Gerardo Ruggero, and records that Drogoul asserted that Pedde, Teodoro Monaco and Angelo Florio were the BNL officials who provided the authorizations he relied on.[^14]

### Cantoni and Secchia

Nesi's successor was [[Giampiero Cantoni]], whose ties to the [[Italian Socialist Party]] and to the former prime minister [[Bettino Craxi]] were, in the words of a [[State Department]] cable, a prerequisite for the appointment.[^1] The director general, [[Paolo Savona]], was close to President [[Francesco Cossiga]] and to [[Guido Carli]], the treasury minister. A capital increase already in preparation would bring in the state insurer INA and the state pension system INPS and reduce the treasury's holding from 75 percent to 56 percent.[^15]

Cantoni and Savona called on Ambassador [[Peter Secchia]] on October 19, 1989, at Cantoni's request, made on the return flight from the United States with President Cossiga.[^16] Savona was concerned about losing the Commodity Credit Corporation guarantee on roughly $1 billion of the bank's three billion dollar exposure in Atlanta. Cantoni said that his American lawyers were urging him to raise the matter to a "political" level, and that they thought charges would be filed under the Rico Act and that BNL or Iraqi assets could be frozen.[^15] The cable reported that the two men wanted to achieve "some kind of damage control." Gonzalez told the House that "on numerous occasions Mr. Cantoni approached United States Ambassador to Italy, Peter Secchia, about achieving damage control," and that less than ten days after one such request the [[White House]] called the Atlanta prosecutor in charge of the case.[^17]

On March 16, 1990, an executive vice president named DeVito told the embassy's economic officer that the bank might soon be indicted in the United States, that "matter was urgent," and that he believed the [[Department of Justice]] had taken the investigation out of the hands of the United States attorney in Atlanta.[^18] On July 25, 1990, eight days before the invasion of [[Kuwait]], Cantoni again "made a pitch for the U.S. government to go slowly before making indictments."[^17] Frantz wrote in November 1992 that a White House lawyer had telephoned the prosecutor early in the inquiry.[^4] On October 24, 1989, Carli responded negatively to a request by opposition senators to conduct an investigation of the affair.[^15]

### The Indictment and Entrade

The indictment, No. 1:91-CR-078 in the [[United States District Court for the Northern District of Georgia|Northern District of Georgia]], was returned on February 28, 1991, the day after President [[George H.W. Bush]] ordered the ceasefire in the [[Gulf War]]. It charged Drogoul on 347 counts with nine other defendants: two other branch officers, [[Therese Barden]] (vice president and operations officer) and [[Amedeo DeCarolis]] (operations assistant); Entrade International and its executive vice president, [[Yavuz Tezeller]]; the Iraqi state [[Rafidain Bank]]; and four Iraqi officials, [[Sadik Taha]], Abdul Rasheed, Raja Hassan Ali and [[Safa al-Habobi]].[^19] Two other Atlanta officers, Thomas Fiebelkorn and Leigh Ann New, were not carried into the indictment because they had negotiated plea agreements (New pleaded guilty on March 6, 1991 and Fiebelkorn on April 3, 1991), and the trade officer [[Paul Von Wedel]] pleaded guilty as a cooperating defendant; the five Atlanta employees were sentenced on August 24, 1993.[^20] Al-Habobi, described in the independent counsel's report as director general of the Al-Nasser Complex of the Iraqi Ministry of Industry and Military Industrialization, was also chairman, president, director or vice president of Iraqi-controlled corporations in England and the United States, including the [[Matrix Churchill|Matrix-Churchill]] entities, and returned to Baghdad after the search of the Atlanta office.[^21]

Entrade was an affiliate of the Turkish conglomerate [[Enka]]. A January 9, 1990 Justice Department letter described Tezeller as someone who could "provide information regarding after sales services (a fancy word for bribes, kickbacks), unearned consulting fees, and other payments to Iraqis."[^22] Tezeller refused to return from [[Turkey]] after the raid. Gonzalez reported that the State Department would not allow the Atlanta investigators to travel to Turkey to question him, and that Entrade had been involved in 52 CCC-guaranteed transactions with Iraq and BNL, in which, he said, a Department of Agriculture investigation found no evidence that the commodities arrived in Iraq.[^22]

Gonzalez also reported that the Justice Department had placed the Jordanian businessman [[Wafai Dajani]] on its list of proposed indictments, and that a February 1991 State Department memorandum said it had "no objections to indictment of any individual on the list" but added a disclaimer on Dajani, describing him as "a Jordanian businessman, not a government official" and noting that he was "considered well connected" and that his brother had been a minister.[^23] [[Joseph D. Whitley]], acting United States attorney in Atlanta from June 1, 1990, recused himself from the case the same day, because his former law firm had represented Matrix Churchill, the Iraqi-owned machine tool company that the branch had financed.[^24]

### Judge Shoob, the Plea and the Lacey Review

Drogoul pleaded guilty to 60 counts in June 1992. At a sentencing hearing on September 23, 1992, [[Marvin Shoob|Judge Marvin Shoob]] asked to see the intelligence underlying Gonzalez's summary of CIA reporting. Shoob took the documents home and concluded from one of them that it did not support the summary's analytical comment.[^25] On October 1, 1992, Shoob orally allowed Drogoul to withdraw the plea and, on the government's motion, recused himself; the written order is dated October 5, 1992. The order stated: "The Court concludes that officials at BNL-Rome were aware of and approved Mr. Drogoul's activities." The order noted that Drogoul had testified "only that his superiors should have known."[^26]

A CIA statement of February 5, 1993 announcing the release of the Inspector General's BNL report said: "The CIA's Inspector General found no evidence of deliberate withholding of information or wrongdoing by any Agency employee in connection with the BNL case." The statement attached a memorandum of January 19, 1993 from the Director of Central Intelligence, [[Robert Gates]], to the Inspector General. Gates wrote that Gonzalez "was given summaries of CIA intelligence reports accompanied by analytical comments that led him to conclude that CIA had information confirming that BNL-Rome knew of illegal lending activity by BNL-Atlanta," and that "the intelligence reporting on which this analysis was based does not support such a definitive conclusion." In the same memorandum Gates wrote two sentences about the CIA reports: "None of the information available to CIA establishes that BNL-Rome did know of the activities of BNL-Atlanta that later became the subject of DoJ's investigation and prosecution." and "Nonetheless, several of the CIA reports clearly contain indications that BNL-Rome may have been aware of BNL-Atlanta's actions." He also wrote that "In retrospect, mistakes clearly were made by Agency officers in their handling of events related to the BNL matter, and there was carelessness and a number of instances of poor judgment."[^27]

On October 16, 1992, [[William Barr|Attorney General William Barr]] appointed the retired judge [[Frederick Lacey]] as an "independent counsel" under Justice Department regulations, rather than under the independent counsel statute.[^28] Lacey's own report gives October 27 as the date on which Barr authorized a preliminary investigation and asked for a report by December 8, 1992. The Senate staff recorded that Lacey submitted his report on December 9.[^28][^29] Lacey wrote that "I find Judge Shoob's specific charges against the USAO, and his concern about the interference of other agencies, to be inaccurate."[^30] Gonzalez called the review "the Lacey Job" and said that "at best Lacey's probe was a whitewash."[^31]

Lacey wrote of the national security adviser and the deputy secretary of state: "It is clear that, while at [[Kissinger Associates]], [[Brent Scowcroft|Scowcroft]] and [[Lawrence Eagleburger|Eagleburger]] came into contact with BNL management. I have seen nothing, however, to suggest that they knew of BNL-Atlanta's wrongful loans to Iraq or influenced the prosecution of this matter."[^32]

On November 13, 1992, the Los Angeles Times reported that Lacey had sent Barr preliminary findings sufficient to proceed to a second phase. Senator [[David Boren]], chairman of the Senate Intelligence Committee, said that the committee was trying to determine "whether a decision was made at some level of government that the gathering of information took precedence over the prosecution of any individuals."[^4]

### Kissinger Associates

According to Gonzalez, Kissinger Associates was set up in 1982 when [[Henry A. Kissinger]] and Scowcroft joined in founding it.[^33] Lacey recorded that Scowcroft had served as vice president of the firm and headed its Washington office, and that Eagleburger had served as its president, before each joined the government.[^32] Gonzalez said that Scowcroft worked on the BNL account, met BNL management on numerous occasions, and briefed the BNL board on three occasions between 1986 and 1989.[^33] He also reported that a former head of BNL's American operations attended a 1987 meeting in New York with [[Alan Stoga]], the head of BNL's Rome headquarters, and Kissinger, Scowcroft and Eagleburger, held to give BNL advice on doing business in several countries including India, after which Stoga and Scowcroft took the BNL officers to lunch.[^34] Gonzalez said that "Kissinger, Scowcroft, and Eagleburger maintain that they were unaware of the BNL loans to Iraq," and added: "I offer no definitive proof that they were aware of the BNL loans."[^35]

Drogoul testified before the [[House Banking Committee]] on November 9, 1993, with other former employees of the bank. He said that he was made aware of Kissinger's membership on the bank's international advisory board at a business meeting in Italy. "As we became involved in the medium-term lending," he said, "it became clear to us that the people at Kissinger and Associates were somehow assisting clients of Iraq in further developing business." He added: "By the time we moved into the 1988 period or early 1989 period, it was fairly clear to me that Kissinger and Associates' people were involved in some way in trying to enhance and develop a relationship between Iraq and the United States. I really shouldn't go farther than that, because the rest is probably speculation."[^36]

Gonzalez reported that the White House had intervened in late 1989 to make sure that Iraq received a $1 billion allocation of Commodity Credit Corporation credits, and that the NSC staff contacted the Department of Agriculture about a delayed second installment of $500 million after the Iraqi ambassador complained to Scowcroft.[^33]

### Commodity Credit Corporation Credits

The Commodity Credit Corporation guaranteed repayment to American banks that financed exports of farm products through its GSM-102 and GSM-103 programs. Gonzalez said that BNL was the largest participant in the program that Iraq used to purchase about $5 billion in American agricultural commodities between 1983 and 1990.[^35] The General Accounting Office reported in October 1990 that the CCC had paid out about $3 billion in claims, was at risk for approximately $2 billion in guarantees on loans that Iraq had stopped paying, and that the Justice Department was investigating irregularities at one bank that had made more than $2 billion in loans to Iraq, only a fraction of them properly authorized.[^37] A March 1991 GAO report found that export credit guarantees approved for Iraq rose from about $400 million in fiscal 1983 to about $1.1 billion in fiscal 1988 and fiscal 1989, and that the Department of Agriculture rated Iraq a high-risk market in fiscal 1989 and 1990 while continuing to approve guarantees.[^38]

On January 21, 1993, Gonzalez told the House that BNL had sued on December 15, 1992 in the United States Court of Federal Claims to recover over $340 million on defaulted Iraqi loans guaranteed under the program.[^39]

### Matrix Churchill and the Arms Network

In a January 1993 floor statement Gonzalez asked whether the British government had informed the United States that the exports of Matrix Churchill to Iran were being financed by the Atlanta branch of BNL.[^40] He said that as early as 1987 Matrix Churchill sources had told British intelligence that machine tools shipped to the Nassr and Hutteen state establishments in Iraq were used in armaments production, and that British officials approved export licenses for the company well into 1990.[^40] Gonzalez called the appointment of [[Scott Inquiry|Lord Justice Scott]] to review the matter "akin to the U.S. Government appointing Judge Lacey to investigate the BNL scandal."[^41]

### Italian Senate Commissions and the P2 Lists

The Italian Senate created a special commission and then, by resolution of February 19, 1991, a commission of inquiry under Article 82 of the constitution into the Atlanta branch and its connections, chaired by Senator [[Gianuario Carta]]. Its preliminary report, approved on December 21, 1991, stated that the thesis limiting the affair to a fraud by a group of unfaithful officials had proved inconsistent, that the Atlanta operations could not have been unknown in Rome and New York and were instead tolerated if not encouraged ("tollerata se non incoraggiata"), and that the Department of Justice had denied the commission access to documents and witnesses, invoking grand jury secrecy and national security.[^6] The commission took testimony in New York in June 1991. Its final report was approved on April 22, 1992 as Doc. XXII-bis n. 4.[^42] The final report stated that the severest of BNL's early-1980s crises was connected to "la accertata partecipazione di gran parte dei suoi alti dirigenti alla loggia massonica P2" (p. 6) and declined to fix the degree to which "Drogoul, i suoi complici e le stesse strutture superiori della BNL" knew of Iraq's military design (p. 8). It recorded that credits to Rafidain Bank before June 1987 had been reported to the Rome head office under an internal circular of June 23, 1983 (p. 20); that dissent existed inside the US administration over licenses and refinancing decisions taken "con il consenso del Consiglio per la Sicurezza Nazionale"; that President Bush had expressly admitted support initiatives for Iraq extending from the start of the Reagan presidency to shortly before the Gulf War; and that Lawrence Eagleburger (adviser to the Ljubljanska Banka), Brent Scowcroft and Henry Kissinger (both BNL consultants) were among those connected to the bank (p. 23). The report asked whether the development of Atlanta as the center for financing Iraq was "casuale e indipendente, anziché organicamente strumentale" to US policy toward Iraq (p. 25). It noted the coming House Banking Committee interim report on the intelligence services and the National Security Council, and the consultancy given to BNL by Kissinger Associates and by Scowcroft "anche diversi mesi dopo il 4 agosto 1989" (p. 34). On the coming Atlanta trial, the report stated that Drogoul could confirm what he had told the commission, "la consapevolezza e la tolleranza dei vertici di Roma della BNL e delle autorità federali a fronte della macchina finanziaria attivata a favore dell'Iraq" (pp. 34-35). A second commission, chaired by Senator [[Giampaolo Mora]], was created by resolution of November 17, 1992, was constituted on February 11, 1993, and approved its final report on February 23, 1994 as Doc. XXII-bis n. 1.[^43] Gonzalez told the House in January 1993 that the second commission's mandate was to probe whether Italian government bodies, possibly acting in concert with foreign governments, had aided Iraq's military industrialization.[^44]

[[Rinaldo Petrignani]], the Italian ambassador in Washington from 1981 to 1991, answered Gonzalez's questions in writing on January 31, 1994 and listed his meetings in 1990: with [[Robert Ross]], assistant to the Attorney General, on March 21, at which he expressed the Italian government's concern about a possible indictment of BNL as an institution and said it seemed possible that Drogoul and his associates would try to place part of the responsibility on BNL; with the State Department legal adviser [[Abraham Sofaer]]; and, briefly, with Attorney General [[Richard Thornburgh]] at a White House reception. Petrignani named the director general for economic affairs at the Italian foreign ministry and the ministry's director of cabinet as the officials who directed him to attend.[^45] The independent counsel's report recorded that on September 21, 1990 Urgenson, the prosecutor [[Gale McKenzie]] and others from the Justice Department met Petrignani, Senator Carta and Professor Zanelli, counsel to the Italian committee, and that Petrignani was reported to have asserted that "BNL was the victim of a terrible fraud" and that the "reputation of BNL was of great importance as the Italian State is a majority owner." A Justice Department memorandum of January 1, 1991 recorded that Carta expressed skepticism that only BNL-Atlanta was involved in the scheme.[^46] In his written answers Petrignani said he had no knowledge of the Italian government's use of BNL-Atlanta to finance the sale of military hardware such as Sidewinder missiles and attack helicopters.[^45]

Sardelli's written responses quoted testimony of the Italian foreign ministry's secretary general, B. Bottai, before the second Senate commission on September 23, 1993: "the behavior of the Embassy was the one suggested also by the Ministry of Treasury, guardian angel of BNL's," and "it was in the interest of Italy that the responsibilities remained circumscribed to the Atlanta Agency." Sardelli also quoted a hearing of August 4, 1993 at which the former foreign minister [[Gianni De Michelis]] said that only one loan to Iraq "had characteristics of irregularity."[^47]

Luigi Sardelli wrote that Professor A. Ferrari, then BNL's managing director, "had to resign in shame in 1981 because of his affiliation with Lodge P2," which he called an illegal lodge in Italy, and that on December 25, 1975 Ferrari had asked him to go to the Gulf area to explore a BNL merchant bank in partnership "with a group of BNL friends." Sardelli wrote that on his return he told Ferrari that the people he had met, Pakistani nationals connected to [[Bank of Credit and Commerce International|BCCI]], were Ferrari's friends and that he was the wrong man to lead the joint venture. He wrote that Ferrari and A. Florio, whom Ferrari had handpicked to head the International Division, organized a BNL financial affiliate in Luxembourg; that Ferrari was a close friend of [[Roberto Calvi]] of [[Banco Ambrosiano]]; that Ferrari, assisted by Florio, put deals that lost money on the books of the BNL affiliate Capitalfin; and that the relationship with BCCI, [[Ghaith Pharaon]] and [[Marc Rich]] was the exclusive domain of Ferrari, Florio and A. Conzara.[^48]

The membership lists of the Propaganda Due ([[Propaganda Due|P2]]) lodge seized at Castiglion Fibocchi in March 1981 and made public by the Italian government on May 21, 1981 include "dott. Alberto Ferrari (Roma, 520)," annotated "ex direttore generale BNL."[^49] The parliamentary commission of inquiry on P2 chaired by [[Tina Anselmi]] reported that among the banks represented in the lists the Banca nazionale del lavoro was one for which the presences were particularly significant for quality and representativeness: four members of the board of directors, the director general, and three central directors, one of them the board's secretary.[^50]

### Venice and Iran

Gonzalez reported in January 1993 that prosecutors in [[Venice]] had filed, in December 1992, a 523-page indictment, after three years of investigation, implicating former top-level BNL officers and Italian export licensing officials in a scheme to send arms secretly to Iran in the latter half of the 1980s, in violation of an Italian government ban on arms sales to either side in the Iran-Iraq war.[^44] He said the indictment charged that senior BNL executives in Rome, and BNL branch officials in Paris and Turin, provided over $100 million in financial support for the shipments, that it also implicated BNL employees together with Italian secret service agents and Ministry of Defense officials who helped disguise the shipments, and that in total 59 persons had been indicted.[^51] Gonzalez noted that some of the BNL Rome executives implicated in the Venice indictment were at the center of the Atlanta loans.[^51] He quoted the Venice prosecutor: "I'm not a politician, I'm a Judge. But my main impression is that it was all greed * * *. Nobody had a straight policy."[^51]

### Drogoul's Sentence

On September 2, 1993 the Eleventh Circuit reversed the district court's refusal to allow depositions of the Italian witnesses in Rome.[^52] Drogoul then pleaded guilty in September 1993 to three counts and was sentenced in December 1993 to 37 months in federal prison.[^53] Alan Friedman wrote that the loans were used by Iraq's government to finance purchases of weapons, weapons components and dual-use industrial equipment.[^54]
