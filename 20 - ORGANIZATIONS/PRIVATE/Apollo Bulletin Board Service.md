The Apollo Bulletin Board Service, commonly known as Apollo BBS, operated as one of the world's largest online distributors of sadomasochistic child pornography during the mid-1990s. Based in the Dutch seaside resort town of Zandvoort, the Apollo BBS distributed child pornography globally through dial-up connections and early internet protocols. The system employed encryption and access controls to limit entry to verified members, creating a platform for the distribution of illegal materials to paying customers around the world.[^1]

### Discovery by Marcel Vervloesem

The Apollo Network came to light in June 1998 when [[Marcel Vervloesem]], a private investigator working with the NGO Morkhoven Workgroup, was investigating the disappearance of [[Manuel Schadwald]]. During his investigation, Vervloesem came across [[Gerrit-Jan Ulrich]], a 49-year-old computer technician who owned a computer store called Cube Hardware. Hulse called him a computer salesman, Davies a Dutch businessman and the Seattle Times a convicted German pedophile, and the 2022 parliamentary commission described him as coming from Germany.[^2][^3][^4][^5] Ulrich invited Vervloesem to his apartment, where he revealed himself as the administrator of the Apollo Bulletin Board Service and handed Vervloesem a collection of encoded disks containing thousands of images and videos of violent child abuse.[^6] Hulse wrote that Vervloesem and Robbie Van Der Plancken met in Amsterdam on June 9, 1998, that members of the Morkhoven group followed a stranger from the meeting to Zandvoort, and that Ulrich telephoned Vervloesem two days later and invited him to the flat. Hulse wrote that Ulrich was suffering from Aids and had only months to live, and that Vervloesem believed he wanted to leave the network.[^2]

Ulrich's apartment contained multiple running computers connected to five phone lines, with digital storage disks and networking equipment throughout. He claimed to have heart disease and not long to live. A few days after this meeting, Ulrich fled the Netherlands and called Vervloesem from Italy, stating he was frightened and revealing a cache of digital disks hidden beneath a floorboard containing information on his network of associates. On June 20, 1998, Ulrich's body was discovered in an Italian forest; he had been shot multiple times, and [[Robbie Van Der Plancken]] was arrested as a suspect in his murder.[^6]

### Network Operations and Scope

The Apollo BBS operation was sophisticated in both technical and organizational terms. As a bulletin board service operating in the mid-1990s, it used dial-up connections and early internet protocols to distribute child pornography to paying customers around the world. The system employed encryption and access controls to limit entry to verified members. The commercial nature of the operation suggested a well-organized business structure with customer management, payment processing, and content distribution systems.[^1] Hulse wrote that the service's paying members dialed in by telephone and could gain access to tens of thousands of illegal photographs, and that Ulrich's flat held five computers connected to phone lines.[^2]

When Dutch police searched Ulrich's apartment after his death, they discovered it contained tens of thousands of images and videos showing extremely violent sexual abuse of children, including infants. A child psychiatrist who inspected the materials stated: "I've never seen anything like this. The pictures show very disgusting things, sexual abuse, violence, and the tying up of young children. There were children aged four to five, children aged eight to nine, and I saw one child about 18 months." Some films were described as "almost killing," as "very special, very aggressive, very hard, mechanically brutal."[^7] Haarlem chief prosecutor Henk van Brummen said in April 1999 that 710 child-pornographic images were found in Ulrich's apartment, most of them years old, and that the Apollo user file held 60,000 photographs, most of them so-called teen pornography; he also reported 370 recognisable portraits of children, three offenders and 184 witnesses heard, and said no evidence existed of an international organisation of child-pornography producers run from Zandvoort.[^8] Davies and the 2022 commission gave 60,000 images overall, and La Repubblica gave about 70,000 images found in the Zandvoort home.[^3][^5][^9]

### International Distribution Network

The Apollo BBS was a distribution hub for materials produced by trafficking networks across Europe. The disks found beneath Ulrich's floorboard contained information on a network of suppliers involved in producing and selling these films and images. Two of those identified by Dutch authorities were [[Warwick Spinks]] and [[Lothar Glandorf]], both major figures in European child trafficking. Warwick Spinks' name was also found in an address book belonging to Robbie Van Der Plancken, who was closely associated with Ulrich.[^10]

The list of 300 names discovered during the investigation was described by media reports as "an international Who's Who of suspected paedophiles." This list included individuals from multiple countries and various professional backgrounds.[^10]

### Connections to Other Trafficking Networks

The Apollo Network served as a crucial intersection point for multiple trafficking operations. It was connected to the Warwick Spinks network in Amsterdam, the Lothar Glandorf operation in Rotterdam, and the broader European child trafficking infrastructure.[^10]

The connections extended to the Marc Dutroux case in Belgium. [[Gina Pardaens-Bernaer]] told friends and colleagues about a snuff film found on the disks that showed a girl being murdered by an associate of the child trafficking network exposed during the Dutroux investigation. She identified one of the perpetrators as an acquaintance of [[Jean-Michel Nihoul]], a key figure in the Dutroux case.[^11]

### The Apollo Disks and Their Contents

The "Apollo Disks" that Ulrich handed to Vervloesem contained encoded materials that, when decrypted, revealed thousands of images and videos of child abuse. These disks represented only a portion of the total materials distributed through the Apollo BBS. The materials ranged from standard child pornography to extremely violent content involving torture and murder.[^6]

Vervloesem, distrusting Dutch authorities, passed the disks to a colleague in the Morkhoven Workgroup, Gina Pardaens-Bernaer, who made copies before providing them to police. On November 15, 1998, a few months after taking possession of the Apollo Disks, her body was found inside the wreckage of her car, which had crashed into the concrete pillar of a bridge at full speed. Two days before her death, she had sent copies of the disks to the International Committee for the Dignity of the Child in Geneva with a letter stating her life had been threatened.[^11]

### Law Enforcement Investigation

The investigation into the Apollo Network was hampered by multiple factors, including the death of key witnesses and the international nature of the operation. Dutch police conducted a thorough search of Ulrich's apartment after his death, discovering the full scope of his operation. However, the investigation was complicated by the fact that the primary suspect was dead, many of the materials were encrypted, and the network extended across multiple countries with different legal systems and law enforcement priorities.[^7] Ulrich was shot on June 19, 1998 near Pomarance in the province of Pisa, according to L'Unità of July 17, 1998.[^12] The NOVA programme broadcast the story on the evening of July 16, 1998, and Dutch special agents raided a Zandvoort seafront flat on Wednesday morning, July 15, 1998, arresting one man and two women.[^12][^13] The 2022 parliamentary commission wrote that the Kennemerland police found more than 60,000 pornographic images on data carriers from the Zandvoort case, several hundred of them of children averaging between ten and twelve years old, and that the Morkhoven group had handed more than 90,000 images to Interpol.[^5] The commission wrote that because Ulrich died, prosecution and trial did not take place, and that cases linked to the Zandvoort material were dismissed for lack of evidence in several countries.[^5]

The Eerste Kamer's list of incoming documents for September 22, 1998 records a letter from the chairman of the Werkgroep Morkhoven "inzake kinderpornonetwerk te Zandvoort" (concerning a child pornography network in Zandvoort), listed as of interest to members of the Justice and Development Cooperation committees.[^14]

Gina Pardaens-Bernaer's death in particular raised serious questions about whether she had been murdered to prevent her from revealing information about the Apollo Network and its connections to powerful individuals. The timing of her death, coming shortly after she had received death threats and sent copies of the disks to international organizations, suggested foul play rather than accident.[^11]
