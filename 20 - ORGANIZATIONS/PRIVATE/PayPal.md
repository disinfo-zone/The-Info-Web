PayPal, Inc. was an online payment service formed in March 2000 from the merger of [[Confinity]], co-founded by [[Peter Thiel]] and [[Max Levchin]], and [[X.com Corporation]], founded by [[Elon Musk]]. It was floated on the [[Nasdaq]] in February 2002 and sold to [[eBay]] that October. Its 2001 registration statement describes its fraud-detection software, contemporaneous press describes its anti-fraud team's work with law enforcement, and its later regulatory history includes sanctions screening, consumer finance and cybersecurity matters.

### Founding and the 2001 registration statement

PayPal, Inc. "resulted from a merger between Confinity, Inc., incorporated in December 1998, and X.com Corporation, incorporated in March 1999."[^1] X.com "intended to provide Internet banking services to our customers."[^1] The two companies merged on March 30, 2000, with X.com as the surviving entity; by October 2000 the company had abandoned internet banking for the PayPal product, and it took the name PayPal, Inc. in February 2001.[^1]

Musk served as chief executive of X.com from March to December 1999 and as chief executive of PayPal from May to September 2000. [[Bill H. Harris]] was chief executive from December 1999 to May 2000. Thiel became chief executive and president in September 2000, and Levchin was chief technology officer from March 2000.[^2] [[David Sacks]] was executive vice president of product and [[Reid Hoffman]] executive vice president of business development, both from May 2000.[^2] [[Roelof Botha]] was chief financial officer and Sarah B. Imbach senior vice president of customer service, operations and fraud.[^2] Sacks became chief operating officer in February 2002; Hoffman was then listed as executive vice president, and Musk and Moritz were directors.[^3] In May 2001 the company entered a separation agreement with Musk, its former president and chief executive.[^4]

Before joining PayPal, Thiel "traded derivatives for CS Financial Products and practiced securities law with [[Sullivan & Cromwell]]."[^5]

The principal stockholders table in the registration statement lists Musk at 14.2 percent, the largest single position. Entities affiliated with [[Sequoia Capital]] held 10.7 percent, and their partner [[Michael Moritz]] appears in the table as a principal stockholder. [[Nokia Ventures]] held 9.6 percent. Thiel held 5.6 percent, Levchin 2.6 percent, and Hoffman less than one percent.[^6]

The company then had 10.0 million registered users as of September 1, 2001, with 2.0 million business accounts and 8.0 million personal accounts.[^1] Payments to business accounts, which the filing calls Gross Merchant Sales, were 88.8 percent of the 746.9 million dollars in total payment volume for the quarter ended June 30, 2001. The filing states that "a majority of these payments relate to sales of goods and services through online auctions."[^1] The filing states that "during the four months between July and October 2000, we experienced a significant fraud episode," with gross unauthorized charge-back losses of 5.7 million dollars for that period. It also records that [[MasterCard]] had fined the company for excessive charge-backs, and that "we resolved this situation to MasterCard's satisfaction."[^7]

### Fraud control and law enforcement

The registration statement describes "PayPal's proprietary IGOR system" as a program that "monitors account and transaction patterns in order to control our loss exposure once fraudulent funds enter the PayPal system."[^8] The fraud rate as a percentage of payment volume was 0.33 percent in the quarter ended June 30, 2001, against 0.54 percent in the same quarter of 2000.[^8]

A [[Newsweek]] article by [[Brad Stone]], published July 16, 2001, reported that the anti-fraud team had 75 members and "regularly works with law enforcement."[^9] The article says the software was named "Igor," "after a Russian mobster who plagued the system last year," and that it searched for patterns such as payments to an account that were consistently close to the maximum limit, or ZIP codes that did not match the area code on an account.[^9] A former Marine Corps investigator hired onto the team proposed the screening program, and the article quotes him describing the work as "the first sheriff to go after Jesse James."[^9] John Kothanek is named in the article as the investigator; executives told the magazine that the team had helped bust organized-crime rings in Chicago, Houston and Nigeria.[^9]

The same article reports that prosecutors in federal court in [[Seattle]] would receive computer logs and testimony from PayPal's anti-fraud team for a trial scheduled for September 2001, in which two men had pleaded not guilty.[^9] The defendants were Alexey Ivanov and Vasiliy Gorshkov of Chelyabinsk, Russia, brought to Seattle in November 2000 through an undercover [[Federal Bureau of Investigation]] operation that created a start-up computer security company named Invita. The Department of Justice announced in October 2001 that a jury had convicted Gorshkov on 20 counts, including fraud against PayPal, and on October 4, 2002 that [[John C. Coughenour]] had sentenced him to 36 months and ordered nearly 700,000 dollars in restitution to Speakeasy and PayPal. According to the evidence presented at trial, the scheme used programs that created thousands of anonymous e-mail accounts, opened PayPal accounts with random identities and stolen credit card numbers, and manipulated eBay auctions so that the conspirators were both seller and winning bidder.[^10][^11] The registration statement says that "federal and state law enforcement agencies could seize customer funds in PayPal accounts that are traceable to suspected criminal transactions," and that the company was subject to [[FinCEN]] regulations on reporting and record keeping.[^12] The filing states that the FinCEN rules would require money services businesses, beginning in 2002, to register with the Department of the Treasury and to report suspicious transactions of 2,000 dollars or more, and that in the quarter ended June 30, 2001, 0.1 percent of PayPal's transactions and 6.1 percent of its payment volume were in that range. PayPal then held money transmitter licenses in Oregon and West Virginia.[^12] The annual report filed March 13, 2002 states that the company had filed for registration with the Treasury and "commenced filing suspicious activity reports," that it held licenses in Oregon, West Virginia and Maryland, and that it had applied for money services business licenses in 18 jurisdictions, with applications to follow in seven more.[^13]

The February 2002 annual report lists two suits over account restrictions. On February 21, 2002, a class action captioned [[Kamdar v. PayPal]] was served in Santa Clara County Superior Court. It alleged that PayPal restricted accounts in breach of its user agreement and converted users' funds. On March 12, 2002, [[Fawcett v. PayPal]] was filed in the United States District Court for the Northern District of California, alleging violations of the [[Electronic Fund Transfer Act]] and conversion.[^14]

eBay's annual reports describe a second line of government inquiry into PayPal's service to online gambling merchants, a business that supplied about 6 percent of PayPal's 2002 revenue. In August 2002 PayPal agreed with the [[New York State Attorney General]] to stop processing payments from New York members to such merchants and to pay the State of [[New York]] 200,000 dollars in penalties, disgorged profits and investigation costs. Beginning in July 2002 PayPal produced documents in response to a federal grand jury subpoena issued at the request of the United States Attorney for the Eastern District of [[Missouri]]; PayPal finished exiting the business in November 2002. On March 28, 2003 the United States Attorney sent a letter asserting that the service violated 18 U.S.C. section 1960, a provision of the [[USA PATRIOT Act]] on the transmission of funds known to derive from a criminal offense, and offering settlement for the amount of PayPal's earnings from gambling merchants. In July 2003 PayPal agreed to pay 10 million dollars as a civil forfeiture and to have its USA PATRIOT Act compliance program supervised by an outside auditing firm. eBay recorded the payment as an adjustment to the purchase price of PayPal.[^15][^16]

### Account restrictions and political targets

In early December 2010, PayPal cut off the account that [[WikiLeaks]] used to collect donations. The company said in a blog posting that the move was prompted by a violation of its policy, which bars use of the service for activities that encourage, promote, facilitate or instruct others to engage in illegal activity.[^17] The account was held by the [[Wau Holland Foundation]], which said in a message on Twitter that its PayPal account had been taken down because of "financial support to WikiLeaks."[^17]

In February 2011, PayPal froze the account of [[Courage to Resist]], which gave donations to the legal defense fund of [[Chelsea Manning]] (then Bradley Manning). A PayPal representative told [[TechCrunch]] that the action was not WikiLeaks related and that the fund had been "only temporarily restricted." The representative said: "This has nothing to do with WikiLeaks."[^18]

On September 21, 2018, PayPal told [[Fortune]] that it planned to end its business relationship with [[Infowars]] and its sister sites following an internal review. Infowars reported that the company had given it ten days to switch payment processors.[^19]

On October 28, 2018, after the [[Pittsburgh synagogue shooting]], PayPal confirmed that it had suspended [[Gab]], a social network. The company said that it had been "in the process of canceling the site’s account before the tragic events occurred." It added: "When a site is allowing the perpetuation of hate, violence or discriminatory intolerance, we take immediate and decisive action."[^20]

### Flotation and sale

PayPal sold 5,400,000 shares at 13 dollars each in its initial public offering. The final prospectus, dated February 14, 2002, was filed with the [[Securities and Exchange Commission]] on February 15, 2002, and the shares were approved for quotation on the Nasdaq National Market under the symbol "PYPL."[^21]

On July 8, 2002, eBay announced that it would acquire the company. Each PayPal share was to be exchanged for 0.39 of an eBay share, and eBay valued the acquisition at 1.5 billion dollars based on its July 5, 2002 share price.[^22] The merger was completed on October 3, 2002.[^23] The completion announcement filed with the Form 8-K states: "Peter Thiel announced today that he has resigned as PayPal's CEO," and that [[Matt Bannick]], eBay's senior vice president for global online payments, would assume all of Thiel's responsibilities.[^24] That announcement valued the transaction at about 1.5 billion dollars on eBay's average closing price over the period two days before and after July 8, 2002.[^24]

Members of the founding group went on to found or lead [[LinkedIn]], [[Palantir Technologies]], [[Founders Fund]], [[Tesla]] and [[SpaceX]], and became known as the [[PayPal Mafia]].[^25] LinkedIn's registration statement of January 2011 describes Hoffman as a co-founder and chair of its board, and as executive vice president of PayPal from January 2000 to October 2002.[^26] Tesla's registration statement of January 2010 describes Musk as its chief executive and chairman, as chief executive and chief technical officer of Space Exploration Technologies, and as a co-founder of PayPal.[^27]

### Palantir and the intelligence community

Palantir Technologies Inc.'s registration statement of August 25, 2020 states: "We were founded in 2003 and started building software for the intelligence community in the United States to assist in counterterrorism investigations and operations."[^28] The filing describes its first platform, Gotham, as "constructed for analysts at defense and intelligence agencies."[^29]

The filing states that Thiel "is one of our co-founders and has served as the Chairman of our Board of Directors since 2003," and that he has been a partner of Founders Fund since 2005. Its biography of Thiel records that he "co-founded PayPal, Inc., an online payment company, where he served as Chief Executive Officer, President, and Chairman of its board of directors from 2000 until its acquisition by eBay in 2002."[^30]

The filing lists government users including the U.S. Army, Navy and Air Force, [[U.S. Special Operations Command]], the [[Centers for Disease Control and Prevention]], the Department of Agriculture, the [[Department of Homeland Security]], the Department of Veterans Affairs and the [[Food and Drug Administration]].[^29] The PayPal name appears in the Palantir filing only in Thiel's biography. [[Forbes]] reported in 2013 that Thiel's "concept for Palantir was to use the fraud-recognition software designed for PayPal to stop terrorist attacks," that the CIA's venture arm [[In-Q-Tel]] made two rounds of investment "totaling more than $2 million," and that "from 2005 to 2008 the CIA was Palantir's patron and only customer."[^44]

### Sanctions screening and OFAC

On March 25, 2015, the [[Office of Foreign Assets Control]] announced a settlement of 7,658,300 dollars with PayPal to settle potential civil liability for 486 apparent violations of the Weapons of Mass Destruction Proliferators Sanctions Regulations, the Iranian Transactions and Sanctions Regulations, the Cuban Assets Control Regulations, the Global Terrorism Sanctions Regulations and the Sudanese Sanctions Regulations.[^31] The release states that "for several years up to and including 2013, PayPal failed to employ adequate screening technology and procedures to identify the potential involvement of U.S. sanctions targets in transactions that PayPal processed."[^31]

The enforcement information describes approximately 486 transactions totaling about 43,934 dollars, and a total base penalty of 17,018,443 dollars. OFAC treated the apparent violations under the WMD Proliferators regulations as egregious.[^32] The account holder, Kursad Zafer Cire, had been designated by the State Department on January 12, 2009 under Executive Order 13382. The enforcement information says PayPal processed transactions for that account holder for approximately three and a half years, and that risk operations agents cleared name matches against the account on six separate occasions after the designation.[^32]

A second paragraph of the release describes 136 transactions totaling 7,091.77 dollars, processed between October 20, 2009 and April 1, 2013, to or from an account registered to an individual on the Specially Designated Nationals list. The automated interdiction filter did not initially identify the account holder as a potential match, and when it did, agents dismissed alerts on six separate occasions after failing to obtain or review documentation.[^31]

The 2015 annual report says the company routinely reports rejected and blocked payments to OFAC, and that after the March 2015 settlement OFAC issued new subpoenas seeking information about other transactions that PayPal had self-reported.[^33]

In its 2002 annual report, the company described procedures to consult "lists of known or suspected terrorists or terrorist organizations provided by U.S. government agencies" before opening an account, under the International Money Laundering Abatement and Financial Anti-Terrorism Act of 2001.[^34]

The 2022 annual report states that between January 2013 and January 2022 the company "voluntarily disclosed to OFAC transactions that were inadvertently processed" and responded to subpoenas and information requests about them. In January 2023 OFAC closed these matters with a cautionary letter and no monetary penalty.[^35]

### Consumer finance and cybersecurity regulators

In May 2015 PayPal entered a Stipulated Final Judgment and Consent Order with the [[Consumer Financial Protection Bureau]] over PayPal Credit practices between 2011 and 2015. The order required 15 million dollars in consumer redress and a 10 million dollar civil monetary penalty.[^36]

The company has since reported several further CFPB demands. An October 2023 civil investigative demand concerned error resolution under Regulation E and the presentment of transactions to linked bank accounts.[^37] A civil investigative demand from the [[Federal Trade Commission]] in February 2022 concerned commercial customers that submit charges on behalf of other merchants.[^37] A demand received in August 2024 related to PayPal Credit and to backup payment options in a digital wallet.[^38] The 2025 annual report also records demands on Regulation E and linked accounts, which the company says it is cooperating with.[^38]

On November 1, 2023, the company received a subpoena from the Securities and Exchange Commission Division of Enforcement relating to the PayPal USD stablecoin.[^37] In 2022 the company disclosed subpoenas from the SEC Division of Enforcement relating to whether interchange rates paid to the bank that issues PayPal-branded debit cards were consistent with Regulation II, and to marketing fees earned from those card programs.[^39]

On December 20, 2022, the State of [[Hawai'i]] Office of Consumer Protection filed suit against PayPal, and the company reported a final settlement on December 17, 2025.[^38] A putative securities class action filed in the [[New Jersey]] federal court in October 2022 concerns statements on net new active accounts and "the detection of illegitimately created accounts."[^38]

On January 23, 2025, the [[New York State Department of Financial Services]] announced a 2 million dollar penalty against PayPal for violations of its Cybersecurity Regulation. The department found that PayPal "failed to use qualified personnel to manage key cybersecurity functions," and that changes to data flows, made to produce [[Internal Revenue Service]] Form 1099-K documents for more customers, left Social Security numbers "unredacted and easily accessible to cybercriminals."[^40] The department's enforcement listing records the consent order as issued to PayPal, Inc. on the same date.[^41]

### Dialog and the Thiel network

A leaked website directory of [[Dialog]], the society associated with Peter Thiel, was reported by [[Wired]] on June 16, 2026. Wired wrote that the website directory names Trump administration officials, two US senators, "six members of the Paypal Mafia," a former Middle East chief of intelligence, a sitting ambassador to the United States, and the founders and directors of several surveillance, data-broker and advertising-data companies.[^42]

### Law enforcement requests

In June 2021 [[Access Now]] published a letter asking PayPal and [[Venmo]] to publish annual transparency reports that, "at a minimum, indicate how many requests to surveil, limit, investigate or shut down accounts were received, how many of these requests were complied with, and how many accounts were impacted."[^43]
