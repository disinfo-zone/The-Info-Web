[[Scientology]] is a body of beliefs and practices invented by the American author [[L. Ron Hubbard]], who published [[Dianetics: The Modern Science of Mental Health]] in 1950 through [[Hermitage House]] and chartered the [[Hubbard Dianetic Research Foundation]] in [[New Jersey]].[^1] A letter of June 12, 1954 on the letterhead of the [[Hubbard Association of Scientologists International]], 806 North Third Street, [[Phoenix]], Arizona, told the Phoenix [[Better Business Bureau]] that Scientology "is described as a science of knowingness" with the goal of increasing "recognition, memory, and reaction time."[^2] The [[Associated Press]] dated the founding of the Church of Scientology in [[Los Angeles]] to 1954, and a [[Marquis Who's Who]] entry reproduced in the [[Federal Bureau of Investigation|FBI]]'s file lists Hubbard as president of the [[Founding Church of Scientology]], Washington.[^3] The British Government's Minister of Health, [[Kenneth Robinson]], described it to the [[House of Commons]] on July 25, 1968 as "a pseudo-philosophical cult introduced into this country some years ago from the United States," with its world headquarters at [[East Grinstead]].[^4] A [[Reader's Digest]] article of May 1980, "Scientology: Anatomy of a Frightening Cult," is preserved in the [[Central Intelligence Agency|CIA]]'s reading room.[^5] The [[Internal Revenue Service]] stated in 1997 that the Church "was recognized as tax exempt after establishing that it was an organization operated exclusively for religious and charitable purposes."[^6]

### Practices, structure and vessels

The Associated Press described "auditing" as a process "in which a device that the church claims can read mental states is used in a process of purging negative thoughts."[^6] The [[Board of Inquiry into Scientology|Anderson Board]] and the [[Foster Enquiry|Foster enquiry]] each examined the Hubbard Association of Scientologists International, Hubbard's position as governing director, and the fees charged for processing.[^7][^4] The Foster report describes a "[[Sea Organization|Sea Org]]" cruising under the Panamanian flag, and a 1978 [[Los Angeles Times]] article in the CIA's clipping file calls the [[Apollo (Scientology ship)|Apollo]], a 3,280-ton vessel, the Church's flagship and "for nearly a decade the personal yacht" of Hubbard; it reports a June 25, 1971 death aboard the ship and [[United States Department of State|State Department]] correspondence about the inquiry of the woman's father at the Moroccan port of Safi.[^4][^8] The [[Los Angeles Superior Court]] wrote in 1984 that Hubbard "runs the Church in all ways through the Sea Organization, his role of Commodore, and the Commodore's Messengers."[^9] The [[Religious Technology Center]], whose director [[Mark Rathbun]] commented on the 1993 settlement, and a [[Flag Operations Liaison Office]], Eastern United States, described in its own letterhead as "a branch of the [[Church of Scientology of New York]]," appear as Church bodies in the AP report and in a 1974 letter to the Navy's records center.[^6][^10]

### Guardian's Office

The August 15, 1978 indictment in the District of Columbia describes the [[Guardian's Office]] as composed in each country of five bureaus, among them the Information Bureau, "assigned the responsibility for the conduct of covert operations including the collection of data and documents of interest to Scientology." It states that Hubbard, as "Commodore," exercised overall supervision; that [[Mary Sue Hubbard]] held the titles Controller and Commodore Staff Guardian; that [[Jane Kember]] was Guardian World-Wide, reporting directly to both; and that [[Morris Budlong]] was Deputy Guardian for Information World-Wide.[^11] The indictment's first overt act is a November 21, 1973 directive from Kember to [[Henning Heldt]] to obtain all [[Interpol]] documents "connecting its founder, L. Ron Hubbard, to criminal activity."[^11]

### Operation Snow White

The stipulation of evidence signed on October 26, 1979 by the nine defendants places Guardian's Office agents in the [[Department of Justice]] ([[Sharon Thomas]], in the Civil Division office handling Freedom of Information Act suits against the CIA, the [[Drug Enforcement Administration]], the [[United States Customs Service|Customs Service]], the [[Defense Communications Agency]], the Army, the [[Energy Research and Development Administration]], the [[United States Postal Service|Postal Service]] and Interpol), the Internal Revenue Service, the [[Coast Guard Intelligence]] office (Thomas) and the Drug Enforcement Administration ([[Nancy Douglass]]), and records the planting of a recording device at an IRS conference on November 1, 1974 and the theft of Interpol files.[^12] [[Michael Meisner]] and [[Gerald Bennett Wolfe]] were caught in the United States Courthouse in Washington on June 11, 1976 with a counterfeit IRS identification card in the name "John M. Foster."[^12] On March 2, 1976 [[Cindy Raymond]] wrote to Mary Sue Hubbard proposing a "[[Operation Snow White|Snow White]] Subprogram Hunter, Hunter X" whose targets included obtaining desired files in government agencies by "job penetration" and other infiltration.[^12] FBI agents searched Scientology offices in Los Angeles and Washington on July 7, 1977.[^12]

Mary Sue Hubbard, Henning Heldt, [[Duke Snider]], [[Gregory Willardson]], [[Richard Weigand]], [[Mitchell Hermann]], Cindy Raymond, Gerald Bennett Wolfe and Sharon Thomas were found guilty on the stipulated evidence, each on one count. Judge [[Charles R. Richey]] sentenced Mary Sue Hubbard on December 6, 1979 to five years and a $10,000 fine and Heldt, Snider, Willardson and Weigand to four years each; the [[United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit|Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit]] affirmed by per curiam opinion on October 2, 1981.[^13] The government's sentencing memorandum stated that the defendants "fulfilled his duties as expected by the Church of Scientology" and that the Guardian Orders "called for the execution of massive criminal conspiracies and rewarded the participants for their success."[^13] The presentence reports had stated, attributing it to the United States Attorney, that the perpetrators "went beyond what the Church of Scientology expected of them"; the government told the court that this misstated its view.[^13]

The CIA reading room holds [[Washington Post]] and other press accounts of the charges ("U.S. Charges Scientology Conspiracy," "11 Church Agents Accused of Spying, Bugging and Theft") and of a 1978 organization called [[American Citizens for Honesty in Government]], described by the [[Austin American-Statesman]] as a four-month-old creation of the Church inviting government employees to report waste and fraud.[^14] On February 17, 1978 the CIA's [[Office of Legislative Counsel]] replied to the [[Office of General Counsel]]'s Freedom of Information Division, in connection with the suit Scientology v. Director, FBI, that its indices and the Review Staff material supplied to the House and Senate contained nothing on the Founding Church of Scientology and its affiliated organizations.[^15]

### Official inquiries, courts and the 1993 tax settlement

The State of [[Victoria]]'s Board of Inquiry, appointed November 27, 1963 with [[Kevin Victor Anderson]], Q.C. as its sole member, reported in 1965: "Scientology is evil; its techniques evil; its practice a serious threat to the community, medically, morally and socially; and its adherents sadly deluded and often mentally ill." It recorded that Hubbard had "served as an officer in the United States Navy and appears to have been seconded to the [[Royal Navy]] for a period."[^7] The Foster enquiry, ordered printed by the House of Commons on December 21, 1971, quotes the Government's July 1968 view that Scientology was "socially harmful," records the Home Office policy excluding Hubbard and Mary Sue Hubbard from the United Kingdom, and states Foster's conclusion that Scientologists' complaints of religious discrimination were "quite unfounded."[^4] The Foster report also discusses the booklet "Brain-washing: A Synthesis of the Russian Textbook on Psychopolitics," remarking that "Its style resembles more that of Mr. Hubbard than that of the Marxist-Leninist phraseology standard in Soviet official literature."[^4] The CIA's STARGATE collection includes a bibliography listing Foster's 1971 report, Hubbard's Dianetics and Dianetics Today, [[George Malko]]'s Scientology: The Now Religion (1970) and [[Roy Wallis]]'s The Road to Total Freedom (1976).[^16]

In Church of Scientology of California v. [[Gerald Armstrong]] (Los Angeles Superior Court No. C 420153), Judge [[Paul Breckenridge]]'s memorandum of intended decision, filed June 22, 1984, held that neither plaintiff had clean hands and that the Church and Mary Sue Hubbard were to take nothing on the tort claims. It found that "the organization over the years with its 'Fair Game' doctrine has harassed and abused those persons not in the Church whom it perceives as enemies," called "the practice of culling supposedly confidential 'P.C. folders or files' to obtain information for purposes of intimidation and/or harassment" "repugnant and outrageous," and quoted a 1970 French police report.[^9]

The Internal Revenue Service dispute with the Church, which the Associated Press dated from 1967, ended in a closing agreement of 1993. According to the AP, the Church dropped its more than 2,000 lawsuits and paid $12.5 million, the IRS declared the Church and related entities tax-exempt (including a trust that oversees the cruise ship [[Freewinds]]), the Church set up a tax-compliance committee, and the IRS could levy up to $50 million in penalties if officials were enriched; Rathbun said the document was "a peace treaty. The war is over."[^6] The IRS issued a statement on December 31, 1997 after the [[Wall Street Journal]] reported the agreement, noting that unauthorized disclosure of a closing agreement would violate Internal Revenue Code Section 6103.[^6]

### Scientologists at Stanford Research Institute

Several people at [[Stanford Research Institute]] in the early remote viewing work, later the [[Stargate Project]], were reported in print as Scientologists. In the CIA STARGATE collection, a psychological report on six subjects in the SRI parapsychology study states that "at least four of the subjects in this study describe to me an interest in scientology and two of them indicate that they have been involved in the Church of Scientology for some time."[^17] [[Time (magazine)|Time]] magazine of March 4, 1974 called [[Hal Puthoff]] a promoter of "the bizarre and controversial cult of Scientology," which [[Ingo Swann]], "another psychic tested by S.R.I., also practices."[^17] The [[New Scientist]] of October 17, 1974 wrote of Puthoff that he "has gone through encounter groups and other West Coast fads, and is now a Scientologist (as is Ingo Swann)."[^17] In [[The Humanist]] in 1977 [[Ray Hyman]] wrote that Puthoff "has made it to the level of a Class III Operational Thetan in the Church of Scientology" and had previously "obtained funding to study the [[Backster Effect]]."[^17] A December 5, 1977 letter to the fellows of the [[Committee for the Scientific Investigation of Claims of the Paranormal]], in the CIA's STARGATE collection, quotes "Dr. Puthoff is a Scientologist" among its examples of "Personal Defamation" aimed at investigators.[^17] The CIA's reading notes on [[John L. Wilhelm|John Wilhelm]]'s [[The Search for Superman]] list "World of the Thetans" and "scientology: various levels of achievement."[^17]

John L. Wilhelm wrote in the Washington Post in 1977, under the heading "The Scientology Link," that "one item that continues to trouble those in government who follow the SRI work is the unusually large number of Scientologists involved with the psychic tests there"; that Puthoff "is a high-ranking Scientologist and has written defenses of his organization's methods and beliefs"; that [[Pat Price]], who "died two years ago, also was a high-level 'Operating Thetan'"; that the laboratory assistant to Puthoff and [[Russell Targ]] during many of the [[Uri Geller]] experiments "was a Scientologist married to a 'minister' of Scientology"; that some of the initial funding came from a ranking Scientologist; that Swann "helped to establish Scientology's 'Celebrity Center' in Los Angeles, the headquarters recently raided by the FBI"; and that Swann at his original testing "revealed that there are 'fourteen Clears [a medium-high level of Scientology achievement], more than I would suspect,' working at SRI."[^18] Wilhelm wrote that the involvement of Scientologists "partially accounts for at least two government agencies pulling back from funding further work there."[^18]

On November 16, 1977 officers of the CIA's [[Office of Technical Service]] briefed staff of the [[House Permanent Select Committee on Intelligence]] on OTS involvement in ESP research, after a Washington Post Parade article of August 7, 1977 titled "Psychic Spying," and left a written outline, an [[IEEE]] article on the SRI work and "a brief article on Mr. Pat Price." The article on Price describes him as the new president of a [[West Virginia]] coal company and quotes him: "He credits Scientology for 'opening new vistas and abilities of self to me and which I now use as a matter of fact.'"[^18] An SRI session transcript of October 4, 1973 has Price tracking Targ, [[Hugh Crane]] and Ann Price and Puthoff through [[Menlo Park]] and saying: "Just took a look at the Scientology Center. They're not there."[^19]

[[Jon Atack]] wrote that Puthoff posted a reply saying his involvement with Scientology was trivial, and that he answered by stating Puthoff was OT III; [[Tony Ortega]]'s introduction to Atack's comments adds an editorial note, citing [[Hugh Urban]]'s history of Scientology, that Puthoff "was OT VII by 1971, the highest level then attainable." Ortega also published a paper by Swann, "Scientological Techniques: A Model Paradigm for the Exploration of Consciousness and Psychic Integration," which he said Swann gave in 1973 at the First Conference on Psychotronic Research in [[Czechoslovakia]].[^20] [[Jim Schnabel]]'s Remote Viewers (1997) writes that Puthoff and Swann had both joined Scientology in the 1960s; that Puthoff resigned his membership in the mid-1970s and afterward supported a group of anti-Scientologists, and that Swann later resigned as well; that Price "up to his death, had been deeply involved in Scientology" and had been known to Puthoff through a Scientology course in Los Angeles; that one of Puthoff's laboratory assistants was a Scientologist; that the astronaut [[Edgar Mitchell]], whose private foundation had funded some of SRI's work with Uri Geller, "had briefly taken an interest in Scientology"; and that a senior official of Price's West Virginia coal company "had heard about Price on the Scientology grapevine." Schnabel dates the conversion of Hubbard's Dianetics organization into the Church of Scientology to 1953.[^21]
