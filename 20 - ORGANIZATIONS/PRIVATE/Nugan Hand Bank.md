Nugan Hand Bank was an Australian merchant bank founded in Sydney in 1973 by [[Michael Hand]], an American Green Beret and Vietnam veteran, and Frank Nugan, an Australian lawyer. It operated internationally for seven years before collapsing in 1980 following Nugan’s death and Hand’s disappearance, leaving approximately $50 million in debts. Australian government investigations and journalist [[Jonathan Kwitny]]’s *The Crimes of Patriots* (W.W. Norton, 1987) documented the bank’s alleged function as a conduit for CIA financial operations, drug money laundering, and covert military funding across Southeast Asia, the [[Middle East]], and the Pacific.[^1]

### Founders

Michael Hand was a U.S. Army Special Forces veteran who served in Vietnam and Laos. His time in Laos connected him to the CIA’s paramilitary operations network there - specifically the networks associated with [[Ted Shackley]], who directed the CIA’s Laos station from 1966 to 1968. Hand’s exposure to the CIA’s covert financial and logistics infrastructure during the Laos war was the foundation of his subsequent career as a banker. After Vietnam, Hand settled in Australia, where he met Frank Nugan through Sydney business circles.[^1]

Frank Nugan was a Sydney lawyer from a fruit-farming family in Griffith, New South Wales - a region long associated with organized crime and drug trafficking in Australian criminology. Nugan brought Australian legal and financial access to the partnership. Together, Hand and Nugan established Nugan Hand Ltd. (later Nugan Hand Bank) to provide offshore banking, currency trading, and financial services to American military and intelligence personnel in the Pacific region.[^1]

In 1969 Nugan and Hand got their start as a business team by forming [[Australasian and Pacific Holding]], which the New York Daily News described as a "tourism" company. Four of its shareholders gave their business address as [[Air America]], the CIA-owned airline.[^2] Kwitny wrote that the list of thirty-seven shareholders of Australasian and Pacific Holdings filed with the government in May 1970 showed four with the address "c/o Air America, A.P.O. San Francisco," one with the address "c/o Continental Air Service," and five reachable through the U.S. Agency for International Development.[^3] CBS reported on September 22, 1982 that four of the original stockholders were Americans who listed their addresses as Air America, Army Post Office, San Francisco.[^4] The Progressive reported in December 1980 that the bank had been founded in 1973 by Hand and several others "listing their addresses as 'c/o Air America, San Francisco'."[^5] The Australian Dictionary of Biography gives the bank's nominal paid-up capital as $1 million.[^6] ProPublica gave the starting capital as $80.[^7] Bob Bottom wrote in Connections 1 that in 1970 Nugan and Hand formalised their relationship by establishing Yorkville Nominees Pty Ltd, an investment advisory company, and launched Nugan Hand Ltd in 1973 with paid-up capital of $1 million, increased to $2 million four years later.[^8]

### Operations and CIA Personnel

What distinguished Nugan Hand from conventional offshore banks was the composition of its board of directors and senior personnel. Among those who held positions at Nugan Hand or served as consultants were:

- Admiral [[Earl P. Yates]], retired from the Navy and formerly head of planning for the U.S. Pacific Command, who headed the Washington, D.C. office in 1980 and was described by the Task Force report as then president of the bank[^9][^10][^11]
- General [[Edwin Black]], former commander of U.S. forces in Thailand, who became president of Nugan Hand, Inc., Hawaii, and acted as the bank's representative there; John Owen, who ran the bank's Bangkok branch, claimed in his book that Black was approached to join the bank and declined[^2][^3][^9][^11]
- General Earle Cocke Jr., a former president of the [[American Legion]] and Korean War veteran, who handled the bank’s Washington connections
- Walter McDonald, a former CIA Deputy Director for Economic Research, who served as a consultant
- Dale Holmgren, a former CIA officer and Air America pilot, who managed the Taiwan office
- William Colby, former Director of the CIA (1973-1976), who served in a legal advisory capacity[^1]

The presence of senior American military and intelligence officials in bank positions - receiving salaries for what appeared to be minimal work - was later characterized by investigators as providing a combination of access, legitimacy, and protection from regulatory scrutiny.[^1]

The Wall Street Journal reported in May 1980 that the bank's Washington, D.C. office was headed by Admiral Yates and that Donald Beazley was president of U.S. operations. Beazley had resigned by May 1980.[^9] A former Green Beret friend of Hand, [[George Farris]], worked in the Washington office, according to the Daily News.[^2] Former CIA director William Colby "confirmed that he had done legal work for Mr. Nugan but said that was the extent of their relationship."[^9]

The Progressive reported in December 1980 that Walter McDonald, described as a twenty-five-year consultant to the CIA, was "a close friend" of [[John Paisley]], a former CIA analyst of Soviet arms developments who died in September 1978, and that Paisley's telephone diary had McDonald's name in it.[^5] The Atlanta Journal reported in September 1981 that Paisley had been "particularly preoccupied" with the bank's operations in August and September 1978.[^12]

### Financial Operations

Australian government investigators and Kwitny’s reporting identified several categories of Nugan Hand’s activities:

The bank operated branches in thirteen countries including Australia, [[Hong Kong]], Taiwan, the Philippines, Thailand, Singapore, the United States (Hawaii and Washington), Saudi Arabia, the Cayman Islands, and Bermuda. Its offshore structure was designed to obscure the origins and movement of funds across jurisdictions with limited regulatory cooperation.[^1]

The journalist Jonathan Kwitny wrote that the bank laundered proceeds from [[Golden Triangle]] opium and heroin trafficking through its Southeast Asian branches, particularly in Thailand and Hong Kong. The connection between CIA-linked operations in the Golden Triangle region, Air America’s acknowledged transport activities, and the bank’s financial flows was documented but not legally established in terms of criminal liability.[^1]

The bank also handled financial transfers for covert operations. Kwitny documented that Nugan Hand managed accounts for clients in multiple countries who appeared to be receiving or disbursing funds for intelligence-related purposes. The specific operations are difficult to fully document because the bank’s records were largely destroyed or disappeared after its collapse.[^1]

The Australian Joint Task Force on Drug Trafficking reported that the bank had offices and mail drops in 13 countries in two hemispheres during its seven years in business, and named 26 reputed drug dealers.[^13] Kwitny wrote in the Journal in August 1983 that Nugan Hand "participated in at least two U.S. government covert action operations, and had stronger links to the U.S. intelligence community than was previously known," a picture he said emerged from the Australian government report and from the Journal's own reporting.[^10] The New York Times reported that the Task Force was told by an American witness that Hand was involved in a 1976 shipment of 10 million rounds of ammunition and 3,000 weapons, which was delivered to U.S.-supported forces in [[Angola]], and that Hand and the bank took part in a 1976 movement of a U.S. Navy spy ship to [[Iran]] with [[Edwin Wilson]].[^13]

### Collapse

On January 27, 1980, Frank Nugan was found shot to death in his car near Bowenfels, New South Wales, with a rifle beside him. His death was ruled suicide. Near his body was a business card for former CIA Director [[William Colby]], who was serving in the bank’s legal advisory capacity. Australian police found Nugan’s body with a bible containing what appeared to be personal notes referencing senior American political and intelligence figures.[^1]

The news of Nugan’s death triggered a run on the bank and rapid dissolution of its operations. Michael Hand convened an emergency meeting of the bank’s staff and reportedly spent days attempting to organize the removal and destruction of bank records before investigators could secure them. New South Wales [[Corporate Affairs Commission]] investigators who arrived found that substantial records had been removed or destroyed.[^11] The Royal Commission recorded that its work was hampered by "the concealment or destruction of a number of relevant documents immediately following the death of Nugan."[^14]

Hand disappeared from Australia on June 14, 1980. According to Kwitny’s reporting, an American identified in sources only as "Charlie" - a former Special Forces soldier and CIA operative - assisted Hand in obtaining a false identity and departing on a flight to Fiji. Hand has not been located by Australian authorities since. A separate Australian police investigation in later years reportedly established that Hand had relocated to the United States under an assumed identity and had been protected by American intelligence community connections.[^1]

A Sydney butcher and friend of Hand, who was granted immunity from prosecution in 1981, told the Task Force that in late February 1980 he met [[Bernie Houghton]] and bank director George Shaw at the bank's headquarters at 55 Macquarie Street, that Shaw filled about fifteen boxes with records from an office in room 1709 of the AMP Centre in Bridge Street, and that the boxes were moved to a flat in Ultimo and later to a cold store. He said Hand asked him not to tell Hand where the records were kept.[^11] A Task Force investigator, [[Kevin Woods]], told the butcher that Hand had stated in his Corporate Affairs Commission cross-examination that he helped remove the records.[^11] The butcher described Hand's departure on a long weekend in June 1980, by way of Fiji and Honolulu to Vancouver.[^11]

### Investigations

The [[Royal Commission into Drug Trafficking]], whose terms of reference under Mr Justice [[Donald Stewart]] were extended on March 28, 1983 to cover the Nugan Hand Group, investigated Nugan Hand’s operations and the circumstances of Nugan’s death and Hand’s disappearance. The commission’s work was significantly hampered by the destruction of records, Hand’s disappearance, and the refusal of American officials and military personnel to provide information. The commission's final report, tabled in November 1985, found that the allegations of CIA involvement lacked substance and that there was no objective evidence that the CIA used or was used by the group, and recorded that its work had been hampered by the concealment or destruction of documents after Nugan's death and by the absence of evidence from Hand.[^14][^15] The Australian Dictionary of Biography says that the 1983 to 1985 royal commission "uncovered little new information," and the New York Times reported that the 1985 report disputed some Task Force conclusions.[^6][^13]

On February 24, 1983 the Prime Minister announced that the life of the Royal Commission had been extended to March 28, 1983 and that the Commonwealth was discussing terms of reference for an inquiry into Nugan Hand.[^16] The first interim report was presented on October 13, 1983 and tabled on December 6, 1983, the second was presented on December 13, 1984 and tabled on March 20, 1985, and the final report was tabled on November 27, 1985.[^14]

The Wall Street Journal reported in November 1982 that a cabinet minister had called the Task Force material "compelling evidence" of widespread criminal activity, and that the report found "strong grounds to believe" the bank's officers were involved with U.S. and Australian intelligence agencies, but not "conclusive proof" that the bank was connected to an intelligence operation.[^17] The Corporate Affairs Commission reported in 1983 that Frank Nugan had been involved in "massive international and local fraud" and had deliberately falsified the bank's accounts to conceal debt.[^6]

[[Neil Evans]], a former bank executive given immunity from prosecution, told a CBS broadcast on September 22, 1982: "We were to become the paymasters for the CIA around the world."[^4] The CIA's response was a terse "no comment" in February 1981.[^2] The Nation reported in March 1984 that the agency flatly denied any connection with the bank and refused to cooperate with the Australian investigation.[^18]

Kwitny’s *The Crimes of Patriots* (W.W. Norton, 1987) provided the most comprehensive reconstruction of the bank’s operations, based on interviews with numerous participants, Australian court and regulatory records, and documents that survived the post-Nugan records destruction.[^1]
