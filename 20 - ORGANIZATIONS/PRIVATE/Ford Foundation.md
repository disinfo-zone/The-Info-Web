The Ford Foundation is a philanthropic foundation chartered in Michigan in 1936 by [[Edsel Ford]] with an initial gift of 25,000 dollars.[^1] Ford Motor Company stock valued at 417 million dollars passed to it after the deaths of Edsel Ford in 1943 and [[Henry Ford]] in 1947. By New Year's Eve 1950 the endowment stood at 492 million dollars, and its assets dwarfed those of the [[Carnegie Corporation of New York]] and the [[Rockefeller Foundation]], the two largest American foundations until then.[^2] From April 1951 the foundation's officers were approached by the [[Central Intelligence Agency]] (CIA) and the [[U.S. Department of State]] about channeling Cold War projects through the foundation. It financed the [[Congress for Cultural Freedom]] (CCF) from 1957 until the successor organization, the [[International Association for Cultural Freedom]] (IACF), was dissolved in March 1978.

### Founding and the Gaither study

The foundation's presidents were Edsel Ford, from February 1936 to June 1943; [[Henry Ford II]], from July 1943 to November 1950; [[Paul G. Hoffman]], from November 1950 to March 1953; [[H. Rowan Gaither]], from March 1953 to October 1956; [[Henry T. Heald]], from October 1956 to December 1965; and [[McGeorge Bundy]], from March 1966 to May 1979.[^3]

Henry Ford II commissioned a blue-ribbon panel led by Gaither to explore the foundation's future. The panel advised that the foundation become a national and international philanthropy, and its recommendations were approved unanimously in 1950.[^4] The Henry Ford museum's collection of foundation papers lists a "Report of the Study for the Ford Foundation on Policy, 1949."[^5] Gaither had helped turn the [[Rand Corporation]] from an Air Force branch into an independent think tank and served as a RAND trustee until 1959. Henry Ford II had brought him in during 1947 to help set the foundation's priorities.[^6]

### Hoffman's first contacts with the CIA, 1951

On April 3, 1951 Hoffman and his associates met Secretary of State [[Dean Acheson]], who assured them that his department would put its liaison machinery "into high gear" for exchanges with the foundation.[^7] The same day they met CIA Director [[Walter Bedell Smith]] and [[Allen Dulles]], who was soon to be promoted to Smith's deputy as director of plans. After instructing their hosts "never to refer to [this meeting] as long as we lived," Smith and Dulles raised the agency's plans. These included the rehabilitation of defectors and the settlement of exiles from the Soviet bloc, medical and laboratory equipment for the [[University of Tehran]], and "area centers" to study regions open to Soviet overtures.[^8]

A memorandum dated April 2, 1951 and signed by [[Max Millikan]] as the agency's assistant director for research and reports listed projects the agency wanted the foundation to support. The list named area studies of Indonesia, Indochina, India, Pakistan and Iraq, and an analysis of the nature, validity and scope of Soviet statistics and statistical methods.[^9]

On April 13, 1951 staff delegations from the State Department and the agency met the foundation in New York. The foundation was told that it might receive project proposals backed by both. The first projects it was asked to support "unqualified and urgent" were the [[Free University of Berlin]], a university for Soviet bloc exiles near Strasbourg, the [[International Federation of War Veterans' Organizations]], and a study of Soviet tactics in the Far East under the auspices of the [[National Committee for a Free Asia]], which the agency had created.[^10]

On April 30, 1951 [[Sidney Hook]] applied for 1,000,000 dollars over three years for the CCF.[^11] [[Joseph McDaniel]], the foundation's Pasadena officer, questioned the CCF's usefulness and advised that the CIA take on full sponsorship. [[Robert M. Hutchins]] found Hook "contentious and erratic" and regarded the CCF's purpose as "mere propaganda." The Pasadena office voted against the proposal "as a matter of policy." On June 27, 1951 the foundation notified Hook that it could not comply.[^12] The outcome was communicated to the agency by [[James Burnham]], who had acted as liaison between the CCF and the agency.[^13]

### The Fighting Group and the National Committee for a Free Europe

In April 1951 the foundation obtained a grant of 150,000 dollars for the Fighting Group against Inhumanity, a West Berlin anti-Communist group. [[Eleanor Roosevelt]] had pressed Hoffman to take an interest in the group, with the approval of the State Department and the CIA. Hoffman required that the money be used for humanitarian activities, namely locating arrested and missing people in the Soviet zone and aiding victims of Soviet injustice, and he stated that "we cannot contribute to subversive activities." The grant was therefore channeled through the [[National Committee for a Free Europe]] (NCFE), which Langenkamp describes as a CIA proprietary set up to carry out covert refugee assistance and counterpropaganda, including the work of [[Radio Free Europe]].[^14]

Foundation officers later became alarmed by the group's "more aggressive action in fields and by means not entirely consistent with the purposes of the Foundation" and moved to suspend the final allotment. [[Bernard Gladieux]], who was still acting as a liaison with the CIA, persuaded his colleagues that cancellation would "completely disrupt the present relations between the NCFE and the Fighting Group."[^15] In 1954 Gladieux acknowledged his continuing relationship with the CIA at a hearing of the [[Reece Committee]].[^16]

In 1952 Hoffman told the trustees that the foundation was not to support activities "which would destroy its effectiveness in working for peace" by appearing as a tool for combating Soviet power.[^17] A proposed "Free University in Exile," an NCFE project, was sidetracked by Hutchins and others on the ground that the NCFE was "a cold war propaganda organization rather than an educational institution." The NCFE's president, [[DeWitt C. Poole]], regarded that reasoning as tenuous, given that the foundation had granted 1,309,500 dollars to the Free University of Berlin.[^18]

By early 1954 the foundation's leadership had adopted, in the words of [[Don K. Price]], the position that "we're just not going to mix in with these action agencies" like the CCF. A renewed request for 500,000 dollars for the CCF's publication activities, submitted by both the CCF and the CIA in March 1954, faced rejection, according to Langenkamp.[^19]

### Hoffman's departure and the Gaither years

In February 1953 Henry Ford II, a staunch supporter of Gaither, forced Hoffman to resign. At the same trustees' meeting Gaither was elected president, director and trustee.[^20] Langenkamp describes the two men's approaches as diverging, with Hoffman favoring international dialogue and Gaither a harder line in the Cold War.[^20]

### Richard Bissell and the staff of 1952

The Eisenhower Presidential Library's finding aid for the papers of [[Richard Bissell]] states that he joined the foundation's staff in January 1952 after leaving the [[Economic Cooperation Administration]] (later the [[Mutual Security Agency]]) and, after a brief tenure, resigned in 1954.[^21] Langenkamp describes Bissell as a consultant to the foundation who left in 1954 to become special assistant to Allen Dulles.[^22] Langenkamp also cites [[Burton Hersh]] for an account in which Bissell, as Dulles's special assistant, helped [[Franklin A. Lindsay]] on an "internal, no-holds-barred study of techniques that could be used to roll back the Communists in Europe."[^23] Langenkamp describes Lindsay as a CIA operative whom the foundation took on after his return from Europe, where he had helped set up the network of counter-front groups that included the CCF.[^23]

### Shepard Stone, John McCloy and the European program

[[John J. McCloy]], the former United States High Commissioner for Germany, had been brought in as a consultant by Henry Ford II. McCloy pressed the foundation to add a full European program, and [[Shepard Stone]], McCloy's former public affairs director at the High Commission, became its director.[^24] On December 31, 1951, as he left Germany, McCloy wrote to Hoffman urging the foundation to "help to carry on certain operations which the future [US] Embassy may find difficult to continue, but which are of great significance to United States objectives in Germany."[^25] Stone regarded the CCF as "the most effective organization in Europe working among political, intellectual, and cultural leaders."[^26]

The foundation's 1965 annual report states that McCloy served on the board of trustees from 1953 and as its chairman from 1959, retiring on December 31, 1965, and that [[Julius A. Stratton]], president of the [[Massachusetts Institute of Technology]] and a trustee since 1955, was elected chairman to replace him.[^27] By 1962, when Stone proposed a guarantee for the CCF, McCloy was chairman of the board.[^28]

On April 29, 1953 McCloy wrote to Allen W. Dulles that he was "delighted to have a report from Shep Stone" that Dulles would participate in a study group. The group would be "undertaken at the Council on Foreign Relations under a grant of the Ford Foundation," with a full-time staff under [[Henry Roberts]], then on leave from [[Columbia University]]. "Though the group will be entirely unofficial, its findings will be made available to our government," McCloy wrote.[^29]

On February 25, 1954 Stone wrote to McCloy about the CCF's funding. "You know the sources of funds that have hitherto been supplied to the Congress, but in our conversation with these gentlemen [Nabokov and Rougemont] we should not reveal what we know," he wrote.[^30] Stone later told the foundation's oral history project that he never held a security clearance, but that he would from time to time approach Dulles for information about Eastern Europe.[^31]

### Price's rule and the CIA arrangement, 1954 to 1962

A memorandum of May 21, 1954 by Don K. Price set the foundation's policy on government-sponsored programs. Such projects could be recommended, infrequently, if they were "worth support on their merits, regardless of any official interest," and only with the prior approval of three trustees: "the Chairman, the President, and 'Mr. McCloy.'"[^32] Langenkamp writes that Price and McCloy later defended the arrangement as a means of limiting the CIA's access to foundation officers and grantees.[^33] In McCloy's words, the foundation could not keep its "shirts clean" if it closed the door on the agency completely. Price had warned Dulles and [[Frank Wisner]] sternly: "The first time you meddle with our boys," he said, "we'll quit this whole program."[^33]

[[Thomas Braden]], the CIA officer in charge of its public and private relations, recalled in an interview that the agency "never used the large and genuine American foundations like the Rockefeller, Ford or Carnegie, except once in the case of the Ford Foundation." Langenkamp notes that it is not clear whether the statement is true, or for which project it would apply.[^34]

Dulles repeatedly sought to recruit the foundation's fellows. In 1958 he invited several foundation officers to Washington for a briefing on agency operations. In the early 1960s Heald repeated the foundation's position to [[John A. McCone]], then the CIA director, without effect.[^35]

In February 1961 the trustees disallowed grants to tax-exempt organizations that could not fully account for their other sources of income. In 1962 they added that institutions receiving funds from "undisclosed United States Government sources" did not qualify.[^36]

### The Congress for Cultural Freedom, 1957 to 1967

In the fall of 1957 the foundation approved a two-year grant of 500,000 dollars toward the expansion of the CCF's international activities, including conferences, seminars and study groups, and an outreach program for intellectuals from Poland and Hungary. Stone's International Affairs Division then extended annual support of 250,000 dollars through the end of 1962.[^37] Langenkamp writes that the CCF "finally achieved self-sufficiency through Ford's 1957-1962 grant" at the same time that the cover of the agency's network of front organizations was blown.[^38]

In 1962 Stone proposed that the foundation guarantee "some $2,000,000 per year for the next five years," so that other foundations could later take over part of the burden. The proposal foundered, despite the support of McCloy.[^39] In 1964 Stone sought a consortium of foundations to raise "a fund of approximately $15-20 million over the next ten years." The Rockefeller entities would not raise that sum without a substantial Ford contribution, and the consortium did not form.[^40]

An attempt to have the foundation adopt full sponsorship of the CCF also failed under Heald. [[Michael Josselson]], the CCF's executive director, later reported that Heald "had little use for culture and equally little use for the earlier CIA involvement."[^41]

In 1964 [[Wright Patman]], chairman of the subcommittee on foundations of the House, held a public hearing at which he named eight of the CIA's funding instruments, the so-called "Patman Eight."[^42]

### The 1966 and 1967 disclosures and the International Association for Cultural Freedom

From April 25 to 29, 1966 the [[New York Times]] published a series of articles on the CIA's funding of private organizations. The April 27 article mentioned the CCF and its magazine [[Encounter]].[^43] [[Arthur Schlesinger Jr.]] wrote to Bundy, who had succeeded Heald in March 1966, asking whether he could "illuminate or reinforce" the CCF's application. Bundy assured him that the foundation would give the CCF's needs careful consideration.[^44] Bundy asked the foundation's Overseas Development Division for a review. Its vice president, [[Champion Ward]], judged in favor of the CCF despite reservations, and [[James A. Perkins]], president of [[Cornell University]], wrote that the CCF was "a gamble, but an important one."[^45]

On September 23, 1966 Bundy wrote to Stratton, then chairman of the board of trustees, that the foundation would make a grant of 7,000,000 dollars to the CCF over six years. "It may raise a few eyebrows among those who know or think they know the previous financial history of the Congress," Bundy wrote, "but it is the right thing to do at the right time."[^46] The foundation's annual report for 1966 records that "in further support of the Congress for Cultural Freedom" the foundation granted 1.5 million dollars, that the congress would use the funds for general support and to strengthen its emphasis on less-developed countries and dialogue with East European intellectuals, and that its new board of trustees was headed by Raymond Aron.[^47]

In March 1967 the San Francisco magazine [[Ramparts]] reported the CIA's funding of the [[National Student Association]]. [[Sol Stern]] wrote the account.[^48] In April 1967 [[Jason Epstein]] published "The CIA and the Intellectuals" in the [[New York Review of Books]]. In May 1967 Thomas Braden, a former chief of the CIA's International Organizations Division, defended the program in the [[Saturday Evening Post]] as a counteroffensive against Communist fronts. The article disclosed, without naming names, agents placed in the CCF and on the editorial board of Encounter.[^49]

On May 13, 1967, at an extraordinary session of the CCF General Assembly in Paris, Josselson disclosed the organization's financial history and the CIA's role in it. The assembly expressed regret that Josselson had accepted CIA money without informing his colleagues.[^50] [[Raymond Aron]], whom Stone had asked to chair the new board, resigned when he judged the organization to be moving in the wrong direction.[^51]

The CCF was renamed the IACF. In September 1967 [[Francis X. Sutton]], vice president of the foundation's International Affairs Division, recommended five more years of support with more non-American financing. Bundy did not reverse the grant, but the last three installments were reduced by nearly 1,000,000 dollars in total.[^52]

Shepard Stone, who had been with the foundation for thirteen years, left in 1967 for Paris to lead the IACF, and [[Pierre Emmanuel]] became its director.[^53] Stone's fundraising in Europe and Japan did not produce a lasting replacement for foundation money.[^54] In 1972 the foundation, on the recommendation of its program officer [[David Heaps]], renewed support for five years at 400,000 dollars a year. The IACF was dissolved on March 31, 1978.[^54]

In a 1977 evaluation of the grant files, [[Craufurd Goodwin]] called the foundation's involvement with the CCF and IACF "one of the most interesting and complex involvements of the Ford Foundation over the course of its relatively brief history," and put the total of its grants at 8,400,000 dollars over two decades.[^55]

In 1972 the foundation granted 50,000 dollars to promote Encounter in the United States. Seventy-three American and English writers and editors protested the grant in a petition organized by [[Partisan Review]], which had itself been rescued more than once by CIA money channeled through the CCF.[^56]

### The Katzenbach Committee and the end of covert grants, 1967

After the 1967 disclosures, [[Lyndon B. Johnson]] established a committee composed of [[Nicholas Katzenbach]], Under Secretary of State, [[John W. Gardner]], Secretary of Health, Education and Welfare, and [[Richard Helms]], the CIA director. Its inquiry concerned government activities that might "endanger the integrity and independence of the educational community." The [[Church Committee]] describes the committee's life as 43 days, and states that President Johnson accepted its recommendations on March 29, 1967.[^57] The committee's report, as printed in the [[Foreign Relations of the United States]] series, recommended that no federal agency provide covert financial assistance to educational or private voluntary organizations.[^58]

On June 4, 1968 [[Dean Rusk]] advised Johnson, in a follow-up on the committee's work: "It seems to me far better to let the CIA matter wither away and let a new administration take a fresh look at the possibilities."[^59] The report of the Rusk committee states that Johnson directed termination of the covert support no later than December 31, 1967; Langenkamp writes that the recommendations were implemented with a few exceptions, among them Radio Free Europe and [[Radio Liberty]].[^60]

### Grants to Massachusetts Institute of Technology

The Church Committee's Book I describes a research institute at a major American university that the CIA helped establish in 1951 and funded for more than a decade, at the request of Under Secretary of State [[James E. Webb]]. The report does not name the institute.[^61] The [[Center for International Studies]] (CENIS) of MIT began operating in the fall of 1951. Millikan, a professor of economics who had spent a year at the CIA, accepted its directorship in February 1952, and in the spring of 1952 the Ford trustees granted CENIS 875,000 dollars to found a research program in international communication, according to [[Daniel Bessner]].[^62] The fiscal 1965 annual report lists a grant of 400,000 dollars for MIT under the Harvard-M.I.T. Joint Center for Urban Studies.[^63]

The foundation's annual report for 1954 describes a program of research in economic development and political stability, carried on by the center, that "also received long-term Foundation support," research "mainly in India, Indonesia and Italy."[^64] The report's grant list for the Massachusetts Institute of Technology carries four columns (unpaid December 31, 1953; grants in 1954; payments in 1954; unpaid September 30, 1954). "Completion of field project in Indonesia by Center for International Studies" shows 50,000 dollars unpaid at the end of 1953 and paid in 1954. "Research in economic and political development by Center for International Studies" shows 175,000 dollars unpaid at the end of 1953 and paid in 1954, and a new grant of 750,000 dollars in 1954 unpaid at September 30, 1954. A further MIT entry, "Research on international communications," shows 500,000 dollars unpaid at both dates, and "Research on international affairs" 5,000 dollars unpaid at the end of 1953.[^64][^65]

### Grants to RAND Corporation

After RAND separated from [[Douglas Aircraft]] on May 14, 1948, it lobbied for and received from Henry Ford II of the foundation an interest-free loan of 100,000 dollars and a credit guarantee of 300,000 dollars. In 1950 the foundation extended the loan to 1,000,000 dollars, and it eventually converted the loan into a grant.[^66] In a memorandum of May 5, 1951 to Gaither, [[Hans Speier]], director of the RAND social science department, advised that before the foundation came to the aid of government-related projects it should establish that its action was "unobjectionable in the light of Foundation objectives and reputation, both in the United States and in the European country concerned."[^67] Speier, one of the foundation's RAND consultants, wrote that private organizations could "play the role which overt government effort can no longer assume."[^68]

### Grants to the Council on Foreign Relations

The foundation funded the 1953 study group on relations with the Soviet Union described in the McCloy letter above. The fiscal 1965 annual report lists a grant of 1,000,000 dollars to the [[Council on Foreign Relations]] under the heading American education in world affairs.[^69]

### Grants to the International Institute for Strategic Studies

The fiscal 1965 annual report states that the [[International Institute for Strategic Studies]] in London received a grant of 550,000 dollars for further internationalization and for expansion of research, conferences, publications, translations and library resources.[^70]

### The East European Fund and Columbia University

Langenkamp writes that the foundation's Free Russia Fund, soon renamed the [[East European Fund]], was created to accommodate defectors, exiles and refugees from behind the Iron Curtain, to further their appreciation of the "free world" and to acquire intelligence about Soviet conduct. [[George F. Kennan]] proposed these projects, and Kennan later admitted that the exile projects he had proposed were ones the State Department had failed to realize.[^71]

Langenkamp notes that [[Philip E. Mosely]] advised the Rockefeller Foundation against an intelligence unit proposed by the State Department's Policy Planning Staff, which Langenkamp calls the "Eurasia Institute." Mosely later ran a Russian program at Columbia University that secured support through the East European Fund, which Langenkamp describes as the Ford Foundation's subsidiary.[^72]

### Behavioral sciences and the Center for Advanced Study

The foundation helped establish the [[Center for Advanced Study in the Behavioral Sciences]] at [[Stanford University]].[^73] The foundation's annual report for fiscal 1954 records that the major grant among sixteen in the behavioral sciences, 3,411,590 dollars, supported the center for five years, that it began operations at Stanford in September 1954, and that thirty-eight scholars formed its first group of fellows.[^74]

### Parapsychology

The [[Parapsychology Foundation]] was founded in 1951 by [[Eileen Garrett|Eileen J. Garrett]] with the support of [[Frances P. Bolton]], and was chartered in Delaware on December 14 of that year.[^75]

### The 1969 tax reform hearings

In 1969 the [[House Ways and Means Committee]] began hearings on general tax reform and devoted its first week of testimony to foundations. Wright Patman was the first witness.[^76] McGeorge Bundy defended travel and study grants totaling more than 130,000 dollars to eight former staff members of [[Robert F. Kennedy]]. Smith writes that the grants "raised suspicion, resembling too closely political favors." Bundy's testimony was not well received, and he later acknowledged as much.[^76]

The grants are recorded in the Rockefeller Archive Center grant file "Travel And Study Awards For Members Of The Staff Of The Late Senator Robert F. Kennedy."[^77] Smith also writes that a 1967 foundation grant to the Cleveland chapter of the [[Congress of Racial Equality]] for a voter registration drive was felt by many to have helped secure the mayoral victory of [[Carl Stokes]], and that the foundation's involvement in the voter registration drives of the [[Southern Regional Council]] offended officials in the South. After Congress passed the [[Tax Reform Act of 1969]], Bundy urged foundations to make the new regulations effective, so that they would become "permanent safeguards against abuse."[^78]

### The 2001 Durban conference

In 2003 the [[Jewish Telegraphic Agency]] published a two-month investigation by [[Edwin Black]], the first part of a series titled "Funding Hate." The series described the foundation as a multimillion-dollar funder of human rights groups that attended the 2001 United Nations World Conference Against Racism in [[Durban]].[^79] It reported that the [[Palestinian Committee for the Protection of Human Rights and the Environment]], known as LAW, received three Ford grants totaling 1.1 million dollars since 1997. It reported that the [[Palestinian NGO Network]] received Ford grants totaling 1.4 million dollars from 1999 to 2002, plus a 270,000 dollar supplement. More than a dozen activist groups received well over one million dollars in Ford grants earmarked for advertising, public meetings and advocacy at the conference. Ford's Cairo office, the series reported, extended more than 35 million dollars to about 272 Arab and Palestinian organizations in 2000 and 2001.[^79]

The foundation's communications vice president, [[Alex Wilde]], initially declined to answer questions. He later sent a written statement: "We have seen no indication that our grantees in Durban or elsewhere engaged in anti-Semitic speech or activities. The Foundation does not support hate speech of any kind."[^80]

### Records at the Rockefeller Archive Center

The Ford Foundation's records are held at the [[Rockefeller Archive Center]] in Sleepy Hollow, New York. The series cited on this page are the Oral History Project (FA618), the Office Files of H. Rowan Gaither (FA621), the Office Files of Robert M. Hutchins (FA703), the Office Files of McGeorge Bundy (FA617), the General Correspondence (FA735), the Office Files of David Bell (FA624), the Office Files of Francis X. Sutton (FA568), the Francis X. Sutton Papers (FA1141) and the grant files (FA732).[^81]
