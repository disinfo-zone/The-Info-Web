Johns Hopkins University is a private research university in [[Baltimore]], [[Maryland]], founded in 1876 as the first American university built on the German research-university model. Its administration of large federal contract-research organizations made it an institutional hub for Cold War military and intelligence science.[^1][^2]

### Operations Research Office

From 1948 the university operated the [[Operations Research Office]] (ORO) under [[U.S. Army]] contract, a classified body that conducted operations analysis during the [[Korean War]] and studies of psychological warfare and unconventional conflict.[^1][^2] A 1995 history of federally funded research centers prepared by the [[Office of Technology Assessment]] records that ORO had been in existence for two years when the Korean War began in 1950, that its director [[Ellis A. Johnson]] personally led a team of four into the battle area, and that the contingent grew to eight teams of 40 analysts. Over 100 ORO staff, subcontractors and consultants worked in Japan and Korea, and 113 of them received the Korean Service Medal of the United Nations Command. The field work included evaluations of close air support, the use of indigenous manpower and the effectiveness of leaflets, and the author [[S. L. A. Marshall]] worked as an ORO consultant and wrote a primer of Chinese tactics that was distributed to platoon level in the Eighth Army.[^3]

The same history states that the Army's interest in psychological warfare in Korea led it to establish the [[Human Resources Research Office]] (HumRRO) in 1951 under a contract administered by [[George Washington University]], and that most of ORO's human factors work passed to HumRRO and later to the [[Special Operations Research Office]] (SORO). It describes SORO as formed in 1957 as a contract agency under [[The American University]] to specialize in what is now called low-intensity conflict or guerrilla warfare.[^3] SORO and its successor [[CRESS|SORO]] ran the controversial [[Project Camelot]] counterinsurgency study.[^1][^2]

The OTA history states that ORO was dissolved and a new organization established in June 1961, and that the change was made so that the Army could remove Johnson. According to that history, Johns Hopkins, ORO's administrative organization, was not willing to remove Johnson under Army pressure, and the two parties agreed to terminate the contract. The Army then established the [[Research Analysis Corporation]] (RAC); a new contract between the Army and RAC took effect on September 1, 1961, and ORO's research program, personnel, physical assets, leases and contracts for supplies and services transferred to it. The history adds that a senior ORO staff member quipped that RAC stood for "Relax and Cooperate."[^3]

### Applied Physics Laboratory

The university's [[Johns Hopkins University Applied Physics Laboratory|Applied Physics Laboratory]] (APL) was founded on March 10, 1942, three months after the United States entered the Second World War, as part of a federal effort to mobilize scientific resources for wartime problems. It was assigned the task of finding a more effective way for ships to defend themselves against enemy air attacks, and it designed, built and tested the radar proximity fuze known as the VT fuze.[^4] The laboratory describes the fuze as having been judged, along with the atomic bomb and radar, one of the three most valuable technology developments of the war, and states that the government, the university and APL then committed to continue their relationship, after which the laboratory became a major contributor to guided missiles and submarine technologies.[^4] APL became a major Navy research center and one of the largest university-affiliated defense laboratories in the United States.[^1]

APL describes itself as a not-for-profit university affiliated research center.[^5] Its 2025 description states that "APL's UARC sponsor is the U.S. Navy," and that each university affiliated research center has a primary sponsor and a primary contract that define the limits of competition.[^6] The same document says APL performs sponsored work for "the Intelligence Community," along with the DOW, NASA and other agencies.[^6]

The Terrier missile became operational aboard [[USS Boston]] in 1956. In 1957 two APL physicists, William Guier and George Weiffenbach, developed a method of tracking satellites from the Doppler shift in the signals of the Soviet [[Sputnik]], and in 1958 APL invented the Doppler-based [[Transit]] satellite navigation system. APL designed, built, tested and operated numerous Transit satellites in the 1960s and 1970s; the system was fully operational in 1964 and improved the ability of U.S. submarines to determine their positions.[^7] In 1974 APL tested an engineering development model of the AN/SPY-1 radar for the [[Aegis combat system|Aegis]] system aboard USS Norton Sound, and in 1981 the guided-missile cruiser USS Ticonderoga became the first vessel with the Aegis combat system, with APL serving as technical advisor.[^8] APL also performed the Polaris flight-test analysis of 1957 and the Trident II accuracy evaluations of 1989.[^7][^8]

APL's Space Department developed a rotatable litter chair for weightlessness experiments on [[Skylab]] in 1973. APL built the Near Earth Asteroid Rendezvous spacecraft, launched on February 22, 1996, which became the first spacecraft to orbit an asteroid on reaching 433 Eros on February 14, 2000, and the MESSENGER spacecraft, launched on August 3, 2004, to orbit Mercury.[^9] The New Horizons spacecraft was designed and integrated at APL, launched on January 19, 2006, and flew past Pluto on July 14, 2015, with [[Alan Stern]] of the [[Southwest Research Institute]] as principal investigator.[^10] The Double Asteroid Redirection Test spacecraft, designed, developed and managed by APL for the [[NASA]] Planetary Defense Coordination Office, launched on November 24, 2021.[^9]

USAspending.gov records prime contract awards to the laboratory under the recipient name "The Johns Hopkins University Applied Physics Laboratory LLC" (unique entity identifier C1HBCJ9RMBV3). A spending-by-awarding-agency query for that identifier, contract award types A through D, with action dates from October 1, 2007 (the earliest date the database accepts) through September 30, 2026, run on October 14, 2026, returned $19,446,135,312.75 from the [[Department of Defense]] and $4,732,501,683.12 from NASA, with smaller totals from the Department of Homeland Security ($385,438,283.40) and the Department of Health and Human Services ($95,385,866.08).[^11]

Annie Jacobsen wrote that scientists from APL built an anechoic facility inside the [[Walter Reed Army Institute of Research]], under the direction of [[Richard S. Cesaro]], in which primates were irradiated with microwave beams at a power density similar to that of the [[Moscow Signal]].[^14] An ARPA memorandum of September 27, 1967, signed by Cesaro as acting director of advanced sensors, concerns a project designated BIZARRE.[^15] [[J. Allen Hynek]] was supervisor of technical reports at APL from 1942 to 1946.[^16] Thornton Page of Johns Hopkins University is among the signatories listed on the Durant memorandum concerning the [[Robertson Panel]].[^17]

### School of Advanced International Studies

The [[School of Advanced International Studies]] (SAIS) was founded in 1943 by [[Paul Nitze]] and [[Christian A. Herter]] along with other leading statesmen. The school became a division of Johns Hopkins in 1950, and in 1989 it was renamed for Nitze.[^18]

Among the school's alumni with intelligence careers is [[John E. McLaughlin]], who received his SAIS master's degree in 1966. In January 2005 the school announced that he had been appointed a senior fellow of the [[Merrill Center for Strategic Studies]]. The announcement recorded that he had been deputy director of the [[Central Intelligence Agency]] since October 2000 and acting director from July to September 2004, and it credited him with founding the [[Sherman Kent School for Intelligence Analysis]].[^19]

Paul Linebarger of SAIS was one of eleven members of the [[Quantico Vulnerabilities Panel]] of 1955, which [[W. W. Rostow]] chaired.[^20] In June 2022 [[CNN]] reported that a Dutch intelligence agency had publicly identified a man who studied at SAIS under the name "Victor Muller" as [[Sergey Cherkasov]]. CNN described him as "alleged to be a Russian spy" and reported that he was known and liked by fellow students and faculty.[^21] [[Ben Buchanan]] became the Dmitri Alperovitch Assistant Professor at SAIS.[^22]

### MKULTRA

A CIA memorandum dated December 2, 1953, addressed to Colonel Edwards by the chief of the agency's SSD, concerns [[Harold Abramson]]. It describes him as a physician educated at [[Columbia University]] who had been chief of an allergy clinic at [[Mount Sinai Hospital]] in New York, and it records that he was an instructor in medicine at Johns Hopkins University from 1928 to 1929. The memorandum states: "Subject is a physician but not known as a psychiatrist."[^23] The document is among the [[Project MKUltra]] records in the CIA reading room.[^23]

The Senate Select Committee on Intelligence and the Subcommittee on Health and Scientific Research of the Committee on Human Resources held a joint hearing on the program on August 3, 1977, titled "Project MKULTRA, the CIA's program of research in behavioral modification."[^24] In his opening statement Senator [[Edward M. Kennedy]] said of the records the CIA had located in March 1977: "Eighty-six universities or institutions were involved."[^25] [[Stansfield Turner]], the Director of Central Intelligence, stated in his prepared statement that the agency held "the names of 185 nongovernment researchers and assistants who are identified in the recovered material dealing with these 149 subprojects," and "names of 80 institutions," which "include 44 colleges or universities, 15 research foundation or chemical or pharmaceutical companies or the like, 12 hospitals or clinics, in addition to those associated with the universities, and 3 penal institutions."[^26]

[[John Marks]] wrote that after the CIA severed the [[Society for the Investigation of Human Ecology]] from [[Cornell University]], the Society's board in 1957 included [[John C. Whitehorn|John Whitehorn]], chairman of the psychiatry department at Johns Hopkins University, together with [[Carl Rogers]] of the University of Wisconsin and [[Adolf A. Berle]]; Marks identified [[Harold G. Wolff]] and Lawrence Hinkle as the board's president and vice president.[^27] [[D. Ewen Cameron]] trained in psychiatry at the Phipps Clinic at Johns Hopkins before going to Canada.[^28]

### Faculty, students and staff

[[Lincoln Gordon]] succeeded [[Milton S. Eisenhower]] as president of the university in 1967 and resigned in 1971; the Baltimore Sun recorded that his adoption of a student-faculty-administration recommendation in 1969 led to women being allowed to enter the university for the first time, and that he faced an operating deficit of more than $4 million.[^29] [[René Girard]] taught at the university as a professor from 1957.[^30] [[Stephen Kosslyn]] was an assistant professor at the university before moving to Harvard.[^31][^32] [[Ray Hyman]] took a bachelor's degree from [[Boston University]] in 1950, a master's degree in 1952 and a doctorate in February 1953 from Johns Hopkins.[^33] [[Jason Matheny]] holds a Ph.D. in applied economics and an M.P.H. from the university.[^34] [[Risa Heller]] took a master's degree in communications at the university after an undergraduate degree at the [[University of Michigan]].[^35][^36] [[S. C. Gwynne]] took a master's degree in writing at the university.[^37] [[George R. Wackenhut]] directed physical education and coached at the university from 1946, and took a master's degree there in 1949.[^38]

### Hospital and medical training

[[Alexander M. Haig, Jr.]] died at [[Johns Hopkins Hospital]] in Baltimore on February 20, 2010, at the age of 85. A hospital spokesman attributed his death to complications of an infection.[^39]

### Bloomberg gifts

On Sunday, November 18, 2018, [[Michael Bloomberg]] announced a gift of $1.8 billion to his alma mater for student financial aid. The [[Associated Press]] reported that the gift was "the largest ever to any education institution in the U.S.," and quoted the university as saying it would allow Johns Hopkins to eliminate student loans in financial aid packages starting the following fall. University president Ronald Daniels said the gift would also let the institution permanently commit to "need-blind admissions."[^40]

On July 8, 2024, [[Bloomberg Philanthropies]] gave a further $1 billion to the university. [[NPR]] reported that the gift would make tuition free for most medical students.[^41]

### Event 201

The Johns Hopkins Center for Health Security, "in partnership with the World Economic Forum and the Bill and Melinda Gates Foundation," hosted Event 201, which the center described as a high-level pandemic exercise held on October 18, 2019, in New York.[^42] The center described it as a 3.5-hour tabletop exercise with fifteen players from global business, government and public health, consisting of pre-recorded news broadcasts, live staff briefings and moderated discussions.[^43]

The exercise scenario was a novel zoonotic coronavirus transmitted from bats to pigs to people, modeled largely on SARS, that begins on pig farms in Brazil, is exported by air travel to Portugal, the United States and China, and ends at the 18-month point with 65 million deaths; in the scenario there is no possibility of a vaccine in the first year.[^44] The players listed by the center include [[Avril Haines]], [[George Gao]] of the Chinese Center for Disease Control, [[Christopher Elias]] of the Gates Foundation and [[Stephen Redd]] of the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention.[^45] The [[World Economic Forum]] announced the exercise on October 15, 2019 as a simulation for business, government, security and public health leaders, with a live virtual stream from 8:50 to 12:30 Eastern time; it listed Haines as a former deputy director of the Central Intelligence Agency and former deputy national security adviser, and quoted [[Tom Inglesby]], director of the Center for Health Security.[^46]

### Guatemala experiments litigation

The [[United States Public Health Service]] ran sexually transmitted disease experiments on prisoners, soldiers and psychiatric patients in [[Guatemala]] from 1946 to 1948. The Fourth Circuit's opinion records that the Surgeon General, [[Thomas Parran]], approved the proposal in 1946, that a grant including $110,450 for the [[Pan American Sanitary Bureau]] was approved, that Parran simultaneously served on the Board of Trustees of the [[Rockefeller Foundation|The Rockefeller Foundation]], and that a junior Public Health Service officer, [[John C. Cutler]], and his research team began infecting test subjects in February 1947. The opinion states that from May 1947 to October 1948 the researchers conducted thirty-two experiments using gonorrhea, seventeen using syphilis and one using chancroid, which involved a total of 1,308 people.[^47]

On April 1, 2015, the Estate of Arturo Giron Alvarez and other plaintiffs sued Johns Hopkins, The Rockefeller Foundation and [[Bristol-Myers Squibb]] under the Alien Tort Statute and the Guatemalan Civil Code. The complaint was filed in Baltimore City Circuit Court and removed to the District of Maryland the same day, and the federal caption is Estate of Arturo Giron Alvarez v. The Johns Hopkins University, No. 1:15-cv-00950.[^47][^48]

The operative complaint alleged that researchers at Johns Hopkins and the Rockefeller Foundation, "who spent their careers studying syphilis," "intentionally chose to conduct nontherapeutic, nonconsensual experiments" because doing so allowed them to identify a large pool of uninfected people and infect them.[^47] Bristol-Myers Squibb was voluntarily dismissed. In 2017 the district court dismissed all claims under the Guatemalan Civil Code, dismissed the Alien Tort Statute claims based on direct liability except a limited claim against the Rockefeller Foundation, and declined to dismiss the claims based on aiding and abetting and conspiracy.[^47]

The district court later granted the defendants' motion for summary judgment. The plaintiffs appealed only the ruling as to the Rockefeller Foundation, and on March 20, 2024, the Fourth Circuit affirmed. The opinion records that the foundation paid the salary of [[Frederick Soper]], an associate director of its International Health Division, until January 1, 1948 while he served at the Pan American Sanitary Bureau, and the court held that Soper was not an agent of the foundation while he directed the Bureau.[^47] The Johns Hopkins claims were not part of that appeal.[^47]

### Henrietta Lacks and the HeLa cell line

The Estate of [[Henrietta Lacks]] filed a complaint against [[Thermo Fisher Scientific]] Inc. in the District of Maryland on October 4, 2021 (No. 1:21-cv-02524). Johns Hopkins is not a party to that case. The complaint alleges that Lacks was admitted to a segregated ward at Johns Hopkins Hospital with a malignant tumor of the cervix, that on February 5, 1951, while she was under anesthesia for radium treatment, a surgeon cut two circular samples of tissue, each about three quarters of an inch across, from her cervix, and that she had not been informed of or consented to the sampling. It alleges that the samples were given to a researcher at the hospital who cultured them into the HeLa cell line, that the cells were distributed to laboratories without payment to Lacks's estate, and that Thermo Fisher Scientific sells HeLa cells and products containing them. The complaint pleads a single count of unjust enrichment.[^49]

The case was terminated on August 1, 2023.[^50] [[Chemistry World]] reported that the family reached a settlement with Thermo Fisher, which had been accused of profiting from HeLa cells derived from Lacks's tumour without her family's approval.[^51]
