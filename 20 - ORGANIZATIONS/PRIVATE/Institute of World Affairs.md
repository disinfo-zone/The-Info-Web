The Institute of World Affairs was a research unit of the [[New School for Social Research]] in New York, founded in 1943 and redesignated the Research Division in 1953.[^1] During the Second World War it served as an unofficial office of information for various federal war offices, and after the war it continued to produce studies commissioned by the [[U.S. Department of State]].[^1]

### Founding and financing

The Institute was modeled on the [[Kiel Institute for the World Economy]], an international research organization studying the world economy, of which [[Adolph Lowe]] and [[Hans Neisser]] had been former members.[^1] Its mission statement set the goal "to perform research on international problems and to serve as a clearing house for information on foreign regions and international relations."[^1]

At its founding the Institute became an independent unit of the New School and took over the collective research projects of the [[Graduate Faculty of Political and Social Science]]. It developed from two divisions within the Graduate Faculty: a [[Study Group on Germany]], whose members included [[Eduard Heimann]], [[Erich Hula]], [[Karl Mayer]], [[Albert Salomon]], [[Kurt Riezler]], [[Leo Strauss]], [[Horace Kallen]] and [[Felix Kaufmann]], and the [[Research Project on Totalitarian Communication]] organized by [[Hans Speier]] and [[Ernst Kris]].[^1] [[Max Ascoli]], an Italian legal philosopher, was involved in the initial planning, fund-raising and faculty research supervision until Lowe became director of research in 1942.[^1]

The founding year is given as 1942 in one description and as 1943 in others.[^2] A folder titled "History of the Institute of World Affairs, 1943" carries the later date.[^1][^3] The inaugural meeting took place on November 17, 1943, when [[Alvin Johnson]] and Lowe gave addresses that were printed as a pamphlet, Studies of the Institute of World Affairs: The Aims and Organization of the Institute of World Affairs (1944).[^4]

Prior funding for 1943 to 1951 is reported at $630,000, coming from [[Independent Aid]], the [[Rockefeller Foundation]], the [[Viking Fund]], the [[Hebrew Technical Institute]], the [[Rosenwald Foundation]] and individual donors.[^1]

### Doris Duke and Independent Aid

The historical note lists Independent Aid first among the Institute's donors for 1943 to 1951.[^1] Independent Aid, Inc. was the first charitable foundation established by [[Doris Duke]], daughter of [[James B. Duke]]. It was incorporated in Delaware in October 1934, shortly after Duke turned 21, and its officers voted in October 1951 to change its name to the Doris Duke Foundation.[^5][^6] The Doris Duke Charitable Foundation gives the age at which Duke formed her first charitable foundation as 23.[^7]

The [[Duke University]] guide to the Independent Aid records states that Duke "had limited direct involvement" with Independent Aid and that its disbursements reflected her interests in the welfare of women and children, social work and mental health, and early family planning; recipients it names include the Center for Self-Supporting Women and Students of New York City, the Mental Hygiene Association and the Child Adoption and Placement Bureau.[^5] Day-to-day operations were administered by Marian Paschal, secretary until her death in 1946, and by her successor Georgea T. Furst; the Doris Duke Foundation guide records that board member [[Clarence E. Pickett]] served as executive secretary during the same period and that Furst had been his assistant from 1943 to 1946.[^5][^6]

The Doris Duke Foundation records, which continue the Independent Aid files, hold two folders titled for the New School: "New School Refugee Fund, 1940-1946" and "New School, 1952-1959," described as containing correspondence with Independent Aid and, in the second, clippings.[^6] The folder titles name the New School and do not name the Institute.

[[Daniel Bessner]], in his 2013 Duke University dissertation on Speier, describes the Institute as "a research center funded by Doris Duke."[^8]

The Doris Duke Charitable Foundation dates "Service for the U.S. Government during WWII, 1944-1945" in a photograph caption that names no agency.[^7]

### Directors and staff

Adolph Lowe, born in Stuttgart on March 4, 1893, had been director of research and educational studies at the [[Institute of World Economics]] at Kiel from 1926 to 1930 and founded the Institute for Business Cycle Research there in 1925. He was dismissed from his post in 1933, was a Rockefeller Foundation fellow from 1933 to 1938 while a lecturer at the [[University of Manchester]], became a British subject in September 1939, and moved to the New School afterward.[^9] The historical note has him become director of research in 1942.[^1] He directed the Institute in October 1943, when Salomon asked Speier to join its council.[^10] A resignation letter from Lowe dated 1951 is filed in the correspondence series; the historical note gives 1953 for his departure from the Institute, and the Albany finding aid to his papers records that he remained a professor at the New School until 1978, "leaving only to take a one-year appointment at the Hebrew University in Jerusalem (1953)."[^1][^9][^11] He edited the posthumous works of [[Karl Mannheim]].[^2] He returned to Germany after 1982 and died in Wolfenbüttel on June 4, 1995.[^9]

[[Hans Staudinger]], born in Worms in 1889, was a member of the [[Social Democratic Party of Germany]] in the [[Reichstag]] from 1927 to 1933 and Prussian state secretary in the Ministry of Commerce in 1929, was removed from office in 1932, was held in police custody in Hamburg from June 16 to July 22, 1933, and emigrated to Belgium, where he was a consultant to [[SOFINA]] in Brussels. He became professor of economics at the [[University in Exile]] in the fall of 1934 and a naturalized U.S. citizen on September 26, 1940.[^12] The Council of Research that he chaired oversaw the Institute's research operations and is dated 1944 to 1951 in the New School's folder and historical note.[^1][^13] His papers chronology gives 1943 to 1953 for his chairmanship of the Council for Research of the Institute of World Affairs, "Director from 1951-1953," and 1951 to 1953 as chairman of the Institute. The same chronology records his service as dean of the Graduate Faculty in 1941 to 1943, 1950 to 1951 and 1955 to 1960, and an entry of September 29, 1944: "received notification of termination of appointment as 'Expert' from the U. S. War Department" ([[United States Department of War]]).[^12] The historical note states that in 1951 the Institute "fell under the direction of Hans Staudinger" and that he "had a hand in the administration of at least forty-five distinct projects, bringing in $500,000 in funds."[^1] He edited the Journal of Social Research from 1959 to 1964, became professor emeritus in 1960 and died in Manhattan on February 25, 1980.[^12]

Neisser served as acting director from 1950, and [[Hans Simons]] took over the post in 1951.[^1] The Albany finding aid to the Simons papers describes him as a German-born political scholar who came to the United States in 1935 after being blacklisted by the Nazis, joined the University in Exile faculty, became an American citizen in 1940, and "was appointed president of The New School in 1945."[^14] Bessner, writing of October 1946, calls [[Bryn J. Hovde]] "the New School's president."[^15]

Researchers associated with the University in Exile included [[Arnold Brecht]], [[Alfred Kähler]], [[Ernest Hamburger]], Erich Hula, Kurt Riezler and [[Frieda Wunderlich]].[^1] The [[Ibero-American Center]] was an independent division of the Institute headed by [[Frederick Haussmann]].[^1] [[Lewis Lorwin]], a sociologist and economist, was engaged as a research consultant to the New School during the Institute's final years.[^1]

Eduard Heimann was a member of the Study Group on Germany.[^1] A research file titled "Labor Economics in Totalitarian Russia" by [[Jacob Marschak]], dated to the 1940s and 1950s, is held in the Institute's research project records.[^16]

### Speier conflict, 1943 to 1944

In October 1943 Salomon asked Speier to join the Institute's council. Speier told Salomon and Lowe, who directed the Institute, that he "would like to be helpful, if I am wanted," and asked about "the functions of the Council." Despite "repeated inquiries," Speier never heard back from either man about his duties.[^10]

From Washington, where he worked for the [[Foreign Broadcast Intelligence Service]], Speier wrote to Lowe in February 1944 that his experiences with the Institute had been "a series of baffling disappointments." Lowe replied that Speier's anger "came to me as a complete shock," and said he had wanted to include Speier on the council because he was "the main representative of collective research in our sociology department."[^10]

On February 25, 1944 Speier wrote to Lowe: "I withdraw my declaration to serve on [the Institute's council] 'in principle' pending further information. ... In the meantime I must ask you not to consider me a member of the Council." He added that if there were "fights" in the faculty "for prestige or influence I don't want to have any part in them as a pawn."[^10] Bessner states that Speier came to believe he was being "used as a pawn in ridiculous faculty politics," citing letters to Staudinger of March 3, 1944 and to Salomon of March 5, 1944.[^10] In the letter to Salomon he called the council's members the Geheimräte (secret councilors).[^10] In a letter to Riezler of February 3, 1944 he wrote that the New School's "atmosphere of freedom has been replaced by strangely autocratic ways."[^10]

### Research programs

The early research papers included "International Trade in Raw Materials at Various Levels of Employment," "Germany's Position in European Postwar Reconstruction" by Brecht, Lowe, Hans Simons and Staudinger, "Technological Trends and the Flexibility of Labor" by Kähler, "Changes in the Democratic Process" led by [[Paul van Zeeland]], and "Comparative Administration" by Brecht.[^1]

The reconstruction study was "funded by the Rockefeller Foundation in order to study the results of post-World War I reconstruction in Germany, with an eye toward the practical implementation of these lessons following World War II."[^17] The Former Jewish Communal Property in Germany survey was compiled and analyzed by the [[American Federation of Jews from Central Europe]].[^18] The Social and Economic Controls in Germany and Russia project, formed in 1941 and financed by the Rockefeller Foundation, "focused largely on labor politics in Nazi Germany and Soviet Russia."[^19]

The Research Project on Totalitarian Communication, led by Speier and Kris for the Graduate Faculty's research center, ran from 1940 to 1944 and was funded by the Rockefeller Foundation.[^20] Bessner dates the end of the project to January 1943.[^21] The [[Propaganda Research Center]], 1947 to 1953, had as principal researchers [[Karl Ettinger]] and [[Saul K. Padover]], and much of its research was performed with assistance from the [[Office of Naval Research]].[^22] Its project files for 1951 to 1953 are titled Brazilian Use of INP Press Releases (1952-1953), Communist Deviations (1952), Evaluation of Filmslide Use in a Field Operation (1952), Foreign Propaganda in the United States (1951-1953), Foreign Repercussions of Domestic Race Relations (1951), Graphology in Political Intelligence (1952), Human Carriers of Communist Propaganda (1951-1952), Legal Problems Resulting from Warfare Disasters (1952), Patterns of Panic (1951-1952) and Testing State Department Movies (1952-1953).[^23] The administrative series holds materials for an April 18 and 19, 1952 conference titled Conference on International Implications of Race Relations in the United States.[^24]

The Leisure-Time project ran from 1948 until 1953 and attempted to analyze the role of leisure time activity in the daily lives of workers in industrial society. Faculty and researchers involved included [[Marie Macario Bestul]], [[Phyllis Poses]], [[Gerda Golder]], [[Elizabeth Todd]], Staudinger, [[Paul Heurath]], [[Siegfried Kracauer]], [[Hilda Weiss]], [[Alexander Ringer]] and [[Laura Himovitch]].[^1] Ringer's thesis, Musical Attitudes of Metropolitan College Students, drew on questionnaires issued to students of the [[City College of New York]].[^1]

The Image of America project, begun in 1945 and filed for 1947 to 1957, aimed to assess the conception of the American in a global context. It included a paper by Kracauer, "Analysis of Images," and Lorwin described it as "a total loss."[^25] The Viking Fund corresponded with Staudinger about the project and about the Leisure-Time study.[^26]

The Ibero-American Center roundtable of 1944 to 1948 drew correspondence among [[Fernando de los Rios]], [[Adolfo Dorfman]], Bryn J. Hovde, Alvin Johnson, Lowe, Staudinger and [[Sumner Welles]].[^27] A study of religion in postwar Germany, initiated and led by [[Carl Mayer]], ran from 1954 to 1957 with [[Helmut R. Wagner]], [[Thomas Luckmann]] and [[Salvator Attanasio]] on the project staff.[^28]

### Government contracts and grants

During and after the war, the Institute and its researchers "worked closely with U.S. intelligence and war offices during World War II, a relationship that continued after the war had ended."[^29] Correspondence and conference minutes for 1946 to 1951 document the relationship with the State Department.[^29] The administrative series also holds a file on the 1950 [[Point Four Roundtable Conference]] on United States foreign policy.[^30]

In 1953 the Institute took on a study of the readability of European wireless files prepared for overseas distribution by the [[International Press Service]], a study contracted and funded by the newly created [[United States Information Agency]].[^31] General correspondence for 1942 to 1946 includes the [[Coordinator of Inter-American Affairs]] alongside the [[Woodrow Wilson Foundation]], the [[United Nations]] and the [[Carnegie Endowment for International Peace]].[^32]

The Rockefeller Foundation granted Speier and Kris $15,960 in March 1941 and an additional $19,000 in 1942 for the Totalitarian Communication project.[^21] The correspondence file for [[Joseph H. Willits]] of the Rockefeller Foundation concerns a Foundation grant for the project of Neisser titled Domestic Determinants of International Trade.[^33] A 1942 study of German academic institutions by Kris drew correspondence about the inability to secure funding for the research from the [[Carnegie Corporation]].[^34]

The Viking Fund, 1940 to 1950, and its successor the [[Wenner-Gren Foundation]], 1946 to 1955, both appear in the correspondence series.[^26] The [[Ford Foundation]] (1950 to 1955), the [[Fels Fund]] (1948) and the [[Twentieth Century Fund]] (1947) have folders of their own.[^35] A folder titled for [[Nelson Rockefeller]] for 1948 to 1949 concerns the rejection of a transportation study by Staudinger and Wohl.[^36]

### Publications

Books published primarily by [[Cornell University Press]] include [[Gregory Bienstock]], [[Solomon M. Schwarz]] and [[Aaron Yugow]], Management in Russian Industry and Agriculture (1944); Ernst Kris and Hans Speier, German Radio Propaganda (1944); [[Ernst Fraenkel]], Military Occupation and the Rule of Law (1944); Arnold Brecht, Federalism and Regionalism in Germany: The Division of Prussia (1945); [[Joseph B. Schechtman]], European Population Transfers, 1939-1945 (1946); [[Henry W. Ehrmann]], French Labor from Popular Front to Liberation (1947); Alfred Kähler and Ernest Hamburger, Education for an Industrial Age (1948); [[Wilbert E. Moore]], Industrialization and Labor (1951); Hans Neisser and [[Franco Modigliani]], National Incomes and International Trade (1953); and [[Julius Hirsch]], New Horizons in Business (1955).[^2] Lowe edited the posthumous works of Karl Mannheim.[^2]

The occasional papers were distributed directly by the Research Division. They include Alvin Johnson and Ernest Hamburger, "The Economic Problem of Germany" (1946); Hans Neisser, "The Significance of Foreign Trade for Domestic Employment" (1946); Franco Modigliani, "Fluctuations in the Savings Ratio: A Problem in Economic Forecasting" (1947); Wilbert E. Moore, "Primitives and Peasants in Industry" (1948); Arnold Brecht, "The New German Constitution" (1949); [[Henry G. Aubrey]], "Deliberate Industrialization" (1949); Helmut R. Wagner, "A New Generation of German Labor" (1956).[^2]

### End of the Institute

By 1953 the Institute "had been brought into closer relationship with the New School for Social Research, and redesignated as the Research Division." It last appeared in the 1954 to 1955 Graduate Faculty Bulletin. The records came to the New School Archives from the [[Raymond Fogelman Library]] in 2012.[^1][^2]

Lorwin, engaged as a consultant during this period, wrote a 1953 report on the New School's research and related activities that described the Institute as having been "started with too large expectations." The research agenda's ambition and its collaborative method "led to numerous incomplete and under-performing research projects that resulted in few publications or other deliverables."[^1]
