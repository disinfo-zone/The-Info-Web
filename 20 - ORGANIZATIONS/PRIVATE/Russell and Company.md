Russell and Company was an American commission house at [[Canton]], [[China]], formed by [[Samuel Russell]] of [[Middletown, Connecticut]]. [[W. C. Hunter]], a partner of the house, wrote that it "confined itself strictly to agency business."[^1] The [[Baker Library]] at [[Harvard Business School]] describes it as "the largest American commission house in China" until its demise in 1891.[^2] Its Canton correspondence includes the Turkish opium trade, and from 1830 the house kept the ship Lintin at anchor as its receiving ship in the opium anchorage off Canton.[^3][^4]

### Founding and partners

The [[Library of Congress]] finding aid for the firm's papers describes it as a "Trading house founded in 1819 by Samuel Russell" in Canton.[^3] Hunter, a partner from 1837 to 1842 whose 1882 book describes the Canton factories, dates the house to January 1, 1824, as the successor of Samuel Russell & Co., which he says existed from December 26, 1818 to December 26, 1823, and records that the Chinese knew the house as Kee-Chang-Hong.[^1] The National Historic Landmark nomination for Russell's Middletown house, prepared by the [[National Park Service]], places his arrival in Canton in 1819 under arrangements made by Edward Carrington and Providence merchants, and dates the commission house in partnership with [[Philip Ammidon]] to 1824.[^5] It adds that the first Canton venture returned about $15,000 to each of five partners in 1823.[^5]

The National Register of Historic Places nomination for the Russell Company Upper Mill in Middletown says that Russell "established his own trading station in Canton in 1818."[^6] In the National Historic Landmark nomination the Providence contract of 1819 and the voyage of the ship Fame, which reached Macao in September of that year, began the venture.[^5]

From January 1, 1824, until the middle of 1830 the sole partners were Russell and Ammidon.[^1] In September 1829 [[William H. Low]] arrived from [[Salem, Massachusetts]], and in November 1830 [[Augustine Heard]] arrived from [[Boston]] in the bark Lintin under Captain R. B. Forbes. Both became partners. Low left Canton in ill health in 1833 and died at the [[Cape of Good Hope]]. Hunter lists the 1834 to 1836 admissions as [[John Cleve Green]], special agent at Canton for N. L. and G. Griswold of [[New York]], [[John Murray Forbes]], who had arrived in the Lintin in 1830 to join the office, and [[Joseph Coolidge]], who arrived in 1832. Heard retired in the same term.[^1] John Murray Forbes's own notes say that he reached China in 1830, was put in by Cushing as the youngest clerk, and was privately promised admission to the firm on January 1, 1834.[^7] The nomination states that Ammidon, unable to return to Canton, was terminated from the partnership after Low and Russell accepted Heard on a recommendation in 1830.[^5]

Forbes's memoir names the first formers of the house as Samuel Russell and Philip Ammidon: "which was first formed by Messrs. Samuel Russell and Philip Ammidon, and after a few years was changed to the present firm of Russell & Co."[^8]

In 1837 [[Abiel Abbot Low]], a nephew of William H. Low, and Hunter were admitted, and [[Edward King]] became a partner in July of that year. [[Robert Bennet Forbes]], the elder brother of John Murray Forbes, arrived in the Bashaw in October 1838 and was admitted on January 1, 1839, becoming chief of the house.[^1] Hunter gives his departure for New York in the Niantic on July 7, 1840.[^1] The Forbes brothers were nephews of [[Thomas Handasyd Perkins]] of the [[Perkins family]], who headed the Boston house of J. and T. H. Perkins, and Robert Bennet Forbes's plans for the Lintin station in 1829 and 1830 ran through arrangements with the Perkins family and the [[Bryant and Sturgis]] house.[^9]

[[Warren Delano Jr.]] joined in the term beginning in 1840 and succeeded Forbes as chief. Hunter describes him as formerly of the house of [[Russell, Sturgis and Company]] of Canton and [[Manila]].[^1] The [[Franklin D. Roosevelt Presidential Library]] catalogues him as Warren Delano II (1809 to 1898), the maternal grandfather of [[Franklin D. Roosevelt]], and dates his partnership to January 1840.[^10] [[Russell Sturgis]] became a partner on January 1, 1842.[^1]

Forbes's own memoir gives his partnership in Russell and Company as running from January 1, 1840 to January 1, 1844, and again from January 1, 1849 to January 1, 1857, and says that he held the vice-consulate while in China on his last visit.[^11] Hunter's list has him admitted in 1839 and leaving for New York in July 1840.[^1]

### Samuel Russell of Middletown

The nomination gives Samuel Wadsworth Russell's dates as 1789 to 1862 and his ancestry as a line from William Russell (1612 to 1664), a founder of the Saybrook and New Haven colonies, through the Rev. [[Noadiah Russell]] (1659 to 1713), second pastor of the Congregational church at Middletown from 1698 and one of the founders of [[Yale University]], and his eldest son the Rev. William Russell (born 1690). It names his grandfather as Captain Samuel Russell (born 1727) and his father as Captain John Russell (1765 to 1801), who died insolvent.[^5] His middle name came from a great uncle, [[Jeremiah Wadsworth]] of Hartford, Commissary General of the Continental Army, who had married the Rev. William Russell's daughter Mehitable.[^5] The nomination records that Russell was twelve when his father died, that he was an apprentice clerk to the Middletown merchants Whittlesley and Alsop, and that after a voyage that ended in 1813 he acted as Middletown commission merchant for Hull and Griswold, and that the first Russell and Company was founded in a building he had leased there.[^5]

The Library of Congress finding aid gives his dates as "1789-approximately 1862."[^3] Houqua's letter of January 14, 1837, held in the Library of Congress collection and quoted in the nomination, says that Russell's name had been associated with the house "during the whole course of its existence" and expresses his "sincere wish that you may live long to enjoy the fruit of your industry."[^12]

The nomination dates his withdrawal from the Canton house to 1836, with his profits reinvested in industry, banking and western railroad stock, and gives his estate at death in 1862 as $630,000, left to his widow and his sole surviving son, Samuel Wadsworth Russell Jr.[^5] Hunter records that Russell and John Perkins Cushing took their final leave of Canton in the Bashaw on February 27, 1831; the nomination says both returned to America upon completing their work in March 1831.[^5][^13] The house at Middletown, designed by [[Ithiel Town]] in 1827 to 1830, passed to [[Wesleyan University]] in 1937 by the gift of his great-grandson Thomas MacDonough Russell Jr.[^5]

The Yale biographical sketches record that the Rev. William Russell of Middletown (Yale tutor, minister 1715 to 1761), the eldest child of the Rev. Noadiah Russell and Mary Hamlin, married Mary Pierpont in 1719 and had four sons and five daughters, of whom the Rev. William Russell (Yale 1745) and the Rev. Noadiah Russell (Yale 1750) and one other son survived him.[^14] [[Matthew Talcott Russell]] was the second son of that Noadiah Russell of Thompson, Connecticut, born March 19, 1761, a lawyer at Middletown, and the father of [[William Huntington Russell]], a co-founder of the [[Skull and Bones]] society, who was graduated from Yale in 1833.[^15][^16] The nomination says that Wadsworth had been apprenticed to his uncle Matthew Talcott of Middletown, and the Yale sketch records that Matthew Talcott Russell was named for Colonel Matthew Talcott of Middletown, who had married his aunt and paid for his education.[^5][^15] The sketch of the Rev. William Russell gives the Rev. Noadiah Russell (Yale 1750) as his son and the father of Matthew Talcott Russell; the nomination gives the Rev. William Russell's daughter Mehitable as the wife of Jeremiah Wadsworth, Samuel Russell's great uncle, and names Samuel Russell's grandfather as Captain Samuel Russell (born 1727).[^5][^14][^15]

The Hamlin family genealogy of 1900 records Samuel Russell, born at Middletown on August 25, 1789, as the son of Capt. John Russell (born August 19, 1765, died December 7, 1801), who was the son of Capt. Samuel Russell (born July 7, 1727), a brother of the Rev. Noadiah Russell (Yale 1750). John Russell and Matthew Talcott Russell were first cousins, and Samuel Russell and William Huntington Russell were second cousins, with William Russell, the son of the Yale founder Noadiah Russell, as their common great-grandfather.[^17] [[Kris Millegan]] calls William Huntington Russell Samuel Russell's cousin in his opening article and at pages 151 to 152, his second cousin at page 313, and, at page 52, calls Samuel Russell his uncle.[^18]

### The 1831 merger with Perkins and Company

The Library of Congress finding aid states that in 1831 the China trading operations of [[John P. Cushing]], [[William Perkins and Company]] of Boston, and [[Houqua]], the Canton merchant, merged with Russell and Company.[^3] The Baker Library guide to the firm's records gives the same event from the Boston side: Cushing, who had been the sole agent in Canton for Perkins and Company, arranged to dissolve the Boston house by merging its Canton business into Russell and Company in 1831.[^2]

Hunter's account is dated by deaths. Thomas T. Forbes, who had succeeded Cushing at Perkins and Company in 1828, drowned in a typhoon on August 9, 1829, with S. H. Monson, the bookkeeper of Russell and Company. Among Forbes's papers was a sealed letter to Russell and Company asking them, in case of accident, to take charge of the local business of his own firm and of J. and T. H. Perkins. They did so, "and naturally came with it that of Houqua, who had been the intimate personal friend of Mr. Cushing during his unbroken residence of over twenty years in Canton."[^19] Cushing, who was in England when the news arrived, returned to Canton in the Bashaw in August 1830, and arrangements "were then definitely concluded by which Russell & Co. were officially appointed sole agents for the Boston house, that of Perkins & Co. being wound up, while the important foreign business of Houqua was then also definitely secured to them."[^19]

The nomination dates the death of Forbes, the Perkins heir who was to have taken control of the firm, to 1827.[^5]

The nomination describes the reorganization as one directed by Cushing: Heard was made the representative of Perkins and their associated Boston firms, Robert Bennet Forbes was given charge of the Lintin station storeship business, and John Murray Forbes was placed with the firm as an assistant in line for partnership.[^5] The editor of John Murray Forbes's letters adds that Cushing, returning from Europe, went overland to Canton to look after the Perkins business and merged it into Russell and Company before the arrival in China of Forbes's father and uncle.[^20] The Baker Library holds the Perkins and Company letter books from Canton that precede the merger, and the Library of Congress finding aid says that more Russell and Company business records are in the William Perkins and Company records at Baker Library.[^2][^3]

The Perkins house at Canton had been run by Ephraim Bumstead, who went out as supercargo of the ship Thomas Russell after 1798, and then by John Perkins Cushing, who went out as a clerk at sixteen in 1803; Cary has Cushing in copartnership until "the dissolution of the house in 1827," and Hunter has him at Canton until 1828.[^21][^22] A George C. Perkins of Boston, bookkeeper of Russell & Co., is named by Hunter among the men drowned in the Lantao Channel, and Forbes's memoir records the death of a Mr. George Perkins in 1854 and the retirement of Edward King and Mr. Perkins from the house in 1849.[^22][^23]

### Turkish opium and the Indian trade

The Library of Congress finding aid says that the correspondence after 1818 contains much information on the daily operation of the Canton company and on the Turkish opium trade, along with the Amsterdam commodity exchanges.[^3] Its subject index includes an entry for the opium trade with Turkey, and among the firms represented in the correspondence it lists [[Clarke and Company]], of Smyrna, [[Turkey]].[^3] The box list includes account books for 1816 to 1829 that carry a manuscript on the Chinese method of preparing opium for smoking.[^24]

The nomination's account of the trade is longer. It states that Americans, largely excluded from the Indian and Persian markets, were for a time restricted to Turkish opium transshipped through American ports, that the Perkins brothers of Boston had been associated with the Turkish opium trade since the 1780s and organized a Smyrna merchant house in 1816, and that there is no evidence that Russell took part in the opium trade during his first Canton venture.[^5] It states that by the time Ammidon left, his dealings "had made Russell & Company one of the most important American sellers of the opium in Canton, second or third in the trade behind the British firms of Jardine Matheson & Company and Dent & Company," that during the first Opium War the firm "managed to hold its position in the Turkish trade and became the third largest purveyor of the drug from India," and that by 1842 it was the largest and strongest American house in China.[^5]

The nomination adds that during Russell's senior partnership the firm sold opium for Perkins and Company on their storeship, that John Perkins Cushing began in 1818 to turn his commission business over to Russell and Company and other Canton houses, which included his opium shipments, and that in 1827 Russell solicited consignment business including "most notably the sale of opium for John Jacob Astor."[^5] Forbes writes that Russell and Company "had for some time done business at Canton previous to Mr. Cushing's leaving, and, in fact, had transacted a part of his business."[^25]

Robert Bennet Forbes's memoir describes a 1828 voyage to Smyrna in the Danube, a vessel fitted out at New York by [[Sturgis and Perkins]]. He states the object plainly: "the principal object being to procure Turkey opium for China."[^26] A letter of December 27, 1796 from J. and T. H. Perkins to Mr. George Perkins at Smyrna asks about the market there for coffee and sugar, and orders of May 24, 1800 for the Perkins ship Thomas Russell direct her to Malaga, the Mediterranean including Smyrna, and Calcutta.[^27] He carried a credit on the [[Baring Brothers]] of about £60,000 and a cargo of coffee and specie. At Smyrna the principal American merchant was [[Joseph Langdon]]. Forbes says that he sailed from [[Vourla]] under the convoy of the United States schooner Porpoise through the Archipelago and then went on to [[Gibraltar]] to await operations on a larger scale.[^28] On the way up the Mediterranean he met the brig Leander, of Salem, bound home from Smyrna, and he suspected from her that her owner, [[Joseph Peabody]], who held an interest in the China and [[India]] trade, had been on the same errand.[^28]

The Indian side of the trade ran through the store-ships anchored at [[Lintin]]. Forbes writes that the store-ships lay outside at Lintin and that the drug was run up the river in small boats, sometimes carried by the mandarin boats sent to protect the revenue.[^29] He gives the annual value of the trade as ten or twelve million dollars and says that the officials encouraged its introduction "from India and Turkey, not only by levying a duty on it for their own benefit, but by consuming it."[^29] Hunter says the Lintin was Russell and Company's "receiving ship" from 1830, and that she held her anchorage for nine years.[^4] Robert Bennet Forbes sought the command of the Lintin station ship in 1829 and 1830. The station ship was then the Tartar of Bryant and Sturgis, commanded by a relative of Mr. Sturgis.[^9] Forbes wrote that from January 1 to the closing of the port the commission account and the gains of the Lintin "showed a grand result."[^30]

### The 1839 surrender at Canton

In March 1839 the imperial commissioner [[Lin Zexu]] demanded the opium held by the foreign community at Canton. The [[Chinese Repository]], published at Canton, printed the sequence. On March 21 the General Chamber of Commerce, whose chairman was [[William S. Wetmore]], resolved in a letter to the Hong merchants that there was "an almost unanimous feeling in the community of the absolute necessity of the foreign residents of Canton having no connexion with the opium traffic," and that night the Hong merchants reported that Lin would be at the Consoo house at ten the next morning unless opium was delivered.[^31] An edict of April 1 addressed the chairman of the Chamber and called on the American consul to surrender a quantity of opium not less than the British superintendent had given up.[^31] A later edict from Lin recorded that the American consul [[P. W. Snow]] had declared that "the merchants of his country, acting in obedience to the new regulations, will not presume to traffic in opium," and objected that Snow's added remark about ordering offending ships home did not accord with conforming to the law.[^32]

[[Charles Elliot]], the British superintendent of trade, notified Lin on March 28, 1839 that he held himself responsible for delivering 20,283 chests of opium owned by British subjects, and that as the chests came into his possession each would be delivered up.[^33] Forbes's memoir records that Elliot called on British subjects and on all who held British-owned opium to surrender it in the name of [[Queen Victoria]], and that schedules were made chest by chest for delivery to Lin's officers.[^34] Hunter gives the count: by May 2, 15,501 chests had been handed over, and the whole quantity, 20,283 chests, was completed on May 21.[^35]

In the committee of the General Chamber of Commerce that met in March 1839 on the bond proposed by the cohong, Warren Delano Jr. moved a resolution, seconded by H. Rustomjee and carried unanimously, that the committee should not enter further correspondence of a political or personal nature with the Chinese authorities, "inasmuch as we are prisoners in our factories, surrounded by an armed force, our trade stopped."[^36]

Hunter wrote that the Americans had not delivered any American-owned opium, "of which we held at the time of surrender about fifty cases of Turkey."[^37] He adds: "The English on leaving placed theirs in charge of the American houses. A large share of it fell under the control of Russell & Co."[^37] He describes a discussion among the Canton agents about charging half-commissions on consignments withdrawn from the trade, and says the charge was dropped on the quantity Russell and Company delivered up, which he gives as nearly 15,000 dollars.[^38] Forbes wrote that the American hong housed the United States consul, P. W. Snow, together with Russell and Company and Russell, Sturgis and Company, and that the foreign community was kept under strict guard while the delivery went on.[^34]

### The memorial of the American merchants

A memorial dated at Canton on May 25, 1839 was printed by the [[United States House of Representatives]] as House Document 40 of the 26th Congress, first session, dated January 9, 1840 and referred to the [[United States House Committee on Foreign Affairs]]. The caption describes it as the memorial of R. B. Forbes and others, American merchants at Canton, for a commercial agent to be sent to China to negotiate a commercial treaty, with a naval force to protect the persons and property of Americans. It is signed by Russell Sturgis, W. Delano Jr., Gideon Nye Jr., R. B. Forbes, A. A. Low, Edward King, S. B. Rawle, and Jas. Ryan.[^39]

The memorial states that the signers were "made prisoners in our factories" on March 22, that the commissioner had obtained "upwards of twenty thousand chests of British owned opium," valued at more than ten million dollars, and that the signers had been threatened over a bond under which a ship found bringing opium would be given up for punishment by death.[^39] It gives the opium sales at Canton by season: 3,210 chests for $3,657,000 in 1816 to 1817, 5,822 chests in 1822 to 1823, 9,535 chests in 1827 to 1828, and 23,670 chests for $15,332,759 in 1832 to 1833. It states that the receiving vessels moved from Whampoa to Lintin about 1821, that the Governor and the Hoppo had boats that received opium at Lintin, and that the officials took a fee of twenty to seventy dollars a chest.[^39]

The memorial also states: "We have no wish to see a revival of the opium trade," that most of the signers had signed a voluntary pledge to abstain from dealing in the drug, and that the drug had been "productive of much evil and of scarcely a single good to the Chinese." It continues that the signers could not "perceive the slightest ground for justification of the robbery committed upon British subjects here." It asks the government to act, if it thought fit, with Great Britain, France and Holland to obtain six demands: envoys at Peking, a fixed tariff, bonding warehouses, trade at other ports, compensation for the stoppage of trade, and limits on punishments of foreigners until Chinese laws were made known. Failing that, it asks for an agent or commissioner with a naval force to protect American commerce and to prevent any paper blockade. It names Snow as the consul whose despatches to the State Department would confirm the account and expresses "entire approbation" of his conduct.[^39]

On April 25, 1840, as the British blockade was expected, a petition to Lin from American houses asking that their ships be allowed to come directly to Whampoa was signed by Augustine Heard and Company, Gideon Nye Jr., A. A. Ritchie, James Ryan, Henry W. Hubbell, Olyphant and Company, Russell and Company, S. W. Lewis, Henry Fessenden, John Shillaber and Isaac M. Bull.[^40] The Chinese Repository gives Lin's reply as a declaration that it was an "egregious mistake" to think the English contemplated a blockade.[^40]

The treaty between the United States and China was signed at the village of Wanghia (Mong-Ha in Hunter's spelling) at Macao on July 3, 1844, by [[Caleb Cushing]] and [[Qiying]]. Hunter, who dates the signing, described the two signatories as together the end of Old Canton.[^41]

### Opium, Houqua and the Chesapeake in 1840

Hunter records that in 1840 Russell and Company bought the British ship Cambridge, renamed her Chesapeake under the American flag, and loaded her with British goods valued at £150,000 for [[Whampoa]]. On June 22, 1840, as H.M.S. Volage took up a station off [[Chuenpi]], she sailed past it, and she was the last vessel to enter the port.[^42] When the Chinese authorities then sought a large foreign ship to anchor above a raft across the river, they applied to Warren Delano Jr., "the then chief of Russell & Co.," who had never left Canton. The Chesapeake was made over to the mandarins, her American papers were removed, and the officials fitted her out as an auxiliary defence.[^43]

Forbes's account gives the firm a separate position on the opium trade itself. He wrote that between January and July 1840 Russell and Company "controlled considerable operations" for the Canton merchant Houqua, whose shipments were made in the firm's name and were known to the consignees to be for his account. When it became certain that the English government would make stringent demands on China, it was "arranged to buy all this property amounting to millions, and account to him for the returns," so that the shipments could not be seized in England, and the firm took its usual commission.[^44] He also wrote that "Russell & Co. considered it important to keep the pledge made by Mr. Green, and for a long time had nothing to do with opium; certainly not within my time, between 1840 and 1844, when my interest ceased for a time."[^45] Forbes also wrote about the money. He said the price of the surrendered drug was contested, one side arguing that it had cost $400 or $500 a chest and the other that it sold for $250 or $300 at the time of seizure.[^45] He wrote that "most of the smaller holders of scrip were great losers, and that the large agents, who sold mostly on commission, turned their attention to speculation in scrip and in the drug itself, and made large fortunes."[^45] He also wrote that he sent out the schooner Anglona for Russell and Company in early 1841, and that she raced the Ariel from Macao Roads round Lintin.[^46] Hunter, for his part, records that Houqua liked to compare himself with "that famous house" with which, through Russell and Company, he had had years of important business. Hunter's footnote identifies the phrase "Ba-Blo" as Baring Brothers & Co.[^47]

The city of Canton was ransomed in 1841, after Sir [[Hugh Gough]]'s British forces took the heights above it. Hunter gives the ransom as six million dollars, five million of which were paid on the 31st, and the Hong merchants contributed two million dollars toward it, of which Houqua gave 1,100,000.[^48] For Houqua's share, Captain Elliot received three promissory notes drawn by Russell and Company at thirty days in favour of Houqua, who endorsed them to Elliot's order. The notes, for 400,000, 400,000 and 300,000 dollars, were deposited for collection with [[Jardine, Matheson and Company]] and [[Dent and Company]], and Russell and Company had moved its own office to Macao.[^47] Hunter writes: "At this time the last-named house held about two and a half millions of dollars belonging to their constituent, the endorser." The sentence follows his statement that the offices of Jardine, Matheson and Company, Dent and Company and Russell and Company had been removed to Macao, and he says the notes were cashed, less a discount of a little under a thousand dollars.[^47]

### Railroads and the partners' American institutions

Several of the partners turned to American railroads and institutions in the next decades. The editor of John Murray Forbes's letters, his daughter [[Sarah Forbes Hughes]], writes that for the ten years before 1856 his business in ships and merchandise had been "gradually decreasing," that his work was "mainly devoted to the Michigan Central," and to the enterprise already, in 1856, known by its permanent title of the Chicago, Burlington and Quincy Railroad, and that the ocean telegraph "was soon to do away with the old-fashioned China merchant."[^49] She quotes his notes that he was "undoubtedly very busy with the Michigan Central Railroad, and also with the small roads which then formed the embryo of the Chicago, Burlington and Quincy."[^50] The two roads are the [[Michigan Central Railroad]] and the [[Chicago, Burlington and Quincy Railroad]].

In 1857 the Michigan Central was in trouble. Forbes was called up from [[Naushon Island]] in October and asked to go to [[London]] to raise a loan of two million dollars, which the editor says might have prevented the railroad's bankruptcy.[^51] The letters print a resolution of the directors of the Chicago, Burlington and Quincy Railroad, dated October 18, 1898, which records their sorrow at Forbes's death.[^52] In the second volume he is described as chairman of the board of that road, with his cousin C. E. Perkins managing it.[^53]

Samuel Russell turned to American investment after he returned to Middletown in 1831. The Library of Congress finding aid says that he founded the [[Russell Manufacturing Company]] for elastic webbing and that his real estate speculation reached [[Chicago]], [[Milwaukee]], and land in Michigan, [[Ohio]], [[Kentucky]], and [[New Jersey]].[^54] Of the later partners, the [[Landmarks Preservation Commission]] designation report for the [[Low Memorial Library]] at [[Columbia University]], given by [[Seth Low]] in honor of his father Abiel Abbot Low, says that the elder Low was a prominent tea merchant in the China trade who established an import business in 1849 and was associated with the construction of the Chesapeake and Ohio Railroad and the founding of [[Newport News, Virginia]].[^55] The Trustee minutes of [[Princeton University]] record that John Cleve Green gave $200,000 for the School of Science on September 20, 1872; the John C. Green School of Science was occupied in 1874 and destroyed by fire on November 26 and 27, 1928.[^56] The university's museum reference entry on Green says that by the time President McCosh retired in 1888 Green and his residuary legatees had contributed "upwards of a million and a half, perhaps two million dollars," and describes his China fortune as derived from tea and textiles and, after the end of the East India Company's monopoly, from the opium trade, enhanced by railroad investments.[^57]

### The Delano family

The Franklin D. Roosevelt Library finding aid for the Delano family papers says that Warren Delano II sailed to China in 1833 as supercargo on the Commerce, became associated at Canton with Russell Sturgis and Company, and in January 1840 became a partner in Russell and Company. During the [[Opium War]] he remained in Canton and [[Macao]] and served as acting consul. The panic of 1857 brought severe losses, and in 1859 he returned to China to restore his fortune. His family, including the president's mother, joined him at [[Hong Kong]] in 1862, and he died at his house [[Algonac]] on January 17, 1898.[^10] The finding aid states that the papers include material on the history of Russell and Company's financial interests in China, among them a series of typescript copies of letters to members of the house for 1840 and 1841 and "Houqua Accounts and Memoranda, 1865-1867."[^10]

The [[Delano family]] of [[Fairhaven, Massachusetts]] traced its American line to [[Philippe de Lannoy]], who reached Plymouth in 1621. Warren Delano II was a son of [[Captain Warren Delano]] (1779 to 1866) and the father of [[Sara Delano Roosevelt]], whose son was Franklin D. Roosevelt. A daughter, Deborah Perry (Dora) Delano, married [[William Howell Forbes]] of Hong Kong at Paris in 1867, and another, Annie Lyman Delano, married [[Frederick Delano Hitch]] of Shanghai in 1877.[^58] Forbes's appendix lists a W. Howell Forbes who entered the house in 1860 and remained a partner.[^59]

Forbes's appendix gives the partnership dates of Warren Delano: partner in Russell, Sturgis and Company from December 31, 1835; admitted to Russell and Company on January 1, 1840 and a partner until December 31, 1846; admitted again on January 1, 1861 and finally retired on December 31, 1866.[^60] Hunter records that when the Chinese authorities wanted a large foreign ship in 1840 the application was made to Delano, and that Delano told Forbes that Houqua was disposed to send orders to India for cotton, three ships being despatched with 100,000 pounds remitted.[^61] *The Chinese Repository* of September 1840 records that after the consul Snow embarked for New York "W. Delano, jun. esq. acts as vice-consul."[^62] In December 1841 he arranged with the hong merchants for the release of about forty survivors of the wrecked East India Company steamer Madagascar and asked that no account be published in the Canton newspapers; the commander of the steamer wrote that [[James Matheson]]'s influence "with the American consul and merchants" had secured the result.[^63] Curtis T. Henson's study of the East India Squadron records that in 1842 Delano, as vice-consul, apprised Commodore [[Lawrence Kearny]] of an outrage against American subjects.[^64]

Forbes's memoir records the terms of a later reorganization of the house in 1846, under which "W. Delano was to retire after a time" and [[Edward Delano]] remained a partner.[^65] The memoir adds that "In 1846 Mr. Edward Delano returned home."[^66]

The Hong Kong directory for 1865 lists Russell and Company at Queen's Road with Paul S. Forbes (absent), Warren Delano Jr., William H. Forbes, George Tyson, David O. Clark, Frank B. Forbes, Henry S. Grew, Richard S. Dana, W. H. Foster Jr., E. D. Barbour and Emil Vogel.[^67]

### William Huntington Russell and the Russell name at Yale

[[Franklin Bowditch Dexter]]'s biographical sketches of Yale graduates give Matthew Talcott Russell as the second son of Rev. Noadiah Russell, Yale 1750, born in 1761. He settled as a lawyer in Middletown in 1786, served sixteen years as city treasurer, and in 1797 married Mary, eldest daughter of Rev. [[Enoch Huntington]] of Middletown. The sketch says their children were seven daughters and six sons, and that "The fifth son was graduated here in 1833."[^15] The Yale obituary record gives William Huntington Russell as the son of Matthew Talcott Russell (Yale 1779) and Mary Huntington Russell, born in Middletown on August 12, 1809.[^16]

The 1856 act incorporating the [[Russell Trust Association]], the trust through which Skull and Bones conducts its corporate affairs, names William H. Russell, [[John S. Beach]], [[Henry B. Harrison]], [[Daniel C. Gilman]], [[Henry T. Blake]] and [[Henry D. White]] as its first incorporators.[^68] A letter to the [[Yale Alumni Magazine]] by [[Bruce L. Reynolds]] in 2015 attributed the money for the society's 1856 building to Russell and Company, citing [[James Bradley]]'s book on the China trade.[^69]

### Failure and closing

The Library of Congress holds records dated 1812 to 1894, chiefly from 1819 to 1840, which were given to the library by [[Thomas Macdonough Russell]] in 1931.[^3] The Baker Library records of the firm are dated 1820 to 1891, and the Baker Library's Forbes family business records include papers on the creation of the Shanghai Navigation Company, a steamboat company on the Yangtze River.[^2]

The second volume of the Forbes letters describes the last years of John Murray Forbes, who died on October 12, 1898. The editor, Sarah Forbes Hughes, writes that he was grieved by "the failure of his old house in China, Russell & Co., occasioned by some speculations in steamers and the close competition by German commission merchants." She adds that his friends, the "time-honored" Baring Brothers, had suspended payment, and that these events gave him "an actual physical shock."[^53] A footnote to that passage prints Forbes's letter of 1859 to the managers of the firm, which argued that its commission business alone could yield $200,000 a year net on a capital of $2,000,000, and that speculations of any sort would damage that capital.[^70]

The [[Hong Kong Daily Press]] of July 22, 1891 reported a petition in bankruptcy before the Acting Chief Justice, E. J. Ackroyd. A Canton merchant petitioned "for the adjudication in bankruptcy" of W. H. Forbes, J. M. Forbes, A. W. Pomeroy, Chas. V. Smith and C. A. Tomes, "trading under the style of Russell & Co. at Hongkong and elsewhere." The petitioner's claim was for $1,044, and the court made an order adjudicating all the debtors bankrupts.[^71] The 1889 directory lists William H. Forbes and John M. Forbes (New York) among the partners of the firm at Hong Kong.[^72] The John Murray Forbes who was a partner at Canton in the 1830s and chaired the Chicago, Burlington and Quincy board until 1898 is not the partner of 1891, John Murray Forbes Jr. (1844 to 1921) of the [[Forbes family]].[^73] On September 1, 1891 the Supreme Court of Hong Kong heard an application in a suit by No Chi Kiu and four others against Ng Woon Soon and others, which the report described as "a case arising out of the How Qua property and the failure of Messrs. Russell & Co." Counsel read an affidavit stating that Russell and Company became insolvent in June, that Hong Kong lots formerly registered in the Land Office as the property of John Murray Forbes junior had been registered in the name of a trustee for the creditors of Russell and Company, that an equitable charge by Russell and Company in favour of John Murray Forbes, trustee for How Qua, secured a sum in taels with interest at seven per cent, and that judgments of about $229,000 had been obtained against the partners in the late firm.[^74]

The Chronicle and Directory for China for 1889 gives the partners of "Russell & Co., merchants, Praya" at Hong Kong as William H. Forbes, John M. Forbes (New York), S. W. Pomeroy (London), Charles Vincent Smith (Shanghai), F. D. Bush (New York), G. H. Wheeler (Shanghai), E. H. M. Huntington and C. A. Tomes, and lists the same names at Shanghai with William H. Forbes at Hong Kong and John M. Forbes in New York.[^72] The 1892 volume's chronology of Hong Kong carries the entry "Russell & Co., suspend payment, 1891."[^75]

On December 10, 1891 the Hong Kong Daily Press printed the report of the official assignee on the last examination of Charles Alexander Tomes, a junior partner. It states that Tomes "was adjudicated bankrupt, with William Howell Forbes, the senior partner at the firm," on July 22, 1891; that William Howell Forbes, as senior partner, had admitted him by a letter without any deed of partnership; and that his admission "has never yet received the sanction of John Murray Forbes, another partner of the firm who conducted the business in New York." It adds that all the partners entered into a trust deed on June 9, 1891 for the benefit of their creditors under the laws of New York, with Henry Hannah of New York as trustee.[^76]

The Massachusetts Historical Society's genealogy for the Forbes family papers has one William Howell Forbes (1837 to 1896), a son of Paul Siemen Forbes, who lived in China, and one John Murray Forbes Jr. (1844 to 1921), a brother of Francis Blackwell Forbes, who went to China in 1857 and became a partner in Russell and Company. The John Murray Forbes Jr. folder holds business letters from Boston, New York and China for 1879 to 1906, and the collection holds letters from Francis Blackwell Forbes to William Howell Forbes, John Murray Forbes Jr., S. W. Pomeroy and G. H. Wheeler.[^73]

The [[Hong Kong Telegraph]] of August 31, 1891 printed a letter signed A. B. McKean from the head office of the [[National Bank of China]], Limited, in Hong Kong. It reported that at a statutory meeting of shareholders in London on July 30 "an informal resolution was passed in favor of winding up the Bank," that the bank's articles of association appointed "the late firm of Russell & Co." secretaries of the bank for fifty years on a commission on profits, and that "the failure of Russell & Co. in June, and their consequent inability to carry out their part, cancels this agreement." It stated that "Rumours, absolutely false, have been circulated to the effect that the Bank has sustained heavy losses owing to the failure of Russell & Co." and that counsel's opinion in London was that the resolution of July 30 was not binding on the bank.[^77]
