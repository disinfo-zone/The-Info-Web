Southern Air Transport was incorporated in [[Florida]] on October 31, 1949, as an air carrier flying under a supplemental certificate, and was bought by the [[Central Intelligence Agency]] on August 5, 1960. The company says its own history goes back to 1947 in [[Miami]].[^1][^2]

### CIA ownership, 1960 to 1973

The Senate Select Committee to Study Governmental Operations with Respect to Intelligence Activities (the [[Church Committee]]) reported that the CIA bought Southern Air Transport because in June 1960 the [[U.S. Department of Defense|Department of Defense]] and the [[Civil Aeronautics Board]] changed the rules for [[Military Air Transport Service]] contracts, requiring a supplemental certificate and participation in the [[Civil Reserve Air Fleet]], which [[Air America]] could not obtain. Director of Central Intelligence [[Allen Dulles]] approved the purchase on July 15, 1960. Air America management surveyed the 18 remaining supplemental carriers and chose Southern Air Transport, which operated two C-46s; the ownership was kept completely separate from Air America to avoid public hearings, but the new company was to fly on equipment under conditional sale from Air America and with transferred personnel, and the Committee wrote that it "was actually an integral part of the complex from a management perspective," its management decisions made by the same CIA consultant and advisory team that set Air America policy. The Agency later acquired [[Boeing]] 727s that Boeing modified to enlarge the ventral exit for airdrops.[^1]

By 1972 the Agency decided to sell. The DCI on May 5, 1972 approved selling the equity to current management; the Committee wrote that "none of the employees of SAT had ever been an employee of the Agency." An accounting firm valued the company, the asking price was set at $2.7 million for stock plus $3.2 million to pay off a note to Air America, and a former owner, who is not named in the report, made offers in December 1972 and January 1973. The DCI approved a sale at $5.605 million on January 19, 1973 ($2.145 million for stock, $3.125 million in debt and a $335,000 credit). Competing supplemental carriers lobbied [[Congress]], the [[General Accounting Office]] and the General Services Administration against the sale and one offered about $2 million more; on July 31, 1973 the DCI ordered the company dissolved, then on October 1 agreed to entertain a sale if the buyer obtained prompt financing. The sale closed on December 31, 1973 after a $4.5 million loan commitment from the [[First National Bank of Chicago]] on November 29. In early 1974 Southern Air Transport exercised its option on a [[Lockheed]] L-100-30 Hercules for $3.15 million and resold it to [[Saturn Airways]] for $4.35 million; the CIA read this as a breach of the agreement against windfall profit, an arbitrator ruled for Air America on September 5, 1974, and the Agency received an additional $1,304,243. Employees filed a class action in March 1974 alleging the sale was illegal; the federal court dismissed it with prejudice on July 17, 1974. The Agency received $6.47 million in total.[^1] *The [[The Christian Science Monitor]]* reported in 1986 that the buyer was [[Stanley Williams]], who had run the company for the CIA for 11 years, and that [[Lawrence Houston]], the CIA's general counsel at the time of the sale, said that as far as he knew there was no continuing relationship; a company spokesman, [[William Kress]], said Southern had "no contracts with the CIA, nor have we since they owned the airline prior to 1973."[^3]

### Contra resupply and the Enterprise, 1983 to 1986

[[CBS News]] reported in 1984 that a CIA-sponsored Southern Air flight landed in [[Honduras]] with 22 tons of small arms on April 9, 1983.[^3] The independent counsel's report states that in the second phase of the Contra arms sales, in January and March 1985, [[Richard Secord]] arranged air transport with the help of [[Richard Gadd]], a former Air Force subordinate who enlisted Southern Air Transport, and that the company subcontracted those flights to a second carrier.[^4][^5] In late 1985 the company set up a [[Panama]]nian corporation, [[Amalgamated Commercial Enterprises]], at Gadd's request as a "financial cut-out," which received $1,540,956 in Enterprise funds and paid for two C-7 and one C-123 aircraft.[^6] The Enterprise paid Southern Air Transport $1,935,596 in 1985 and 1986 for contra-related services "including the purchase of a C-123 cargo plane, repair and maintenance of aircraft, sale of spare parts and the supply of cash advances," and $200,000 for a Jetstar used by Secord.[^6] The company disbursed about $467,000 in cash from petty-cash funds for the resupply operation, carried to Central America in amounts up to $50,000.[^7] [[Robert Dutton]]'s mid-1986 inventory of the operation lists "Maintenance Support Miami," where a crew worked closely with Southern Air for procurement and delivery.[^6] On April 11, 1986 a Southern Air L-100 under contract to the private benefactors, which had delivered [[Department of State|State Department]] aid to [[Ilopango Airbase]], dropped arms and supplies to contra forces in southern [[Nicaragua]].[^8]

Southern Air Transport also held the State Department's [[NHAO|Nicaraguan Humanitarian Assistance Office]] contract for $27 million of nonlethal aid, so that the two flows used the same aircraft and sometimes legs of the same trip; *The Christian Science Monitor* wrote in December 1986 that "the two operations are woven so closely together they are sometimes difficult to separate," that the fleet had grown to seven times the three Hercules the company operated in 1983, and that about half its contracts were with the [[Military Airlift Command]].[^9] In early 1986 [[Alan Fiers]], chief of the CIA's Central American Task Force, saw a Southern Air C-130 being loaded at Ilopango on [[Felix Rodriguez]]'s authority, and persuaded North to have Rodriguez cancel the flight.[^10] The notes of [[Donald Gregg]] on an August 8, 1986 meeting with Rodriguez record, concerning a C-123 sitting in Miami, "Southern Air Transport said plane OK to go. Called Jim Steele," the U.S. Military Group commander [[James Steele]].[^11]

### The Hasenfus aircraft and crew

The independent counsel's report records that after the October 5, 1986 shootdown of the aircraft carrying [[Eugene Hasenfus]], news reports linked it to Southern Air Transport, which had in fact contracted to service the resupply network's airplanes, and that Fiers was concerned because the company had been used for NHAO deliveries.[^12] A CIA memorandum of October 10, 1986 on a [[Senate Foreign Relations Committee]] briefing states that the co-pilot [[Wallace Sawyer]] "later worked for Southern Air Transport" and at one point asked to become a CIA staff employee, and that the [[U.S. Customs|Customs Service]] would investigate the company's activity.[^13] *The Christian Science Monitor* reported that the company had serviced the downed plane, that Sawyer had worked for it until April 1986, that it reportedly said it did not own the plane, and that news reports said the plane was bought six months earlier by a California company, [[Four Aces Inc.]], and leased to Southern Air.[^3][^9] *The [[The Washington Post]]* reported that a Southern Air credential issued to the pilot [[William Cooper]] was found in the wreckage, that Kress said it let Cooper onto the facility to supervise maintenance, and that a second camouflaged C-123 serviced at Southern Air flew from Miami toward Honduras in the days after the shootdown; the only FAA record of that aircraft was a July 3 reservation by a mechanic for "Corporate Air Service," with a Miami address that was Southern Air's.[^14] In December 1986 an air-support manager carried $6,000 in cash from Southern Air in Miami to [[Sally Hasenfus]].[^15]

On the aircraft identity, the CIA Inspector General's December 24, 1996 review of [[Iran]]-Contra files, Southern Air files from the 1960s and 1970s, and other records found no document indicating a CIA link with N4410F and no document that Southern Air Transport owned, operated or leased a C-123K, and listed review of further Southern Air files as an action remaining.[^16] The Enterprise paid Southern Air for the purchase of a C-123, per the independent counsel; the report does not identify the airframe.[^6]

### Drug-trafficking records

The CIA Inspector General's 1998 report recorded that on January 21, 1987 Deputy Director [[Robert Gates]] sent Assistant Secretary of State [[Morton Abramowitz]] a memorandum stating that the Customs office in New Orleans was investigating an allegation of drug trafficking by Southern Air Transport crew members, that the source was a senior official of the FDN, and that the official feared the scandal "could redound badly on FDN interests"; Gates's transmittal letter attributes the allegations to [[Mario Calero]]. A February 23, 1991 [[DEA]] cable stated that Southern Air Transport was "of record" in the DEA's database from January 1985 to September 1990 for alleged involvement in cocaine trafficking, and that an August 1990 entry alleged that $2 million was delivered to the company's business sites and that several pilots and executives were suspected of smuggling "narcotics currency."[^17]

[[Gary Webb]] wrote in *Dark Alliance* that the Miami [[Federal Bureau of Investigation|FBI]] informant [[Wanda Palacios]] reported in 1986 that she saw Southern Air planes loaded with cocaine and unloading guns in [[Barranquilla]], [[Colombia]], in 1983 and October 1985, that she accompanied [[Pablo Escobar]] to the landing site and spoke with [[Jorge Ochoa]], who said he worked with the CIA, and that the recovered flight logs showed flights to Barranquilla in October 1985; Southern Air said the planes carried drilling equipment. Webb wrote that [[Enrique Miranda]] testified cocaine from Ilopango was loaded onto Salvadoran Air Force aircraft and Southern Air planes under the supervision of [[Marcos Aguado]] and [[Norwin Meneses]], that Southern Air officials denied involvement and sued news organizations, and that the informant [[Allen Raul Rudd]] reported Escobar's statement that planes like C-130s flew guns to Colombia and cocaine to U.S. military bases. Webb is the sole source for these passages.[^18]

### Later operations

Southern Air Transport's operations continued under the Southern Air name after 1999. Southern Air Holdings filed Chapter 11 petitions in the Bankruptcy Court for the District of [[Delaware]] on September 28, 2012, describing itself as a long-haul wide-body air cargo provider for governments and commercial users, a participant in the Civil Reserve Air Fleet with contracts with the Department of Defense and the [[United Kingdom Ministry of Defence]], and [[DHL]] as its largest commercial customer; it attributed the filing to reduced Department of Defense air cargo spending as [[Afghanistan]] deployments wound down.[^19] [[Atlas Air Worldwide Holdings]] completed its acquisition of Southern Air Holdings, including Southern Air and [[Florida West International Airways]], for about $110 million in cash on April 7, 2016.[^20]
