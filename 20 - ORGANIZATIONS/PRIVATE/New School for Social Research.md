A private New York City university founded in 1919 by professors who had been dismissed or had resigned during the First World War, the New School for Social Research opened as an adult school of social science with money from the circle around [[The New Republic]].[^1] Its Graduate Faculty, known from 1933 as the [[University in Exile]], took in German and Central European scholars, and during the Second World War members of that faculty worked for the [[Office of Strategic Services]], the [[Foreign Broadcast Intelligence Service]] and the [[Office of War Information]].

### Founding, 1917 to 1922

In 1917 [[Nicholas Murray Butler]], president of [[Columbia University]], dismissed [[Henry Dana]] and [[James McKeen Cattell]] for their outspoken opposition to the war. [[Charles Austin Beard]] and [[James Harvey Robinson]] resigned in protest, and Robinson then persuaded [[Herbert Croly]], the first editor of the New Republic, to join them in founding a new school.[^1] The 1919 founding proposal rejected endowment. Beard wrote that "Endowment spells dry rot," and the founders planned to rely on tuition and annual gifts.[^1]

[[Dorothy Payne Whitney]], the Whitney heiress who had married [[Willard Straight]] and co-founded the New Republic with Croly, had discussed an independent social-science institute with Croly, Beard and Robinson from 1916. Her husband, then serving in Europe, warned her against the two men. She pledged $10,000 a year for ten years.[^2] The ten-year pledges reached nearly $300,000, much of it from Straight, and she paid largely for a renovated townhouse.[^3] A women's group that met to support the school included Mrs. Willard Straight, Mrs. Learned Hand and, later, Mrs. [[Thomas W. Lamont]].[^4]

The school did not rely on endowment. The [[Rockefeller Foundation]], the Carnegie and the Rosenwald foundations turned down an early request for an endowment, and the school could not count on the Rockefellers, the Whitneys, the Goodyears or the Guggenheims.[^5] In 1922 the New Republic was short of money and its owners wanted the school closed so that Straight would support the magazine.[^5] Straight stayed on the board with Croly, [[Wesley Clair Mitchell]], Caroline Bacon and [[Emily James Putnam]].[^5] In April 1922 [[Alvin Johnson]] took over as director and wrote to Straight about the change.[^6]

Dorothy Payne Whitney remained on the Board of Directors until 1930, after 1925 as Mrs. Leonard Elmhirst, and continued financial support for several years after 1929 through the [[William C. Whitney Foundation]].[^7] A Cornell finding aid for her papers states that the school "was begun with the backing of the Whitney Estate in 1919."[^8] In 1969 Milton C. Rose, her son's attorney, told the FBI that the foundation was created by her and Willard Straight in memory of her father and that [[Michael Whitney Straight]] had been its president "since its inception in 1937."[^9]

### University in Exile, 1933

Johnson had co-edited the [[Encyclopaedia of the Social Sciences]] from 1930 to 1935 and had corresponded with German-speaking scholars who contributed to it. After the Nazi seizure of power he set out to shelter them. In April 1933 he began to raise $60,000 a year for two years to support a faculty of fifteen scholars.[^10] Before the largest gift he had raised between $7,000 and $15,000.[^11]

[[Hiram J. Halle]] telephoned Johnson after a letter Johnson had written to [[Edwin R.A. Seligman]] reached him. Johnson's account has Halle telling him "I am prepared to guarantee the sixty thousand a year for two years." Halle wanted his name kept out of the announcement so that other donors would not hold back, and [[Frank Altschul]] vouched for his credit.[^12] The school's own history describes Halle's gift as the entire sum Johnson required.[^10] Johnson's account has Halle guaranteeing $60,000 a year for two years.[^12] Bessner writes that Johnson had collected $120,000 from Halle to fund fifteen professorships at $4,000 a year and raised further money through the Institute of International Education, and that Lederer, then in London, asked Speier to help, so that in the summer of 1933 Speier carried contracts between London and Berlin to prospective members.[^13]

The first appointments were made with the Rockefeller Foundation. Johnson and the Foundation agreed on a dozen individuals to rescue, and the Foundation's Paris associate [[Tracy Kittredge]] wrote on the State Department's view of the question on March 29, 1933.[^14] The [[Emergency Committee in Aid of Displaced Foreign Scholars]] acted for the Foundation in these early cases and did not seek permanent United States posts for the men it supported.[^14] The school's history lists the original members as the agricultural economist [[Karl Brandt]], the public finance specialist [[Gerhard Colm]], [[Arthur Feiler]], [[Eduard Heimann]], [[Herman Kantorowicz]], [[Emil Lederer]], [[Hans Speier]], [[Erich von Hornbostel]], [[Max Wertheimer]] and [[Frieda Wunderlich]]. By October 1933 ten academics were teaching, and by 1935 six more had been added.[^10]

The Emergency Committee was founded in May 1933 when Alfred E. Cohn, Bernard Flexner and Fred M. Stein approached [[Stephen Duggan]] of the [[Institute of International Education]]. [[Felix Warburg]] and [[Alan Gregg]] advised the organizers, and [[Livingston Farrand]] chaired it. The Committee made grants to host institutions rather than to scholars, and it made 335 grants from about 6,000 applicants. [[Edward R. Murrow]] was its assistant secretary until 1935.[^15] Duggan invited Johnson to lunch after the University in Exile was announced and told him of the Committee's plan.[^16] The Committee's records file [[Kurt Riezler]], Speier, [[Max Ascoli]], Colm, [[Arnold Brecht]], [[Hans Simons]], [[Hans Staudinger]], [[Jacob Marschak]], [[Adolph Lowe]], Lederer and [[Wilhelm Reich]] under the heading "Non-Grantees," which the finding aid defines as applicants not aided through the Committee.[^15] The school's history lists Colm and Speier among its original members.[^10]

### Federal contacts

[[Peter M. Rutkoff]] and [[William B. Scott]] write that after 1947 Johnson acknowledged in private letters that he had been meeting regularly with [[Federal Bureau of Investigation|FBI]] officials since the 1930s to report on the faculty, and that a Rockefeller Foundation memorandum of January 27, 1942 indicated that he had also monitored files of "enemy aliens" in the early 1940s for the federal district attorney.[^17] According to [[Sidney Hook]], Johnson on at least one occasion "wanted to get rid of a Communist Party member" but "delegated it to [[Clara Mayer]]." The Graduate Faculty charter adopted an explicitly anticommunist clause in 1940, after difficulties with the State Department and immigration authorities.[^17] In early 1942 Johnson requested a meeting at which he and Staudinger appeared before a panel drawn from the Federal Bureau of Investigation, the Visa Division, Naval Intelligence and the Army, and Johnson had to defend himself against charges of heading a "Communist outfit."[^18] In the early 1950s the school's administration received "inquiries from congressional and Senate committees, the FBI, and numerous individuals."[^19] Johnson also sat on a wartime enemy alien hearing board whose cases had been "well prepared in advance by the FBI."[^12]

### Graduate Faculty and wartime government work

The Office of Strategic Services considered the New School "the best collection of German, left-leaning intellectuals in the United States." Over two hundred students who had studied at the University in Exile worked for the government during the war, and New School scholars were involved in more than twenty-five government-associated projects.[^20] Foreign nationals on the faculty could not work directly for Washington, so agencies used intermediaries at the Rockefeller Foundation to assign projects to them. The National Defense Commission asked the Graduate Faculty in the winter of 1939 to 1940 for analyses of Nazi successes. Organizations that used Graduate Faculty research included the Board of Economic Warfare, the Foreign Economic Administration, the National Economic and Social Planning Association and the Public Administration Committee of the Social Science Research Council.[^13][^20] The Graduate Faculty also produced a translation of Hitler's Mein Kampf in 1938 and 1939, which Bessner writes the OSS and the OWI used, possibly through Speier.[^13]

The [[Research Project on Totalitarian Communication]], led by Speier and the psychoanalyst [[Ernst Kris]], was funded by the Rockefeller Foundation until 1943.[^21] The project ran from 1940 to 1944 in the school's finding aid.[^22] The preface of its 1944 book dates the start of work to April 1, 1941. The Rockefeller Foundation granted $15,960 in March 1941 and $19,000 in 1942, and Johnson housed the staff in New School rooms, which Bessner attributes to his wish to show the government and the intellectual community that he was not hiding a fifth column. Its research papers went to agencies in Washington, London and Ottawa.[^23] Its analysts studied radio broadcasts from totalitarian countries and coordinated their work with the [[British Broadcasting Corporation]] and United States agencies. Many of the researchers later held federal posts.[^21] The project's staff included Sidney Axelrad, Joe Carmichael, Rose M. Chayes, Henry Elkin, Erik Estorik, Jacob Goldstein, Hans Herma, George Hilton, Eleanor Horn, Gertrud Kurth, Stanley Lipkin, Janice Loeb, Rita Lowe, Margaret P. Mascret, Alexander Mintz, George A. Nelson Jr., Margaret Otis, Heinz Paechter, Thomas Rubinstein, Dorothy L. Sandler, Joseph Shor, Ursula Wasserman, Florence Weil and Howard B. White.[^24] The project's 1944 book, German Radio Propaganda, went to allied agencies, and the acting head of the British Political Warfare Mission in Washington called it "a magnificent piece of work."[^25]

Exiles from the Graduate Faculty held wartime posts across the government. Bessner's list places [[Franz Borkenau]], [[Joseph Bornstein]], [[Leo Löwenthal]] and [[Heinz Paechter]] at the Office of War Information, and [[Felix Gilbert]], [[Emil Gumbel]], [[Arkadij Gurland]], [[Hajo Holborn]], [[Otto Kirchheimer]], [[Herbert Marcuse]], [[Franz Neumann]] and [[Sigmund Neumann]] at the Office of Strategic Services.[^26]

Speier's wartime career ran from the project into the Foreign Broadcast Intelligence Service, where [[Goodwin Watson]], head of its Analysis Division, offered him the post of Senior Political Analyst in February 1942; he became Principal Political Analyst in November 1942, and Bessner writes that Watson later "left the FBIS under pressure from enemies of the New Deal, and Speier became the FBIS's Acting Chief Analyst."[^27][^28] On May 22, 1944 he transferred to the Office of War Information's Overseas Operations Branch.[^28] A connection he made at the OWI with [[Leo Rosten]] brought him to the [[Rand Corporation]], where he headed the Social Science Division from July 1948.[^29][^30] [[John Foster Dulles]], a Rockefeller Foundation trustee, urged the Foundation after the war to continue work in this field in order to counter Soviet propaganda in Europe.[^21]

### Institute of World Affairs

The [[Institute of World Affairs]] was founded as an independent unit of the New School in 1943, taking over the collective research projects of the Graduate Faculty, and was redesignated the Research Division in 1953. It developed from a Study Group on Germany and from the Totalitarian Communication Project organized by Speier and Kris, and its studies included work done in conjunction with, or on the commission of, United States war offices, among them Germany's Position in European Postwar Reconstruction. Max Ascoli handled initial planning and fund-raising until Adolph Lowe became director of research in 1942.[^31] The records' abstract gives 1942 as the founding year.[^32] Funding for 1943 to 1951 is reported at $630,000, from [[Independent Aid]], the Rockefeller Foundation, the Viking Fund, the Hebrew Technical Institute, the Rosenwald Foundation and individual donors.[^31] Bessner describes the Institute as "a research center funded by Doris Duke."[^33] Independent Aid, Inc. was the first charitable foundation established by [[Doris Duke]]; it was incorporated in Delaware in October 1934 and renamed the Doris Duke Foundation in October 1951.[^34]

In October 1943 [[Albert Salomon]] asked Speier to join the Institute's council. Speier replied that he "would like to be helpful, if I am wanted," and later withdrew his declaration to serve "in principle" pending further information, writing to Lowe on February 25, 1944 that he did not want to be considered a member of the council.[^35] Bessner states that he came to believe he was being "used as a pawn in ridiculous faculty politics."[^35] In 1947 and 1948 he planned a Division of International Studies, which was elected on December 17, 1947 and never realized.[^36]

### Ecole Libre des Hautes Etudes

In 1941 Johnson facilitated the establishment of the [[Ecole Libre des Hautes Etudes]] at the New School, also called the Free French School. Between 1942 and 1946 it offered courses, mostly in French, in the social sciences and humanities. The Ecole Libre was recognized by the de Gaulle and Belgian governments as equivalent to a university degree. Its board included [[Henri Focillon]], [[Jean Perrin]], [[Paul van Zeeland]], [[Jacques Maritain]] and [[Gustave Cohen]]. Some faculty held that the school should be scholarly and non-political, while others, supporters of the Free France movement led by [[Charles de Gaulle]], held that it had an intrinsic relationship to that movement. The Ecole Libre was severed from the New School in June 1947.[^37]

### Faculty

[[Leo Strauss]] came to the school on a path that ran through the Rockefeller Foundation. The Foundation and [[Harold Laski]] of the London School of Economics recommended him to Johnson.[^38] Strauss taught at the New School from 1938 to 1948.[^39] [[Hannah Arendt]] was University Professor in Philosophy from 1967 to 1975. The school's archive holds her office files, including correspondence with [[Elizabeth Young-Bruehl]] from 1969 to 1975.[^40] The Rockefeller Foundation records list a grant in aid to Arendt under the New School.[^41]

Riezler, a diplomat and philosopher, emigrated at the end of 1938 and held a Graduate Faculty post. The Editors' Note in the Spring 1956 issue of [[Social Research]] states that he was Professor of Philosophy from 1939 to 1952, when he became Professor Emeritus; [[Wayne C. Thompson]] writes that he retired in 1952, the Marbach authority record gives his New School affiliation as circa 1939 to 1954, and the Neue Deutsche Biographie has him returning to Rome in 1954.[^42][^43][^44][^45] Johnson recorded Riezler as his last appointment: "With Riezler the roster was closed."[^46] The Institute of World Affairs finding aid lists him among the Study Group on Germany, attended by Eduard Heimann, [[Erich Hula]], [[Karl Mayer]], Albert Salomon, Strauss, [[Horace Kallen]] and [[Felix Kaufmann]], and among the researchers associated with the University in Exile.[^31] The papers of Reuben Abel list him among the lecturers Abel attended, and the school's faculty vertical files hold a file for him.[^47][^48] Riezler and Strauss taught two courses together in the mid-1940s, one on Aristotle's De anima and Descartes's Passions of the Soul with [[Solomon Asch]] and one on Plato's Theaetetus with [[Alexandre Koyré]], and Riezler published "Some Critical Remarks on Man's Science of Man" in Social Research in 1945.[^49][^50] Strauss's eulogy for Riezler appeared in Social Research in 1956 beside a memorial article by [[Theodor Heuss]]. His daughter Maria married the New School professor [[Howard B. D. White]].[^45][^51][^52] Ascoli joined the University in Exile in late 1933 after Johnson recruited him as one of two Rockefeller scholars, and he later edited [[The Reporter]].[^53]

Wilhelm Reich appears in the school's archive through a bound photocopy of his 1940 New School lectures, transcribed from the archive of the Orgone Institute.[^54] The NYPL files list a Reich file for 1939 and 1940 under non-grantees.[^15] An official of the school, whose name is redacted, told FBI agents in December 1941 that Reich had been an associate professor for two years, during 1939 and 1940, at about $1,500 a year, and that the connection ended after the spring term of 1941; the school, the official said, "makes no inquiry of their political views or background."[^55] The same official described Reich as conducting "a lecture in psychiatry" and said he "claims to have a cure-all for cancer," all of which the official "stated smacked of 'quack' tactics on subject's part."[^55] A Bureau memorandum of May 6, 1941 records that Reich's non-quota visa of August 11, 1939 was issued because his "New York Seminar for Vegetotherapy" was part of the New School; the immigration record gives his arrival as August 28, 1939 and his destination as the school at 66 West 12th Street, and the New York report names "Dr. Alvin Johnson, Director" as the destination.[^56] A Bureau summary of February 1957 (M. A. Jones to L. B. Nichols) condenses the 1941 interview to a statement that Reich entered the United States in 1939, taught psychology at the New School "until let out by the school because his teaching 'smacked of quack tactics,'" and was apprehended as a German alien on December 12, 1941.[^57] The school's archive also keeps a faculty vertical file for [[Gregory Bateson]].[^58] [[Erich Fromm]] taught courses at the school from 1941 to 1959.[^59]

### Parapsychology listings and teaching, 1968 to 1994

The school appears in several parapsychology directories and reports in the CIA-held STARGATE record. A special report in the Princeton Alumni Weekly by [[Robert G. Jahn]] lists the New School among the colleges and universities in its survey of psychic research in the Western world.[^60] The 1975 survey New Psychic Frontiers by [[Walter Uphoff]] and [[Mary Jo Uphoff]] lists under the New School an entry naming [[Robert M. Brier]], [[Martin Ebon]], [[Gilbert Roller]] and others at 66 West 12th Street, New York 10011, in its chapter on research, investigation and teaching; the same survey places Brier at [[C.W. Post College]] in its philosophy department and [[Hans Holzer]] at the New York Institute of Technology.[^61] The Gilbert Roller of this entry is the Gilbert Roller who as a teenager took part in the early 1930s [[Bindelof séance circle]]: [[Rosemarie Pilkington]], author of *The Spirit of Dr. Bindelof*, wrote in 2007 that she "knew Gil Roller for more than 30 years" and called the first part of her book an account of "Gil Roller's and the Bindelof group's extraordinary experiences."[^62]

The school's Bulletin for spring 1975 lists "Experimental Parapsychology" (course 2750-0, Bob Brier, Wednesdays from February 5, $90), "Paramechanics: A Study of Paranormal Psychical Phenomena" (2755-3, Gilbert Roller, Tuesdays from February 4, $90), "The Challenge of Psychic Phenomena" (2760-3, Edith Wegman) and a parapsychology course by Parimal Das (5747-3). Roller's faculty biography reads that he conducted investigations into paranormal phenomena, particularly physical manifestations, and was a faculty member of F.I.T. and president of Hepworth Films Corp.; Parapsychology Review reported that Roller taught a course on telekinesis, poltergeists and ectoplasmia.[^63] Ebon's own lecture series "Parapsychology: From Magic to Science" ran from 1966 to 1968, and The Writer's Directory lists him as lecturer in the Division of Social Sciences in 1949 to 1950, 1955 to 1956 and 1967.[^64] The archive holds a file "Eileen J. Garrett Scholarship, 1975" and a file "Soviet Affairs Forum: Related Courses, 1964 January 29."[^65]

The 1968 *Journal of Parapsychology* index lists the New School for Social Research, New York, at pages 171 to 173, within the pages (167-175) of the article "The Possible Effect of the Checker in Precognition" by [[Sara R. Feather]] and Robert Brier.[^66]

The author biography of Gilbert Roller's 1975 book *A Voice From Beyond* states that he teaches a course in paramechanics at the New School for Social Research in New York City, which "comprises an inquiry into telekinesis, poltergeists, ectoplasm and thought photography." The same biography records a childhood poltergeist, his mother's séances and a teenage group of friends who "held séances regularly for about two years."[^67]

A 1982 engineering article by Jahn in the Proceedings of the IEEE (February 1982) includes a roster of United States universities and colleges where parapsychology is pursued that names the New School for Social Research, and the same roster appears in a 1991 anthology compiled by [[Science Applications International Corporation]].[^68] A conference program circulated by email in March 1994 lists [[Sheila Davis]] of the New School with a paper titled "Cerebral function, Jungian typology and language style: Toward a new theory."[^69]

### MKULTRA projects, 1962

The school's archive holds a folder of the records of president [[John R. Everett]] headed "Central Intelligence Agency (CIA), 1977-1978." The folder contains correspondence between Everett and the CIA about the release of [[Project MKUltra|MKULTRA]] details, including the release of universities' involvement, and "a report of two projects conducted at the New School in 1962 that received funding through MKUltra." The first attempted to discern relationships between body type and personality characteristics, and the second concerned graphology and psychopathologies. The description states that the researchers on at least one of them appear to have been unaware of CIA sponsorship.[^70] The description does not name the researchers or give subproject numbers. Two released subproject files, Subprojects 134 and 137, match the two descriptions and are set out on the Project MKUltra page.[^71]

The copy of the two subproject files that the CIA sent the school on September 7, 1977 carries red insertions of the school's name on the Subproject 134 memorandum of May 29, 1961 and on the Subproject 137 memorandum of August 24, 1961, where the insertion reads "New School for Social Research, New York City." The agency's cover letter, signed by [[A. R. Cinquegrana]] of the Office of General Counsel, states that "the name of the School has been reinserted wherever it appears in the original documents."[^72] The investigators' names remain blacked out in that copy.[^73]

The agency first notified the school by a letter from General Counsel [[Anthony A. Lapham]] that Everett's reply of August 16, 1977 calls "Your letter of August 12th." Everett wrote that it "came as quite a shock to me," that the relevant people at the school "know nothing about the matter," and that "some have left and others have died."[^74] The school's typed draft statement on the notification, undated, states that the school "chose not to notify either its own members or the public of its involvement pending its receipt of additional information from the CIA."[^75] Cinquegrana wrote on March 17, 1978 that "to date we have received no response from your institution."[^76]

The graphology teacher [[Klara G. Roman]] taught at the school from 1948 until shortly before her death in 1962. Her Encyclopedia of the Written Word, a graphology lexicon, was edited after her death by [[Maurice Edwards]] and [[Rose Wolfson]], and its acknowledgments thank "The Handwriting Institute and The Human Ecology Fund" and Daniel S. Anthony "of The New School for Social Research."[^77] Subproject 137, a $2,000 grant paid in 1961 for the manuscript of a graphology lexicon by an unnamed woman who died in 1962, has a curriculum vitae in the released file that lists the invention of a "Graphodyne"; Roman invented the graphodyne. The CIA copy does not name her. The Human Ecology Fund's 1961-1963 report, as tabulated by David H. Price, lists $2,000 to "Roman, Klara G."[^71] The first project, on body type and personality, matches Subproject 134, whose investigator is not named in the released file.

On October 9, 1977, the New York Times reported that the list of 80 institutions given to Senate investigators "is still classified, but each of those institutions has been notified separately by the C.I.A." that it had hosted agency research, and that 26 colleges and universities had acknowledged the research publicly.[^78]

### Presidents

The finding aid for the school's presidential records lists Hans Simons (1950 to 1961), [[Henry David]] (1961 to 1963), [[Robert MacIver]] (1963 to 1964) and Everett (1964 to 1982).[^79] The school's history and the Adult Division records date Simons's retirement from the presidency to 1960, when he moved to a consultancy and directorship with the [[Ford Foundation]].[^80][^81] The press releases for the appointment of Henry David fall between January 18 and March 19, 1961, and his inauguration was held on October 23, 1961.[^82][^83]

Simons's presidency is also documented in the school's own files. His "Future of the New School," dated September 18, 1958, responds in a Publicity Office record to what the finding aid calls the "McGrath Report."[^84] The New School Associates records hold a presidential talk on "Hans Simon's report on India," dated January 28, 1960.[^85]

Everett's tenure is described as one of the longest in the school's history, second only to Johnson's.[^86] The New School Archives' Everett records list Everett's presidency as 1964 to 1982 and describe the Everett papers' Britannica period as 1962 to 1964, before his New School appointment.[^87] [[Jonathan F. Fanton]], appointed in 1984, left the $250,000 post in September 1999 for the [[MacArthur Foundation]], and on February 17, 2000 the school named [[Bob Kerrey]], who said he had been planning to run for a third Senate term until the school approached him, as its seventh president, to start after he left the Senate in January 2001.[^88] A fact sheet captured by the Internet Archive on December 11, 2000 lists [[John L. Tishman]] as chairman of the board of trustees, [[Arnold H. Aronson]], [[Michael E. Gellert]] and [[Nancy B. Peretsman]] as vice chairs and Scaturro as treasurer, and names Scaturro as chancellor, [[Elizabeth Dickey]] as acting provost and [[James Murtha]] as executive vice president.[^89] Kerrey was installed at [[Carnegie Hall]] on February 20, 2001, with Tishman, "Chair of the Board of Trustees," giving remarks, and Tenet Healthcare's proxy statement of August 2001 gives him as "President of New School University in New York City since January 2001."[^90] On December 10, 2008 the faculty voted no confidence in him, which the Chronicle of Higher Education described as "nearly unanimous" with 74 votes in favor, two against and one abstention in a summary by Faculty Senate co-chair Jim Miller; the trustees met the same day and, in a university statement, unanimously expressed confidence in him. The amNewYork newspaper reported on December 23, 2008 that a majority of senior faculty approved a no-confidence measure "by a vote of 269 to 18" after a Faculty Senate meeting a week before the student occupation of the cafeteria at 65 Fifth Avenue.[^91] [[David E. Van Zandt]], dean and professor of law at the Northwestern University School of Law, was announced on August 26, 2010 as the eighth president and took office on January 1, 2011, with Kerrey remaining president until the end of 2010; Van Zandt's installation ceremony took place on September 15, 2011, and the archive's biographical note for Kerrey dates his presidency "from 2001 through 2010."[^92][^93] [[Dwight A. McBride]] was the ninth president from 2020 to 2023, [[Donna Shalala]] served as interim president from August 2023, and [[Joel Towers]], a member of the school since 2004 and a former executive dean of [[Parsons School of Design]], was appointed the tenth president effective August 1, 2024.[^94]

### Trustees and finances

[[Philip Scaturro]] was chairman of the board in 2004, when [[Peggy Brim Bewkes]], [[Eileen Naughton]], [[Richard Reiss Jr.]] and [[Bernard L. Schwartz]], chairman and chief executive of [[Loral Space & Communications]], joined the Board of Trustees on September 1.[^95] On November 4, 2004 the school announced a $10 million grant from the [[Starr Foundation]], the largest foundation gift in its history, to establish the [[India China Institute]], led by Provost [[Arjun Appadurai]] and Graduate Faculty dean [[Benjamin Lee]], and a $3.7 million gift from Schwartz for the Bernard Schwartz Center for Economic Policy Analysis and the Irene and Bernard L. Schwartz Professorship of Economics and Policy. The announcement attributes the Schwartz gift to Schwartz and, in Kerrey's quoted remarks, to the Bernard and Irene Schwartz Foundation.[^96]

On November 9, 2001, the school announced a gift of $1.1 million from Schwartz, then chairman and chief executive of Loral Space & Communications, for the Bernard L. Schwartz Lecture Series and Project in Markets, Equality and Democracy, a four-year project based at the Graduate Faculty's Center for Economic Policy Analysis.[^97] The November 4, 2004 release says the center had been established in September 2004 and would be renamed the Bernard Schwartz Center for Economic Policy Analysis "in recognition of the Schwartz Foundation's gift," and that it would continue to sponsor the Irene and Bernard Schwartz Lecture.[^98]

The same release describes the Starr Foundation as established in 1955 by [[Cornelius Vander Starr]], an insurance entrepreneur who founded the American International family of insurance companies, now [[American International Group]], and as holding assets of approximately $3.5 billion.[^99] The House Select Committee on U.S. National Security and Military/Commercial Concerns with the People's Republic of China deposed Schwartz on November 21, 1998 and reported that Schwartz, chairman and chief executive of Loral Space & Communications, Ltd., met directly with Commerce Secretary [[Ron Brown]] on at least four occasions after 1993.[^100]

The school's fiscal 2022 Form 990 lists [[Linda E. Rappaport]] as chair, [[Elliot Stein]], [[Franci J. Blassberg]] and Susan L. Foote as vice chairs, and McBride as president with reportable compensation of $815,044 and estimated other compensation of $604,206; the trustees listed include [[Beth Rudin DeWoody]] and [[Bevis Longstreth]].[^101] The school's board page lists current trustees without titles, with Rappaport first and Towers as president ex officio; life trustees include Scaturro, who was the school's chancellor when Kerrey was named.[^88][^102] The school's federal filings, retrieved through the ProPublica Nonprofit Explorer summary data for EIN 13-3297197, show total revenue of $602,901,362 and total assets of $1,706,553,269 for the fiscal year ending June 2023. Contributions reported in that year were $35,451,892, down from $55,910,574 in fiscal 2022.[^103]

### Epstein payments

Bank statements for [[Jeffrey Epstein]]'s account at [[Deutsche Bank]] record payments to the school. The August 2015 statement lists an outgoing transfer on August 6 of $16,083.00 to a [[Bank of America]] account, with the beneficiary line "E NEW SCHOOL OPERATING ACCOUNT."[^107] The October 2016 statement lists a preauthorized debit dated October 27 of $17,030.00 described as "TUITION THE NEW SCHOOL."[^108] The May 2017 statement lists a preauthorized debit dated May 10 of $10,856.00 described as "TUITION THE NEW SCHOOL." The same statement page lists an outgoing transfer on May 11 of $3,589.00 through Sterling National Bank with the beneficiary line "FASHION INSTITUTE OF TECHNOLOGY."[^109] A Deutsche Bank presentation to the Office of the United States Attorney for the Southern District of New York, dated September 12, 2019, lists in an exhibit titled "Tuition Payments on Behalf of Children / Women" nine payments to The New School from Epstein accounts: $29,025 on February 4, 2014; $15,704 on January 5, 2015; $4,730 on June 12, 2015; $16,083 on August 6, 2015; $16,656 on December 9, 2015; $4,420 on June 14, 2016; $16,850 on July 8, 2016; $3,756 on January 13, 2017; and $8,790 on January 10, 2019. The exhibit's only [[New York Film Academy]] entry is $3,442 on March 10, 2017.[^110]

Emails released by the Department of Justice show the payments were for one student's tuition, invoiced by the school each term from fall 2014 to spring 2019. Epstein's bookkeeper [[Bella Klein]] wired the payments, and his accountant [[Richard Kahn]] forwarded invoices for approval. On December 9, 2015, Klein asked Epstein to "confirm that i can proceed with wire in the amount of $16,656," and he replied "yes."[^111] On September 20, 2016, Kahn forwarded a fall invoice with "approval to pay thank you."[^112] The fall 2015 invoice of $16,083 matches the August 6, 2015 wire to the school's operating account.[^113] On August 1, 2018, Klein wrote: "Today I paid balance for her fall semester in the amount of $7719."[^114] The spring 2019 invoice was $8,790.[^115]
