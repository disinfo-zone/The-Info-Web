El Mercurio is the principal conservative daily newspaper of [[Chile]], owned by the family of [[Agustin Edwards|Agustín Edwards]]. General [[Rene Schneider|René Schneider]] stated in an interview with it on May 8, 1970, that the army would not interfere in the election.[^1]

### Washington, September 1970

After [[Salvador Allende]] won a plurality on September 4, 1970, Edwards went to Washington. [[Donald Kendall]], head of [[PepsiCo|Pepsi-Cola]], arranged for him to see [[Richard Helms]] at a hotel and to have breakfast on September 15 with [[Henry A. Kissinger|Henry Kissinger]] and [[United States Attorney General|Attorney General]] [[John Mitchell]], to discuss "the political situation in Chile and the plight of El Mercurio and other anti-Allende forces." Edwards told Helms that a coup required "neutralizing Schneider." That afternoon President [[Richard Nixon]] ordered the [[Central Intelligence Agency|CIA]] to organize one. Helms testified: "I have this impression that the President called this meeting where I have my handwritten notes because of Edwards' presence in Washington and what he heard from Kendall about what Edwards was saying about conditions in Chile."[^1][^2]

Two days later [[Hal Hendrix]] and [[Robert Berrellez]] of [[ITT Corporation|ITT]] recommended to their company that it and other American firms "pump some advertising into Mercurio," which had begun, that help be given to some twenty radio and television propagandists formerly supported by "the Matte and Edwards groups," and that the [[United States Information Agency|United States Information Service]] be pressed to circulate the paper's editorials in [[Latin America]] and Europe.[^3]

### The Subsidy

"The [[40 Committee]] authorized $700,000 for El Mercurio on September 9, 1971, and added another $965,000 to that authorization on April 11, 1972," the Senate's staff reported. A [[National Intelligence Estimate]] of August 1971 had found that the paper "had managed to retain its independence" under the pressure of the Allende government, which the staff called "at some variance" with the case made for the money a month later.[^3]

The agency's own records credit "the El Mercurio project" with "a significant role" in bringing about the coup of 1973. It had paid reporters and editors of the paper through the 1960s. In September 1971 a representative of the Edwards group asked for "covert support totaling $1 million." On September 14 President Nixon personally authorized 700,000 dollars, "and more if necessary," and Richard Helms told the Western Hemisphere Division it might "exceed the authorized $700,000 and go up to, and even over, $1,000,000 provided it was warranted to keep the paper going." A CIA officer, [[Jonathan Hanke]], recorded on May 15, 1972, that Hal Hendrix of ITT had told him of deposits of 100,000 dollars that ITT was making secretly to Edwards: "He had told me money for the Edwards group went through a Swiss account."[^4]
