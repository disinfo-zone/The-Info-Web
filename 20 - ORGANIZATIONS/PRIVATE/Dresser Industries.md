Dresser Industries was an oilfield equipment and engineering company that began in 1880 as a rubber packer business in [[Bradford, Pennsylvania]]. It was headquartered in [[Dallas, Texas]] from 1950. From 1928 to 1962 it was run by [[Neil Mallon]], a member of [[Skull and Bones]] in the [[Yale University]] class of 1917, after an investment-banking firm bought the family business. Its subsidiaries supplied the oil industry and wartime production, and its Soviet business appears in [[Central Intelligence Agency]] and [[United States Department of Commerce]] records for 1948, 1959, 1978 and 1982. In 1998 the company merged into [[Halliburton]], then led by [[Dick Cheney]].[^1][^2]

### Solomon R. Dresser and the Bradford packer, 1880 to 1905

[[Solomon R. Dresser]] took out U.S. Patent No. 227,419, "Packing for Artesian Wells," on May 11, 1880. The specification describes a rubber seal that expands against the borehole wall when the tubing is pulled and contracts when it is lowered.[^3] The Handbook of Texas entry describes the product as the Dresser Cap Packer, sold from a frame building in Bradford, in the heart of the oilfields.[^1] The company history gives the same year and says Dresser opened the business in 1880 as S. R. Dresser and Company.[^4]

The packer was not the product that made the company national. Dresser devised a rubber-sealed pipe coupling in 1885, perfected it two years later, and used it to join gas pipelines so that they would not leak. The company history says that a [[Standard Oil]] superintendent placed an experimental order for nine of Dresser's sleeves in 1894, and that orders from other companies followed.[^5] Dresser incorporated the business in December 1905. The charter financial statement gave company resources of $550,000, of which fourteen patents accounted for almost $180,000.[^6]

The corporate lineage is recorded differently in later filings. The 1993 annual report to the Securities and Exchange Commission says the company was incorporated in Delaware in 1956 as a successor to a Pennsylvania corporation organized in 1938 by the consolidation of S. R. Dresser Manufacturing Company and Clark Bros. Company, and that both predecessors were "carrying on businesses founded in 1880."[^7] The 1997 annual report repeats the 1956 Delaware date.[^8] The Handbook entry and the 1979 corporate history by [[Darwin Payne]] both date the founding to 1880.[^1][^9]

### The Harriman purchase and Mallon's presidency, 1928 to 1962

In 1928 the Dresser family sold the business to [[W. A. Harriman & Company]], an investment-banking firm that intended to make the company a public corporation with separate ownership and management. The number of shares rose from 5,000 to 300,000. Of these, 100,000 Class A shares were offered at $48 each, and the Class B shares were held back until 1931. The aging [[Fred Miller]], who had run the business, stayed on as chairman of the board in a consultative capacity. Mallon became president at the age of thirty-three.[^10] The Handbook entry states that Mallon "was selected as president and chief executive officer" and held that position until his retirement in 1962.[^11]

The dates are not uniform. Payne's 1979 history lists the presidents as Solomon R. Dresser from 1880 to 1911, Fred A. Miller from 1911 to 1929, H. Neil Mallon from 1929 to 1962 and [[John Lawrence]] from 1962 to 1970.[^9] Prescott Bush, quoted by [[Garry Wills]] in 1992, dated the purchase to 1929: "In 1929 we bought Dresser Company of Bradford, Pennsylvania for four million dollars, and we sold securities against that company and refinanced it, you see, so that we retained a substantial measure of control."[^12] The company histories give 1928, and the recollection gives 1929.

The W. A. Harriman people named in the Dresser histories include [[E. Roland Harriman]], whom Payne describes as a son of the railroad magnate E. H. Harriman, and [[Prescott Bush]]. The Handbook entry says that Bush "had been a W. A. Harriman and Company executive who had been involved in the conversion of Dresser to a public company, and he served on the board of directors for twenty-two years."[^13] Wills quoted Bush's statement that he was "Neil Mallon's chief adviser and consultant in connection with every move that he made with that business during that period of twenty years."[^12] The 1917 Yale class history lists Bush, Mallon, E. Roland Harriman and [[Knight Woolley]] as members of Skull and Bones, and Payne says the same four men had been fellow members of the society.[^14][^15]

The W. A. Harriman firm had a Soviet connection before it bought Dresser. A manganese concession that the Soviet government granted to a Harriman company in 1925 is documented in the papers of [[W. Averell Harriman]].[^16]

### Acquisitions and the Liechtenstein subsidiary, 1930 to 1967

Dresser expanded after 1930 because welding was threatening its coupling business. The company bought makers of valves, heaters, pumps and engines, opened a Toronto subsidiary in 1931 after Canada limited imports, and acquired [[Bryant Heater Company]] in 1933 for $65,000.[^10][^17] In 1937 it acquired [[Clark Brothers Company]] of [[Olean, New York]], a maker of gas engines and compressors, and the two were consolidated in 1938.[^10][^7]

Dresser's wartime production included aircraft landing gear, wing flaps and bomb doors, engines for freighters, and pipeline components. The company history says that one of its most urgent assignments was at the [[Manhattan Project]], which was engaged in research to develop the atomic bomb, and that sales for 1944 were just under $55 million.[^18] Payne's history states that [[International Derrick and Equipment Company]] (IDECO), a maker of derricks and drilling rigs, was acquired by Dresser in 1944.[^9] In October 1944 the company changed its name to Dresser Industries, and the headquarters moved to [[Cleveland, Ohio]], where the new offices opened on March 19, 1945.[^9]

In November 1945 Dresser acquired [[Security Engineering]] of [[Whittier, California]], a maker of drilling bits.[^9] In November 1949 it bought the [[Magnet Cove Barium Corporation]] of [[Houston]] for $2.8 million. The company history says that Magnet Cove was later known as Magcobar.[^19] The headquarters moved to Dallas in 1950, which the company history describes as moving the company to "the epicenter of the U.S. oil business."[^19]

The company history states that in 1952 Dresser set up a Liechtenstein subsidiary, Dresser Vaduz, charged with executing and monitoring all foreign license agreements, and that the subsidiary "also funneled profits between the different countries, thus saving the parent company huge U.S. taxes by keeping profits overseas."[^20]

In 1955 Dresser Industries became the owner of all the stock of [[Lane-Wells]] Company, the Los Angeles well-logging and perforating firm, and on April 30, 1958 it liquidated Lane-Wells into a Dresser division, according to the statement of facts in a patent case that reached the Supreme Court.[^21] A Lane-Wells advertisement of July 1955 described the company as "one of the Dresser Industries."[^22] A July 1955 issue of the magazine of the [[Dallas Chamber of Commerce]] counted nine affiliated Dresser companies. Ideco and the Security Engineering Division were in Dallas, and Lane-Wells was among the units named elsewhere, with Clark, Dresser-Ideco, Dresser Manufacturing, Magcobar, Pacific and Roots-Connersville.[^23]

John Lawrence succeeded Mallon in 1962 and aimed acquisition offers at companies ancillary to the oil and gas supply business. [[Harbison-Walker Refractories Company]], a maker of heat-resistant brick and other materials for industrial furnaces, was merged into Dresser on October 26, 1967; its chairman, A. B. Wilson, was named to that office on that date, according to the annual report of its Canadian affiliate. The company history describes Harbison-Walker as a $125 million company that cost Dresser about $150 million.[^24][^25] The asbestos claims discussed below concern mainly refractory products of that kind.

### Soviet business, 1948 to 1959

The company history credits [[John O'Connor]], an executive vice president of Dresser known throughout the company as "the world's most travelled executive," with persuading the [[Soviet Union]] to build a $6 million natural gas liquefaction plant outside [[Moscow]]. The entry states that the plant was slated for completion in 1949, "fell victim to a Commerce Department trade ban early in 1948," and was unfinished until 1954.[^26]

A memorandum of January 14, 1959 by [[Lawrence R. Houston]], General Counsel of the Central Intelligence Agency, records a telephone call from a Department of Commerce official about a proposal by an American company to export technical data on petroleum engineering to Russia. Houston wrote that "this is connected with the Dresser Industries matter in which we had been involved about a year ago. Dresser had purchased a number of rotary drills from the Russians and had promised in return to give information or equipment to the Russians if permission to do so were granted. The Department of Commerce refused such permission to Dresser, and we were involved in the consideration of the case." The new application, covering petroleum engineering information including certain drill parts and their manufacture, "was turned down in the first instance on the advice of representatives of the Bureau of Mines and the Gas and Oil Division of Interior." Houston recorded that "the drills obtained from the Russians are now being manufactured by Dresser, and the American Petroleum Industry has benefited considerably thereby," that Dresser was appealing, and that it contended "anyone in the Soviet Bloc could buy their equipment in third countries." Houston arranged for a specialist in the Petroleum Division of the Office of Research and Reports to sit with the Commerce panel. The memorandum carries a copy line to the Director of Central Intelligence.[^27]

### The Dallas Council on World Affairs, 1951

The World Affairs Council of Dallas/Fort Worth states that it was founded in 1951 when Dresser Industries' chairman and president Henry Neil Mallon saw the need for a local institution to encourage citizen understanding of international affairs. Mallon gathered seventeen founders from the corporate and civic communities to create the Dallas Council on World Affairs, which became affiliated with the World Affairs Councils of America, an organization founded in 1918 in conjunction with the [[Foreign Policy Association]].[^28]

### Anthony Kimery and the CIA cover claim

The Central Intelligence Agency enters the Dresser record through Mallon's contacts. [[Anthony L. Kimery]] wrote in the CovertAction Information Bulletin in 1992 that Mallon "was a friend to numerous ranking Cold War era intelligence officials, including [[Allen Dulles]]," and that Mallon "steered prospective candidates for spy work to Dulles and often provided cover employment to CIA operatives."[^29] Kimery wrote that Dresser "had routinely served as a CIA cover" and gave as his basis for that statement interviews in 1990 and 1991 with two former Operations Directorate field officers, one of whom claimed to have worked for Dresser as his cover.[^29] He also wrote that [[George H.W. Bush]] began his career as a salesman for IDECO, a Dresser subsidiary.[^29]

Kimery wrote that Mallon introduced the Russian-born petroleum geologist [[George de Mohrenschildt]] to Bush at about the time Mallon gave Bush responsibility for negotiating deals with East-bloc oil officials, and that when a Yugoslav oil industry official came to the United States in 1948 to talk to Dresser, he went directly to Bush in Midland. Kimery quoted [[Victor Marchetti]], a former CIA officer: "It's inconceivable then that the CIA didn't debrief Bush after each and every meeting [he had with the East's representatives]." A retired CIA operative who said he had worked for Dresser as a cover told Kimery that "Bush had all the characteristics of being a spook."[^30]

### George H.W. Bush at Ideco, 1948 to 1951

A CIA biographical sheet on Bush lists him as an "Oil field supply salesman, Dresser Industries, Midland, Texas, 1948-51," and records that he formed Bush-Overbey Oil Development, Inc., in 1951.[^31] Bush's own account in *All the Best* says that he "accepted the job from Neil Mallon and drove our 1947 Studebaker (a graduation gift from my parents) to Odessa, Texas."[^32] In *Looking Forward*, written with [[Victor Gold]], Bush wrote that Mallon was "a friend and a mentor second only to my father," and that he became "a full-fledged Ideco salesman, selling drilling bits."[^33] An illustration in Payne's company history carries a caption identifying George Bush as an oil-field-supply salesman for Ideco, and Payne's index lists Bush at pages 232 and 233 and Prescott S. Bush at pages 111 to 115.[^34]

Two 1988 accounts in the CIA file tie the job to Mallon. [[Bob Woodward]] and [[Walter Pincus]] reported that Mallon, "another member" of the society, "gave Bush his first job out of Yale."[^35] [[Randall Rothenberg]] reported that Mallon offered Bush "a bottom-of-the-ladder job in Midland, Tex., at Dresser Industries."[^36] Bush's April 12, 1951 letter to "Mr. H.N. Mallon, Dresser Industries, Dallas, Texas" begins "Dear Neil, This will let you know that we are in business."[^32] The Bushes named their third son for Mallon. [[Neil Bush]] was born in Midland on January 22, 1955.[^32]

### John V. James and the Dresser leadership, 1967 to 1982

[[John V. James]] was an executive vice president of Dresser Industries in Dallas in 1968, when he joined the board of the Canadian affiliate of Harbison-Walker.[^24] Dresser's president and chief executive from 1970 was James. The company history describes him as a man "whose talents lay in planning," who had gained experience in an army intelligence unit during the Second World War.[^37] Under James the company acquired [[Lodge-Cottrell]] in 1972 and [[Jeffrey Galion]] in 1974, and it introduced multiproduct plants.[^37] James was chairman in 1982, the company history says, and was succeeded in 1983 by [[John J. Murphy]]. A 1986 joint venture with Halliburton created M-I Drilling Fluids Company, and in 1987 a 50-50 venture with [[Ingersoll-Rand]] created [[Dresser-Rand]].[^37]

The Business Council, a Washington association of corporate chief executives, listed "John V. James, Chairman & President, Dresser Industries, Inc." among its members in 1977, on a roster that also named [[Malcolm Baldrige]]. [[Stansfield Turner]], the Director of Central Intelligence, attended the council's session at The Homestead in May 1977, and a council officer invited him back for the session of October 13 to 15, 1977. In a letter of October 18, 1977 Turner thanked a council member for the hospitality and for "the reports on the Soviet and Chinese oil production."[^38]

### Questionable foreign payments, 1976 to 1980

On January 27, 1976 Dresser representatives met the staff of the [[Securities and Exchange Commission]] and agreed to conduct an internal inquiry into questionable foreign payments under the Commission's Voluntary Disclosure Program. Dresser filed Forms 8-K reporting one questionable foreign payment on January 28, 1976, the results of its inquiry on November 11, 1976, and a further payment on February 10, 1977.[^39] On July 15, 1977 Dresser refused the SEC staff access to the documents underlying its report, arguing that disclosure could endanger certain of its employees working abroad. The Department of Justice, which had a task force on transnational payments to which two SEC attorneys were assigned, received the SEC files on the roughly 400 participating companies, Dresser among them, in August 1977, and presented Dresser's case to a grand jury in the District of Columbia on January 25, 1978.[^39]

Dresser sued the SEC and Justice in Houston to stop the investigations. The grand jury subpoenaed its documents on April 21, 1978, the SEC issued a formal order of investigation and a subpoena, and on June 30, 1978 [[Thomas A. Flannery]] of the District Court for the District of Columbia ordered Dresser to comply, in an opinion reported at 453 F. Supp. 573.[^39] The Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit, sitting en banc with Chief Judge J. Skelly Wright writing, affirmed on July 16, 1980 in *Securities and Exchange Commission v. Dresser Industries, Inc.*, 628 F.2d 1368, and the Supreme Court denied review. The opinion records that the District Court had rejected an attempt by Edward R. Luter, a senior vice president of Dresser, to intervene on behalf of himself and other employees.[^39][^40]

A press clipping in a CIA file of September and October 1978 on the Justice Department's overseas-corruption task force reported that Dresser's chairman, John V. James, "resisted testifying before the grand jury on grounds that he was exonerated by a prior agreement with another government agency in regard to a $1.4 million payment to a foreign official to consummate a $40 million deal."[^41]

### Cold War exports and the 1978 drill bit contract

In June 1978 Soviet trade organizations awarded Dresser two contracts totaling $180 million. One supplied equipment and technology for manufacturing drill bits for oil and gas wells.[^42] The license was issued on August 9, 1978, according to a dispatch by Bernard Gwertzman in the Washington Star of August 30, 1978, and the Foreign Relations of the United States volume cites a State Department telegram of August 11 reporting that the Carter administration approved the export license in August.[^43][^42] The same volume states that [[Juanita Kreps]], the Secretary of Commerce, approved a $140 million drill bit deal in August, and that [[Zbigniew Brzezinski]] learned of the approval from the newspaper.[^44]

The Washington Star reported that Dresser issued a statement denying that the $144 million sale of advanced oil drilling technology would enhance Soviet military potential. In it Dresser accused Brzezinski, Energy Secretary [[James R. Schlesinger]] and Senator [[Henry M. Jackson]] of being "willing to go to any lengths to question the president's decision and kill the Dresser transaction as a symbol of their hard line attitude." Dresser called a report by J. Fred Bucy, president of Texas Instruments, prepared for the Pentagon's Science Advisory Board, "pre-ordained," and said the Pentagon had already judged that the drill bits "would not significantly enhance Soviet military capability." The Star reported that Brzezinski had convened a Cabinet-level meeting to seek approval for suspending the license and had failed to win the backing of the State and Commerce representatives.[^43]

On August 17, 1978, at a meeting of Secretary of State [[Cyrus Vance]] with representatives of the National Conference on Soviet Jewry, Eugene Gold, the conference's chairman, "questioned whether enough spade work had been done before the Dresser (drill bit) plant decision had been taken" and said that "First the President had said the entire license was under review, then 'bang' it was approved." Vance told him that the letter authorizing Dresser to sell the equipment had already been transmitted. Later in the meeting Yehuda Hellman, executive director of the Conference of Presidents, asked whether trade might be used as a better carrot with the Soviets. According to the memorandum of conversation, Vance replied that "the problem was that other countries had the same carrot. In the case of the Dresser license, for example, some thought that Dresser was the sole source of (drill bit) technology, but that was simply not true. Others could sell this (drill bit) technology to the Soviets as well."[^42]

Brzezinski's memorandum to [[Jimmy Carter]] of August 29, 1978 recommended a suspension of the Dresser licenses rather than revocation. It summarized a [[Defense Science Board]] report that called deep-well drilling technology "a critical technology, which is wholly concentrated in the U.S.," and rock drill bit manufacture critical as well, and it listed tungsten carbide as militarily significant, "easily diverted" and "not foreign-sourced, at this scale of manufacture." Bucy, a board member, wrote in his letter of transmittal that "a decision regarding the export of a turnkey factory for the manufacturing of drill bits for the exploration of oil is one of those" decisions that can change the course of history. Brzezinski wrote that to ignore the report "would be to play into Senator Jackson's hands," and he described the State and Commerce decision as "clearly hasty."[^45] Carter's marginal note beside the first conclusion of the report reads "We knew this."[^46]

Jackson wanted to prevent the deal by launching an investigation into its military and political ramifications, as [[Rowland Evans]] and [[Robert Novak]] wrote in the [[Washington Post]] of August 16, 1978.[^47] The FRUS editors' note records that Schlesinger opposed the technology deal, believing that national security would be compromised, and that Brzezinski questioned whether it was still appropriate to continue with it.[^44]

### The Kuybyshev plant and the tungsten carbide assessment, 1982 to 1984

The plant was built at Kuybyshev. A research paper of the CIA Directorate of Intelligence dated September 1984 states that it was designed to produce 100,000 bits annually, 86,000 of them with tungsten-carbide inserts and sealed-journal bearings, that production reached an annual rate of 100,000 as of early 1984, and that quality problems persisted, some aspects of which "probably stem from the inability of Dresser to have provided all of the necessary technical training." Bits from the plant lasted 50 to 60 hours, about one-third the life of comparable bits made in the United States.[^48] A CIA table of foreign availability of petroleum equipment states that "the Dresser drill bit plant would substantially improve Soviet bit productivity, which is poor by international standards" and that foreign competitors could not build a plant as quickly as American companies.[^49]

In February 1982 [[William J. Casey]], the Director of Central Intelligence, wrote to Commerce Secretary Malcolm Baldrige: "As the result of our analysis of recently acquired intelligence information the CIA now believes that the tungsten carbide production technology transferred as part of the Dresser drill bit plant sold to the USSR will be used in improving the armor-penetrating performance of anti-tank ammunition." Casey wrote that the Soviets still desired the hands-on assistance by American technicians called for in the Dresser contract, that denial of it "could stretch out the time required to assimilate the technology to produce improved anti-tank penetrators," and that the attached assessment was sent "because of your current review of this case." The enclosed assessment stated that "we believe this effort derives from Dresser Industries sales to the USSR" and that the know-how "probably was supplied as part of the drill bit turnkey plant built by Dresser Industries in Kuybyshev," adding that the specifications Dresser provided in 1978 and 1979 "included the know-how to vary precisely the composition of tungsten-carbide powders used to make sintered drill bit cones."[^50][^51]

A CIA memorandum for the record on a meeting of February 11, 1982 with two staff members of the Senate Select Committee on Intelligence states that they "were not impressed with the new information or our analysis commenting that they had come to that conclusion some three years ago," and that the CIA officers "pointed out that we also had hypothesized this conclusion in 1978 but had no intelligence that confirmed the likelihood." One staff member observed that "they perhaps had focused on the wrong questions in 1978," the concern being whether the technology had been transferred to another plant where military production takes place. The memorandum's author wrote that the staffers wanted the new information used "to prove that they (Senator Jackson's Staff) had been right in their earlier assessment," and that he expected "continuing pressure to break out this material for this purpose."[^52]

The Los Angeles Times reported on May 4, 1982 that a senior military official, speaking on condition of anonymity, said intelligence information showed the Soviets had made at least indirect use of the plant's manufacturing technology, and that the plant had been prepared for conversion to weapons production. The newspaper reported that a Dresser senior vice president for finance said the company had not previously heard the assertion and could not respond to it. The article states that the license was granted for a factory at Kuibyshev, that a Senate investigation of the deal took place in October 1978, and that Army officials had warned in 1978 that the sale would enhance Soviet production of anti-tank weapons.[^53]

### Siberian pipeline sanctions, 1982

The United States imposed controls on oil and gas equipment and technology for the Soviet Union on December 30, 1981 and extended them on June 22, 1982. On August 19, 1982 the General Counsel of the Treasury, [[Peter J. Wallison]], wrote to Secretary [[Donald Regan]] that the State Department had been notified the previous afternoon that the French government was about to order Dresser/France, "a wholly-owned subsidiary of Dresser Industries," to deliver three compressors to a Soviet ship in [[Le Havre]] for use in the Soviet gas pipeline. He wrote that "we may have an opportunity to enjoin Dresser Industries from permitting its subsidiary to violate U.S. export controls" by a temporary restraining order, and that acting against the American parent would answer European concerns about extraterritoriality. He wrote that seeking a restraining order "would send a strong signal that the President intends to enforce the gas pipeline regulations by whatever means available," and that "if there is to be a challenge in the U.S. courts to the validity of the regulations ... the case against Dresser might well be our strongest litigating position."[^54]

A memorandum of August 23, 1982 to the Senior Interdepartmental Group on International Economic Policy, from the legal offices of the Departments of State, Defense, Treasury, Justice and Commerce and the Trade Representative, described the company: "Dresser/France has a contract to produce over twenty compressors for the pipeline. As a wholly-owned French subsidiary of a U.S. company which has been told by the French Government that the French will take action to force the shipment of the compressors, Dresser/France is in the middle of a difficult French-U.S. jurisdictional tug-of-war." It stated that Dresser Industries "has apparently ordered the subsidiary to obey the export controls and not to ship," that Dresser/France "has reportedly told its freight forwarder not to ship," and that "neither an injunction to force them to do what they have already done nor issuance of a denial order to punish them for what they are trying to prevent appears reasonable." The memorandum continued that "should the French Government seize Dresser/France to force the shipment or should Dresser change its position, consideration should be given to issuing a denial order against Dresser/France."[^55]

On August 23, 1982 Dresser Industries and its French subsidiary, [[Dresser France]] (Dresser (France) S.A.), sued in the United States District Court for the District of Columbia to restrain Baldrige, the Secretary of Commerce, and other Commerce officials from imposing sanctions on the subsidiary. The court's opinion describes the regulations at issue as prohibiting the export of certain goods and technology to the Soviet Union, particularly goods sought for the construction of the gas pipeline between the Soviet Union and Western Europe, and as promulgated as part of a major foreign policy exercise.[^56] Flannery, who had ordered Dresser to comply with the SEC subpoena in 1978, denied a temporary restraining order on August 24, 1982, with a written order the next day.[^56]

On August 26, 1982 Commerce Hearing Commissioner Thomas W. Hoya issued an order temporarily denying all export privileges to Dresser (France) S.A., which the Federal Register published on August 30. The order recites the Department's statement that Dresser-France was "almost entirely owned by Dresser A.G. (Vaduz), a Liechtenstein corporation which is a wholly-owned subsidiary of Dresser Industries, a United States corporation," and that its investigation gave it reason to believe that Dresser-France "has manufactured compressors using United States-origin technology for use in, for, or by the Soviet Union" and "has exported, or has placed beyond its control to prevent the export of, these compressors from France to the Soviet Union contrary to the Regulations." The Hearing Commissioner found the order "required in the public interest to facilitate enforcement of the Export Administration Act of 1979" and "to permit completion of the Department's investigation."[^57] A Commerce briefing prepared for Casey's use stated that Dresser (France), of Le Havre, was "a second tier subsidiary of Dresser Industries, Inc. in Dallas, Texas," held through a Liechtenstein parent at Vaduz, that it had received its compressor technology from the Dresser Clark Division in Olean before December 30, 1981, and that it was subcontracted to [[Creusot-Loire]] to provide 21 centrifugal compressor units, against 42 for Creusot-Loire, 57 for Nuovo Pignone and 5 for Demag.[^58]

Dresser appealed the denial order on September 2. The Hearing Commissioner modified it by an order dated September 7, published September 9, to limit its scope to commodities and technical data relating to oil and gas exploration, production, transmission or refinement.[^59] On September 9 [[Joyce Hens Green]] set a schedule under which discovery requests would be served by September 9, responses would be provided by September 14, and a hearing on the motion to vacate the denial order would be held before the Hearing Commissioner by September 17. She denied the restraining order without prejudice to renewal if that hearing did not take place.[^56] The reported decision is *Dresser Industries, Inc. v. Baldridge*, 549 F. Supp. 108 (D.D.C. 1982).[^60]

A Commerce memorandum of October 6, 1982 on the sanctions stated that Creusot-Loire "served as the conduit of payment by Machinoimport to Dresser France," and that Creusot "signed a Declaration of Conformity subsequent to the June 22, 1982 U.S. export controls which stated that the Dresser France compressors conformed to the technical specifications of the September 28, 1981 supply contract between Creusot-Loire, Dresser France and Machinoimport."[^61] On November 16, 1982, after the amendment of the regulations to rescind the controls of December 30, 1981 and June 22, 1982, the Hearing Commissioner vacated the order against Dresser (France) on the Department's motion, noting that "the administrative proceeding against Dresser-France was based on the now-rescinded controls" and that the Department "will take no further action to enforce those controls." The order was published on November 22, 1982.[^62]

### Harbison-Walker and the asbestos claims

Dresser's annual report for fiscal 1993 states that the company had approximately 42,800 pending asbestos claims, about 12,000 of them filed in 1993. The company states that it "has never been a miner or processor of asbestos," but it produced refractory products that contained some asbestos, and about half the pending claims alleged exposure to those products.[^63] Since 1976 the company had tried, settled or disposed of about 13,000 claims for $29 million including legal fees, with insurance covering 60 to 67 percent of legal fees and settlements. About 80 percent of the settled claims related to refractory products.[^63]

The refractories business was separated from Dresser in a spin-off to INDRESCO, Inc. Under the agreement made at the spin-off, INDRESCO assumed liability for asbestos claims filed after July 31, 1992 relating to refractory products of the Harbison-Walker Refractories Division. The report for the fiscal year ended October 31, 1995 names the successor as Harbison-Walker Refractories Company, "formerly INDRESCO Inc."[^64] The 1999 report of Halliburton states that Global Industrial Technologies, Inc., formerly INDRESCO, now disputes that it assumed responsibility for any of those claims based on negligence. It also reports that about 183,000 claims had been filed against Dresser and its former divisions over twenty years, with 29,400 new claims in 1998.[^65]

### Baroid and the 1990s

In fiscal 1993 Dresser reported consolidated sales and service revenues of $4,216 million, divided into three segments: Oilfield Services, Hydrocarbon Processing Industry and Engineering Services.[^66] The company acquired Bredero Price Holding B.V., a Netherlands pipe-coating business, on February 1, 1993, and TK Valve & Manufacturing, Inc., a Texas maker of ball valves, on April 1, 1993.[^66] The Baroid merger, effective January 21, 1994, combined Dresser with [[Baroid Corporation]], a Houston drilling-fluids and offshore services company. Baroid's shareholders received approximately 37.3 million Dresser shares. The Antitrust Division of the [[Department of Justice]] required Dresser to dispose of either its 64 percent interest in [[M-I Drilling Fluids Company]] or Baroid's drilling fluids business, and to divest the United States diamond drill bit business of DB Stratabit, Inc. The 1993 report names Halliburton as the minority partner in M-I, whose consent the sale of that interest required.[^66] The Handbook of Texas entry states that upon completion of the Baroid merger Dresser became "the third-largest oil-services company in the world."[^67]

### Halliburton merger, 1998

The merger was negotiated between Dick Cheney, then chief executive of Halliburton, and [[William E. Bradford]], chairman and chief executive of Dresser. The registration statement on Form S-4 that Halliburton filed on May 18, 1998 recounts that the two companies' predecessors had discussed a combination in 1994 under a confidentiality agreement, and that those talks ended without agreement on essential terms.[^68]

According to the S-4, Cheney and Bradford met briefly on August 25, 1997 concerning Halliburton's pending acquisition of [[NUMAR Corporation]], and met again in Dallas on October 2, 1997. On January 17, 1998, during a customer's quail hunt in South Texas, they agreed to discuss the matter further in Dallas.[^69] At the Dallas meeting on January 27, 1998, Bradford "clearly stated that Dresser was not for sale."[^70] Cheney proposed a merger of Dresser into a Halliburton subsidiary with an exchange ratio of 0.95 Halliburton share for each Dresser share. He proposed that Bradford become chairman of the combined company, that [[Donald C. Vaughn]], Dresser's president and chief executive, become vice chairman, and that Cheney and [[David J. Lesar]] keep their Halliburton posts.[^68] Bradford said he would expect an improved ratio. The final exchange ratio was one Halliburton share for each Dresser share.[^68][^71]

Halliburton had engaged [[SBC Warburg Dillon Read]] and [[Goldman Sachs]] as financial advisers on November 1, 1997. The definitive merger agreement was executed on February 25, 1998.[^71] The merger brought approximately 175 million Halliburton shares to Dresser's holders, and it was completed on September 29, 1998 under pooling of interests accounting.[^65][^71]

### DII Industries and the Chapter 11 filings, 2002 to 2005

In the second quarter of 2002 the former Dresser Industries, Inc. became a limited liability company and was renamed DII Industries, LLC, as part of a reorganization in which Halliburton placed the entities of its Engineering and Construction Group under the former Dresser entity.[^72] A bankruptcy court temporary restraining order entered on February 14, 2002 stayed more than 200,000 pending asbestos claims against DII Industries.[^72] In April 2001 Halliburton had sold the Dresser Equipment Group, which was renamed Dresser, Inc., retaining a 5.1 percent equity interest.[^73]

Halliburton's 2002 annual report describes a proposed global settlement of up to $2.775 billion in cash, 59.5 million Halliburton shares and notes with a net present value expected to be less than $100 million, payable to a trust for current and future asbestos and silica claimants. DII Industries and [[Kellogg, Brown & Root]] would retain the first $2.3 billion of insurance proceeds, and the settlement was to be implemented through a pre-packaged Chapter 11 filing.[^72] The same report describes the disclosure statement to be mailed to the more than 347,000 current claimants.[^74]

According to Halliburton's 2003 annual report, DII Industries, Kellogg, Brown & Root and some of their subsidiaries filed Chapter 11 proceedings on December 16, 2003, in bankruptcy court in Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania.[^75] The order confirming the plan of reorganization became final and nonappealable on December 31, 2004, and the plan became effective in January 2005, when Halliburton contributed approximately $2.345 billion in cash, 59.5 million shares of its common stock and notes to the trusts.[^76]

If the settlement had failed, Halliburton's 2002 report stated, asbestos claims would have been resolved in the tort system or, for Harbison-Walker claims, possibly through the Harbison-Walker bankruptcy proceedings.[^77] In a statement filed on December 3, 2004, Halliburton said that a Third Circuit decision in the [[Combustion Engineering]] bankruptcy had no impact on the DII and Kellogg, Brown & Root bankruptcy, because in the DII case no asbestos liabilities were channeled to a trust other than liabilities of the debtors.[^78]
