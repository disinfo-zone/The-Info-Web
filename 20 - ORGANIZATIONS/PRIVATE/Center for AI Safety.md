The Center for AI Safety describes its mission as "to reduce societal-scale risks from artificial intelligence" through research, field-building and advocacy, and runs a compute cluster that it says supports about 20 research labs.[^1] [[Open Philanthropy]] recommended grants to it of 5,160,000 dollars for general support in November 2022, 4,025,729 dollars for general support, 1,433,000 dollars for the CAIS Philosophy Fellowship, and a final "Exit Grant" of 1,866,559 dollars in October 2023.[^2]

### The Extinction Statement

On May 30, 2023, CAIS published a one-sentence "Statement on AI Risk": "Mitigating the risk of extinction from AI should be a global priority alongside other societal-scale risks such as pandemics and nuclear war." Its signatories included [[Geoffrey Hinton]], [[Yoshua Bengio]], [[Demis Hassabis]] of [[Google DeepMind]], [[Sam Altman]] of [[OpenAI]] and [[Dario Amodei]] of [[Anthropic]].[^1] The *[[Politico]]* reporter [[Brendan Bordelon]] noted in October 2023 that the letter had been organized by a group that "received a $5.2 million grant from Open Philanthropy late last year."[^3]

### The Action Fund

The Center for AI Safety Action Fund is a 501(c)(4), so contributions to it are not tax-deductible, and it describes itself as "dedicated to advancing public policies that maintain U.S. leadership in AI and protect against AI-related national security threats."[^4] Its report on 2024, its first year, stated that it "led a bipartisan congressional effort" with Representatives [[Haley Stevens]] and [[Jay Obernolte]] and Senators [[Martin Heinrich]] and [[Mike Rounds]] that "secured $10 million to establish" the [[National Institute of Standards and Technology]]'s [[U.S. AI Safety Institute]]; that it "co-sponsored California Senate Bill 1047," which the legislature passed and Governor [[Gavin Newsom]] vetoed; that it advised the [[White House]] and the Commerce Department on the "AI Diffusion" export-control rule; and that it worked with the office of Senate Majority Leader [[Chuck Schumer]] on the SAFE Innovation Framework. A photograph of its Capitol Hill launch event in the report shows [[Jaan Tallinn]], co-founder of [[Skype]].[^5] Heinrich and Rounds each had an AI fellow paid through the Open Philanthropy-funded [[Horizon Institute for Public Service]].[^3] In April 2024 [[Gina Raimondo]] named [[Paul Christiano]], a former technical adviser to Open Philanthropy and an original trustee of Anthropic's [[Long-Term Benefit Trust]], head of AI safety at the institute.[^6]

### Dan Hendrycks

Hendrycks, the center's executive and research director, with [[Oliver Zhang]] as managing director,[^1] advises [[Elon Musk]]'s [[xAI]], which he joined in 2023 for a salary of one dollar a year, and [[Scale AI]], which he began advising in 2024 for twelve dollars a year. He told an audience at [[TechCrunch]] Disrupt in October 2025 that he had helped craft SB 1047 and that Musk was the only AI company head to support it: "No other A.I. companies officially supported that bill, and that's because he takes this much more seriously."[^7] In July 2024, as SB 1047 moved through the legislature, the AI security company [[Gray Swan AI]] announced that Hendrycks, one of its advisers, had "decided to divest his entire financial stake in Gray Swan" "to avoid even the appearance of a financial conflict," and would remain an unpaid adviser without equity.[^8] In March 2025 Hendrycks published "Superintelligence Strategy" with the former Google chief executive [[Eric Schmidt]] and Scale AI's founder [[Alexandr Wang]], proposing "Mutual Assured AI Malfunction (MAIM): a deterrence regime resembling nuclear mutual assured destruction (MAD) where any state's aggressive bid for unilateral AI dominance is met with preventive sabotage by rivals," through means "ranging from covert cyberattacks to potential kinetic strikes on datacenters."[^9]

### Lobbying and FTX

The center and the unaffiliated [[Center for AI Policy]] registered their first federal lobbyists in late 2023. The CAIS Action Fund spent 80,000 dollars on congressional and agency lobbying from October to December 2023 and engaged [[Varun Krovi]], a former chief of staff to Representative [[Brenda Lawrence]], to lead it. Its senior policy counsel, [[Nathan Calvin]], said that Open Philanthropy "has not made any contributions" to the Action Fund, whose lobbying he said was funded by a "mix of donors." *Politico* reported in February 2024 that Open Philanthropy had given the center "more than $10.5 million" since November 2022, a figure below the sum of the four grants listed on Open Philanthropy's grant pages, and that the center had received 6.5 million dollars from the cryptocurrency exchange [[FTX]] before its collapse in late 2022. The bankrupt FTX later "demanded information from CAIS regarding that donation," and Calvin declined to say whether the center would return the money. The center had also partnered with the [[United Kingdom]] on AI risks and was among the first members of the Commerce Department's AI Safety Institute Consortium, announced in February 2024.[^2][^10]

The *Politico* article quoted [[Suresh Venkatasubramanian]], a Brown University professor and co-author of a 2022 White House document on AI's near-term risks: "It's about who has more money, and [who] wants to fund their agenda through infusions of cash from a rich doomsday cult." [[Divyansh Kaushik]] of the [[Federation of American Scientists]] said such organizations were "trying to build more mature efforts to get in front of more members, make arguments that at least sound reasonable."[^10]
