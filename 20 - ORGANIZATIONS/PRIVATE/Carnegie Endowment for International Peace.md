The Carnegie Endowment for International Peace is a foreign-policy research institution headquartered at 1779 Massachusetts Avenue NW in [[Washington, D.C.]], which describes itself as the oldest international-affairs think tank in the [[United States]].[^1][^2] It was founded in 1910 with a gift of 10 million dollars from [[Andrew Carnegie]], then, in its words, "one of the richest people in the world after the sale of Carnegie Steel to J.P. Morgan," who sought to "hasten the abolition of war, the foulest blot upon our civilization."[^1]

### History

In 1945 the former Carnegie president [[James T. Shotwell]] led a delegation of advisers to the conference that drafted the [[United Nations]] charter and persuaded the U.S. delegation to add an amendment establishing the permanent UN Commission on Human Rights. From the 1970s the endowment built a program on reducing nuclear risks and became, in its own description, "the premier authoritative source of expertise on nonproliferation." Before the [[Iraq War]] it convened a working group on a weapons-inspection regime, several of whose recommendations were incorporated in UN Resolution 1441. A Middle Class Task Force launched in 2018 produced a report that, the endowment states, "helped form the basis of President [[Joe Biden]]'s reinvigorated foreign policy."[^1]

Besides its Washington and California offices it runs centers in Asia, [[Beirut]], [[Berlin]], [[Brussels]] and [[New Delhi]]: Carnegie China; Carnegie Europe, founded in 2007; Carnegie India, founded in April 2016; the Malcolm H. Kerr Carnegie Middle East Center, founded in 2006; and the Carnegie Russia Eurasia Center in Berlin. It lists "over 200 experts" and states that it conducts "strategic dialogues and back-channel diplomacy" and that its scholars "have served in nearly every administration since the endowment's founding." Its trustees have included [[Dwight D. Eisenhower|Dwight Eisenhower]], [[Elihu Root]], [[John Foster Dulles]], [[Nicholas Murray Butler]], [[Bill Bradley]], [[Robert Zoellick]], [[Ngozi Okonjo-Iweala]] and [[Penny Pritzker]].[^1]

### Presidents

[[William Burns]], who had retired from the Foreign Service in 2014 after serving as deputy secretary of state, was president from 2015 to 2021.[^2] [[Mariano-Florentino Cuéllar]], a former justice of the [[Supreme Court of California]] who had served on the [[President's Intelligence Advisory Board]], was president from November 2021 to June 2026. His Carnegie biography lists him as a director of [[Inflection AI]], [[Harvard University]] and [[LawZero]] and as a trustee of [[Anthropic]]'s [[Long-Term Benefit Trust]], which he joined in January 2026 while still president. On August 4, 2026, Anthropic announced that he would join it as its first chief global affairs officer.[^3][^4][^5]

[[Alger Hiss]] became the endowment's president. He testified before the [[House Committee on Un-American Activities]] in 1948 that the board had elected him in December 1946 and that he assumed office on February 1, 1947; asked whether John Foster Dulles, chairman of the board of trustees, was the first to approach him, he answered "He first approached me."[^6] Hiss was at the same time a trustee of the [[World Peace Foundation]], whose president was [[Harvey H. Bundy]].[^7] The Cox Committee report on tax-exempt foundations stated that Hiss's "nomination and election came about through the efforts of men of proven loyalty and broad experience in public affairs" and that "during his term as president of the Carnegie Endowment, Hiss took no official action to further the Communist cause."[^8]

After [[Whittaker Chambers]]'s August 1948 testimony Hiss offered his resignation. The trustees, meeting on December 12 and 13, 1948 under [[John W. Davis]] (Dulles was abroad), tabled it and granted a leave of absence at full pay, which the executive committee extended on February 17, 1949 to the annual meeting of May 5, 1949. James T. Shotwell acted as president from September 1948. According to Shotwell, [[William C. Bullitt]] asked Hiss at the December dinner whether he had checked with [[James F. Byrnes]] before taking the post.[^9]

In 1950 a nominating committee of Dulles (chairman), Bundy, [[David Rockefeller]], Earl Harrison and James T. Shotwell chose [[Joseph E. Johnson]], who was elected president on May 11, 1950; Shotwell told Federal Bureau of Investigation agents in 1951 that Johnson "was backed by Harvey H. Bundy" and that Bundy "was able to influence Dulles."[^10][^11] Bundy was vice chairman of the board by December 1951. On December 8, 1952, after Dulles resigned the chairmanship on his designation as Secretary of State, the board elected Bundy chairman to fill the unexpired term; he was elected chairman again on May 7, 1953 and May 6, 1954, and the rosters printed in *International Conciliation* list him as chairman in November 1957. By March 1961 he is listed among the honorary trustees and [[Whitney North Seymour]] as chairman.[^12][^13][^14]

### AI Governance

In March 2022 [[Open Philanthropy]] recommended 597,717 dollars over two years to the endowment "to support a research project on international AI governance led by Matt Sheehan," under its focus area "Potential Risks from Advanced AI." The same grant page listed another Open Philanthropy grant of 613,380 dollars over three years to the endowment for a project on "Chinese and Indian Perspectives on Biotech Security Risks."[^15] [[Matt Sheehan]], a senior fellow in the Asia Program and co-director of the China AI Initiative, lived and worked in China from 2010 to 2016 and was previously a fellow at MacroPolo, the think tank of the [[Paulson Institute]].[^16]

On April 29, 2024, [[Holden Karnofsky]] announced that he had left Open Philanthropy to join Carnegie as a visiting scholar and would "analyze and write about topics relevant to AI risk reduction," while continuing to serve on Open Philanthropy's board. He wrote that Cuéllar "has been an advocate for taking (what I see as) the biggest risks from AI seriously," that "Carnegie is looking to increase its attention to AI risk," and that its scholars on the subject included Sheehan, "who specializes in China's AI ecosystem." He cited among his reasons for leaving Open Philanthropy "the fact that I'm married to the President of a major AI company," [[Daniela Amodei]] of Anthropic.[^4] He joined Anthropic in January 2025.[^17]
