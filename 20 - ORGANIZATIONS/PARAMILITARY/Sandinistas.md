The Sandinista National Liberation Front (Frente Sandinista de Liberación Nacional, FSLN) was formally organized in Nicaragua in 1961. The [[Library of Congress]] country study states that it was founded by [[Carlos Fonseca|José Carlos Fonseca Amador]], [[Silvio Mayorga]] and [[Tomas Borge|Tomás Borge Martínez]], who began in the late 1950s as Marxist student activists at the National Autonomous University in [[Managua]], and that it was "officially founded in [[Honduras]] on the symbolic date of July 26, 1961," the eighth anniversary of the launching of the [[Cuba|Cuban]] Revolution by [[Fidel Castro]]; its rural tactics were patterned after Castro's army and its forces were trained in Cuba.[^1]

### The war against Somoza

The FSLN first drew wide attention with its December 1974 raid and hostage-taking at a reception for the U.S. ambassador, after which the government of [[Anastasio Somoza]] accepted demands for ransom and the release of fourteen prisoners. The seizure of the National Palace by a small group in August 1978 set off a mass uprising, and the Front mounted a "final offensive" in May 1979; Somoza resigned on July 16 and fled, and the [[National Guard]] collapsed two days later.[^1] The congressional Iran-Contra Committees give July 17, 1979 as the date Somoza and his family fled, ending a 43-year dynasty and a civil war with more than 130,000 casualties.[^2] The Sandinista victory was supplied in part through [[Panama]]: the [[Kerry Committee]] reported, on the testimony of [[Jose Blandon]], that [[Manuel Noriega]] resold weapons through Panamanian end-user certificates and that "his earliest clients included the Sandinistas." The arms were bought in Europe, moved to [[Costa Rica]] under the eye of Noriega's partner, Costa Rican Security Minister [[Johnny Echevarria]], and flown in on planes from Panama loaded with [[Cuban]] weapons, refueled and repaired at the crop-dusting business of the Costa Rican [[Jaime Guerra]] in the north of the country. Former Costa Rican law enforcement officials told the subcommittee that during the revolution they were instructed to keep their narcotics investigators away from the Nicaraguan border and to let flights go even when they had information about drugs aboard the aircraft delivering weapons.[^3] [[David Westrate]] told the subcommittee in July 1988 that "people on both sides of the equation (in the Nicaraguan war) were drug traffickers, and a couple of them were pretty significant."[^3]

### Government, 1979 to 1990

A five-person Provisional Government directorate took office in 1979: [[Violeta Chamorro]], widow of the murdered *[[La Prensa]]* editor, [[Alfonso Robelo]], [[Sergio Ramirez]], [[Moises Hassan|Moisés Hassan]] and [[Daniel Ortega]]. The Committees' report states that the Sandinistas took over television and radio stations, censored *La Prensa*, forced Chamorro and Robelo to resign from the governing council, and announced on September 19, 1980 that national elections would not be held until 1985; the [[Jimmy Carter|Carter]] administration gave $39 million in emergency food aid and suspended it as U.S. intelligence found the Sandinistas helping the Marxist rebels in [[El Salvador]]. Cuban military personnel helped set up the Sandinista army.[^2][^1]

In the election of November 4, 1984, about 75 percent of registered voters went to the polls, the FSLN won 67 percent of the vote, the presidency and 61 of the 96 seats in the National Assembly. The Library of Congress study states that foreign observers generally reported the election to be fair, while opposition groups said Front domination of government organs, mass organizations and much of the media precluded a truly open election. Ortega was sworn in on January 10, 1985, the seventh anniversary of the assassination of the editor [[Pedro Joaquín Chamorro]]. The Front lost the 1990 elections, and a government headed by Violeta Barrios de Chamorro was installed in April 1990.[^1]

Webb wrote that after taking power the Sandinistas imprisoned or executed captured National Guard officers, and that former officers who escaped into El Salvador, Honduras and Costa Rica became the founding cadre of the [[Contras]], reassembled by the [[Central Intelligence Agency]] starting in 1980.[^4] The congressional Committees describe the Contras as three elements: former National Guardsmen and right-wing figures, anti-Somocistas who had supported the revolution but felt betrayed, and Nicaraguans who opposed the Sandinistas' increasingly anti-democratic regime; "initial support" came from another country, understood to be [[Argentina]], which organized and supplied paramilitary forces in early 1981.[^2]

### United States policy and the Contra war

In December 1981 President [[Ronald Reagan]] signed a Finding authorizing a covert CIA operation to support the Contras, with the administration telling Congress the aim was to limit the spread of communism in the region. The first [[Boland Amendment]] of December 21, 1982 barred use of federal funds to overthrow the Nicaraguan government; in April 1984 press accounts reported that the CIA had mined Nicaraguan harbors; and the October 1984 Boland amendment barred any agency "involved in intelligence activities" from supporting military or paramilitary operations in Nicaragua "directly or indirectly."[^2][^5] [[Lawrence Walsh]]'s Final Report records that Reagan compared the Contras' struggle against Nicaragua's "communist-supported Sandinista government" to that of the American revolutionaries.[^6] The [[International Court of Justice]], in its judgment of June 27, 1986 in *Nicaragua v. United States*, rejected the U.S. claim of collective self-defense and found the United States had breached customary law duties not to interfere in another state's internal affairs, not to use force, not to violate sovereignty and not to interrupt peaceful maritime trade, and that it had broken the 1956 Treaty of Friendship, Commerce and Navigation; the United States had announced on January 18, 1985 that it would not participate. Nicaragua discontinued the proceeding in September 1991 and the case was closed on September 26, 1991.[^7]

On October 5, 1986 a C-123 cargo aircraft belonging to the Enterprise run by [[Oliver North]] and [[Richard Secord]] was hit by a Sandinista SAM-7 missile over Nicaragua, killing three of the four crew; the survivor, [[Eugene Hasenfus]], was captured.[^2][^8] The Contras' principal arms and money pipeline of those years is described on the [[Iran-Contra Affair]] page.

### Drug-trafficking allegations and the Seal sting

The Kerry Committee found that the U.S. [[United States Department of State|State Department]], in an International Narcotics Control Strategy Report, noted "no evidence" of the use of Nicaragua to ship drugs to the United States "since the allegations made in 1984" in connection with the case of the [[DEA]] informant [[Barry Seal]]. Seal, working undercover after being caught smuggling drugs, flew to Nicaragua with cameras installed in his plane and obtained photographs of [[Federico Vaughn]], whom U.S. authorities identified as a Nicaraguan government official, and [[Pablo Escobar]] loading the plane with drugs; the committee found this material "became the central evidence thereafter used by U.S. officials citing Sandinista involvement in narcotics." After the operation was exposed Vaughn disappeared, and the [[House Judiciary Subcommittee on Crime]] found that the telephone number Vaughn used in calls he received from Seal was controlled by the U.S. Embassy.[^3] The Kerry report also states that the Seal operation "directed at officials of the Sandinista government in Managua was prematurely announced publicly by U.S. government officials, shortly before a crucial Congressional vote on Contra aid, thereby jeopardizing an ongoing DEA investigation," and that DEA officials testified before the House Judiciary Subcommittee on Crime on July 28, 1988 that in June 1985 North suggested that $1.5 million in drug money carried aboard Seal's plane be given to the Contras, which DEA rejected.[^3][^9]

Webb wrote that the CIA informed the [[Senate Select Committee on Intelligence]] in April 1984 that "although uncorroborated reports indicating Nicaraguan involvement in the shipping of cocaine to the United States had been received, CIA was unable to confirm reports implicating high-level Sandinistas in drug trafficking," that [[United States Department of Justice|Justice Department]] officials reached the same conclusion two years later, that Reagan displayed a photograph of Vaughn, "a top aide to one of the nine commandantes," loading an aircraft with drugs, and that the House Judiciary Committee investigation found Vaughn likely a U.S. double agent whose house had been "continuously rented" by the U.S. Embassy since 1981 and who was associated with [[Norwin Meneses]] and North. Webb also wrote that the former Sandinista intelligence officer [[Enrique Miranda]] became a CIA asset, DEA informant and right-hand man to Meneses from 1989 to 1991.[^4]

The Kerry Committee recorded two other approaches. In a closed session on June 25, 1987 the money launderer [[Ramon Milian Rodriguez]] testified that he had offered to help entrap the Sandinistas in a drug sting in return for dropping charges against him; [[Felix Rodriguez]] denied his version of a meeting in which Milian offered drug money to the Contras, and no action was taken by the [[Federal Bureau of Investigation|FBI]] or CIA. A Miami attorney who in 1985 met lawyers for the [[Medellin Cartel]] in [[Bogota]] and later its leaders in [[Medellin]] told the DEA in October 1986, and passed a polygraph on his account, that a Bogota lawyer for the Cartel said [[Jorge Ochoa]] "finances both Sandinista and anti-Sandinista forces in Nicaragua by setting up drug operations there"; the Cartel proposed to collect intelligence on guerrillas for six months to a year in return for amnesty, and the DEA and FBI broke off contact with the attorney.[^3]
